# 🦵 Spinal Reflexes

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> A **reflex**[^ety-reflex][^pre-re] is an automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, run through a fixed **reflex arc**: stimulus → receptor → afferent → centre → efferent → effector → response. In **spinal reflexes** the centre is the **spinal cord**[^ety-spinal] itself. The chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ What does the **muscle spindle**[^ety-spindle] (length sensor) do — the **stretch reflex** (muscle **contracts**, **monosynaptic**[^ety-synapse][^pre-mono][^pre-syn])?
> - ✅ What does the **Golgi tendon organ**[^ety-golgi][^ety-tendon] (tension sensor) do — the **inverse stretch reflex** (muscle **relaxes**, **disynaptic**[^pre-di])?
> - ✅ Which **other reflexes** (withdrawal, crossed extensor, postural reactions, mass reflex) use the cord, the midbrain or the cortex?

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Two Sensors on One Muscle

Think of a crane lifting a load. A **length gauge** on the boom (the **muscle spindle**) says "you are being pulled longer — pull back" → the stretch reflex **contracts** the muscle. A **load cell** on the cable (the **Golgi tendon organ**) says "the cable is about to snap — let go" → the inverse stretch reflex **relaxes** the muscle. The **γ motor neuron** is the mechanic who keeps the length gauge tight however short the boom becomes.

```
STRETCH REFLEX          stretch → muscle spindle (length) → Ia, II → cord (1 synapse) → α MN ⊕ → CONTRACTION
INVERSE STRETCH REFLEX  overstretch → Golgi tendon organ (tension) → Ib → cord (2 synapses, inhibitory interneuron) → α MN ⊖ → RELAXATION
γ MOTOR NEURON          contracts spindle ends → spindle stays taut → keeps reporting length (α-γ coactivation)
WITHDRAWAL              pain → many synapses → flexor ⊕ same side + extensor ⊕ opposite side (crossed extensor)
```

## 🧷 Stretch Reflex

### Stimulus

```
Stimulus (Passive stretch) ──── induces ────► Muscle contraction
```

- A muscle that is **passively stretched** contracts back against the stretch — the classic example is the **knee jerk**: tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps, which then contracts.[^2]

### Receptor — Muscle spindle

**Muscle spindle** — a tiny spindle-shaped capsule of special muscle fibres lying **in parallel** with the ordinary muscle fibres; it measures **muscle length**.

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Extrafusal fibers (EFF)**[^ety-fusal][^pre-extra] | The ordinary working muscle fibres (**actin & myosin**[^ety-actin-myosin][^pre-myo]) — supplied by the **α motor neuron** |
| **Intrafusal fibers (IFF)**[^pre-intra] | The fibres inside the **muscle spindle** — supplied by the **γ motor neuron** |
| **Motor innervation**[^ety-innervation][^pre-in][^suf-ation] | **α motor neuron**[^ety-neuron] (→ EFF) and **γ motor neuron** (→ IFF) |
| **Sensory innervation** | **Group Ia** and **Group II** afferents (from the spindle) |
| **Tendon** | Where the muscle ends; site of the Golgi tendon organ |

![[Spinal Reflexes - muscle spindle.png]]
*Muscle spindle — intrafusal fibres (IFF, the spindle) inside the extrafusal fibres (EFF, actin & myosin); motor innervation by α and γ motor neurons, sensory innervation by group Ia and II afferents; tendon below (cropped from the handwritten page).*

#### Types of muscle spindle (intrafusal fibres)

1. **Nuclear**[^ety-nucleus] **bag fibers (NBF)** — nuclei clustered in a central "bag":
   - **Static bag fibre (SBF)**
   - **Dynamic bag fibre (DBF)**
2. **Nuclear chain fibers (NCF)** — nuclei lined up in a single row ("chain").[^3]

### Innervation

**Sensory afferents:**

| | **Group Ia** | **Group II** |
|---|---|---|
| **AKA** | **Annulospiral**[^ety-annulospiral] **endings** — **Primary** | **Flower-spray endings** — **Secondary** |
| **Innervates** | Static bag fiber, **Dynamic bag fiber**, NCF | Static bag fiber, NCF[^4] |

**Motor efferent:**

| Motor neuron | Supplies | Types |
|---|---|---|
| **γ motor neuron** | **Intrafusal fibers** | – **Dynamic (End plates): DBF** <br> – **Static (Trail plates): SBF, NCF** |
| **Alpha motor neuron** | **Extrafusal fibers** | — |

- 🧠 *Plain words:* Ia endings wrap around the middle of **every** intrafusal fibre and report both how far and how fast the muscle is stretched; group II endings sit on the static fibres and report only how far.[^5]

### Centre — Spinal cord

- **Afferent – sensory (Ia, II)** enters the dorsal horn and synapses **directly** on the **α motor neuron** in the ventral horn.
- **Efferent – motor (α motor neuron)** → **effector – skeletal muscle (extrafusal fiber)** → **response (muscle contraction)**.
- One synapse only → **monosynaptic** **reflex (simplest)**.[^6]

![[Spinal Reflexes - monosynaptic stretch reflex arc.png]]
*The stretch reflex arc in the cord — afferent (Ia, II) → α motor neuron → extrafusal fibres contract; a monosynaptic reflex, the simplest of all (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Response — Alpha Gamma Coactivation

**Alpha gamma coactivation**[^ety-coactivation][^pre-co] — when the brain orders a contraction it fires **α and γ motor neurons together**, so muscle contraction happens by two routes:

```
                         Muscle contraction
               ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
            DIRECT                        INDIRECT
       α motor neuron                γ motor neuron (innervates periphery
            ↓                                      of the spindle)
   actin–myosin of EFF contract                    ↓
                                       Central portion stretch
                                                   ↓
                                           Stretch reflex ⊕
                                                   ↓
                                           Muscle contracts
```

![[Spinal Reflexes - alpha gamma coactivation.png]]
*α–γ coactivation — the α motor neuron drives actin and myosin of the extrafusal fibre (EFF) directly; the γ motor neuron contracts the two peripheral ends of the intrafusal fibre (IFF), stretching its central part. The γ chain "central portion stretch → stretch reflex + → muscle contracts" is written out in the flow above; a short gap in the spindle outline is where a watermark was removed (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Why both?* The **γ motor neuron contracts only the two ends (periphery)** of the intrafusal fibre; the ends pull on the **central, receptor-bearing part**, stretching it — exactly as if the whole muscle had been stretched — so the stretch reflex adds its own push to the contraction.[^7]

### Applied aspect

| State | What happens to the spindle |
|---|---|
| **1. At rest** | Spindle lies taut beside the extrafusal fibres (A = actin, M = myosin) |
| **2. On contraction** (α alone) | Extrafusal fibres shorten → the spindle goes slack — **flabby** — and stops signalling |
| **3. On γ motor neuron action** | The spindle ends contract (**shortening**) → **muscle spindle shape maintained** → **muscle spindle functions properly** |

![[Spinal Reflexes - spindle at rest, contraction and gamma action.png]]
*Applied aspect — at rest the spindle is taut; on α-only contraction it becomes flabby; on γ motor neuron action its ends shorten, so the spindle keeps its shape and functions properly (cropped from the handwritten page).*

```
Clenching teeth   (or)   Interlocking fingers (and pulling)
                 │
            Followed by
                 ▼
            Knee jerk  ──►  BRISK knee jerk reflex
                            (D/t α & γ motor neuron coactivation)
```

![[Spinal Reflexes - Jendrassik maneuver.png]]
*Jendrassik maneuver — clenching the teeth or interlocking the fingers and pulling, followed by a knee jerk, gives a brisk knee jerk reflex because of α and γ motor neuron coactivation (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Use at the bedside:* when a knee or ankle jerk seems absent, distracting the patient with this manoeuvre raises γ drive to all spindles and unmasks the reflex.[^8]

## 🛡 Inverse Stretch / Relaxation Reflex

- **Protective**[^ety-protective] **reflex** — it stops a muscle from tearing itself or its tendon.

### Stimulus
- **Overstretching of muscle spindle** (i.e. excessive tension on the muscle).
- **Eg:** **Weight lifting** — a load too heavy to hold makes the arm suddenly give way.

### Receptor — Golgi tendon organs
- Lie in the **tendon**, **in series** with the muscle fibres, and measure **tension**.
- **Golgi tendon organ afferent = Group Ib (sensory)**.[^9]

![[Spinal Reflexes - Golgi tendon organ.png]]
*Golgi tendon organ at the musculotendinous junction — its afferent is a group Ib (sensory) fibre (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Centre — Spinal cord

```
Group Ib afferent
      ↓  1st synapse
Inhibitory neuron            ┐
      ↕  2nd synapse         ├─ DISYNAPTIC reflex
α motor neuron (efferent)    ┘
   = INHIBITED
      ↓
Skeletal muscle (effector) → Response (Relaxation)
```

- Two synapses (Ib → **inhibitory**[^ety-inhibition] interneuron → α motor neuron) → **disynaptic reflex**.

![[Spinal Reflexes - disynaptic inverse stretch reflex arc.png]]
*Inverse stretch reflex in the cord — group Ib afferent → 1st synapse on an inhibitory neuron → 2nd synapse on the α motor neuron (efferent), which is inhibited → skeletal muscle (effector) relaxes: a disynaptic reflex (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Response
- **Relaxation**[^ety-relaxation] **of muscle.**

### Summary

| Components | **Stretch Reflex** | **Inverse Stretch Reflex** |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | Stretch | Overstretch |
| Receptor | **Muscle spindle (Length sensors)** | **Golgi tendon organ (Tension sensors)** |
| Sensory input | Ia, II | Ib |
| Center | Spinal cord | Spinal cord |
| Motor output | α motor neuron **stimulated** | α motor neuron **inhibited** |
| Response | **Contraction**[^ety-contraction][^pre-con] | **Relaxation** |
| Other names | **Myotatic**[^ety-myotatic] **reflex** | **Lengthening reaction**[^10] |
| Number of synapses | **Monosynaptic** | **Disynaptic** |

### Clinical aspects

```
            CLASP KNIFE SPASTICITY                            ANKLE CLONUS
  Initial ↑ in resistance (D/t stretch reflex)      Sudden & sustained stretch
                   ↓                                           ↓
  ↓ in resistance (D/t inverse stretch reflex)              Ankle clonus
                                                 (D/t cycle of stretch & inverse stretch reflex)
```

- **Clasp knife spasticity**[^ety-spasticity][^suf-ity] — bending a spastic limb meets a **rising resistance** (overactive stretch reflex) that **suddenly melts away** (inverse stretch reflex) like the blade of a folding pocket-knife.[^11]
- **Ankle clonus**[^ety-clonus] — a sharp, sustained dorsiflexion of the foot sets off **rhythmic beats**: stretch reflex contracts the calf → tendon organ relaxes it → the foot is stretched again → and round it goes.[^12]

## 🐾 Other Reflexes

### 1. Withdrawal reflex

| Leg | Movement | Muscles |
|---|---|---|
| **Affected leg** (painful stimulus +) | **Flexion**[^ety-flexion] — withdraws from the stimulus | **Flexor ⊕, extensor ⊖** |
| **Balancing leg** (opposite side) | **Extension** — takes the body weight (**crossed extensor reflex**) | **Flexor ⊖, extensor ⊕** |

- **Polysynaptic**[^pre-poly] **reflex** — many interneurons in a chain, so it can spread to both sides of the cord.[^13]

![[Spinal Reflexes - withdrawal and crossed extensor reflex.png]]
*Withdrawal reflex — the leg that steps on the painful stimulus flexes (flexor ⊕, extensor ⊖) while the balancing leg extends (flexor ⊖, extensor ⊕): a polysynaptic reflex (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Myogram**[^ety-myogram][^suf-gram] of the two reflexes:

| | **Myogram — Flexor Reflex** | **Myogram — Crossed Extensor Reflex** |
|---|---|---|
| **Latency**[^ety-latency] | **No latency** (immediate, first / earliest response) | **Latency (+)** |
| **Curve on continuous stimulus** | Rapid rise → plateau → slow fall = **fatigue**[^ety-fatigue] | Slow rise after a delay |
| **After discharge**[^ety-discharge][^pre-dis] | Brief — **to move limb away from pain stimulus** | Prolonged — **to maintain posture / balance** |

![[Spinal Reflexes - myogram of flexor and crossed extensor reflexes.png]]
*Myograms — the flexor reflex (flexor contraction against seconds) rises at once (no latency), fatigues on a continuous stimulus and shows a short after-discharge; the crossed extensor reflex (extensor contraction) starts only after a latency and has a long after-discharge for posture and balance. The arrow at 0 s marks stimulus (+) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### 2. Positive supporting reaction / Magnet reaction

```
Foot pad pressure ── Integration at spinal cord ──► Limb extension (provides support / balance)
```

- Pressing the pad of the foot makes the limb **stiffen in extension**, and the limb follows the pressing finger as if pulled by a **magnet**.[^14]

![[Spinal Reflexes - positive supporting reaction.png]]
*Positive supporting (magnet) reaction — pressure on the foot pad, integrated at the spinal cord, extends the limb to give support and balance (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### 3. Placing reaction

```
Dorsum of foot on table (touched by the table edge)
        ↓
Flexion of knee & hip       ← Integration: CEREBRAL CORTEX
        ↓
For firm placement of foot on table
```

![[Spinal Reflexes - placing reaction.png]]
*Placing reaction — when the dorsum of the foot touches the edge of the table, the knee and hip flex so the foot is placed firmly on the table; integration is in the cerebral cortex (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### 4. Righting reflex

```
Laid on side → Series of body movements → (Integration: MIDBRAIN) → Standing
```

![[Spinal Reflexes - righting reflex.png]]
*Righting reflex — an animal laid on its side makes a series of body movements, integrated in the midbrain, that bring it back to standing (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### 5. Hopping reflex

```
Standing on two legs → Moved horizontally → (Integration: CEREBRAL CORTEX) → Quickly hop (Postural reflex)
```

![[Spinal Reflexes - hopping reflex.png]]
*Hopping reflex — an animal standing on two legs and pushed sideways quickly hops to keep its limbs under the body; a postural reflex integrated in the cerebral cortex (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Level ladder:* support reaction = **cord** · righting = **midbrain**[^ety-midbrain] · placing & hopping = **cerebral cortex**[^ety-cortex] — the higher the centre, the more the reflex needs vision, touch and balance put together (**integration**[^ety-integration]).[^15]

### 6. Mass reflex

```
Paraplegic patient
      ↓
Mild painful stimuli
      ↓
Profuse sweating, systolic BP > 200 mg   (D/t activation of multiple spinal segments)
      ↓
BOWEL & BLADDER EVACUATION
```

- Seen in a **paraplegic**[^ety-paraplegic][^pre-para][^suf-plegia] patient (cord cut off from the brain): a trivial stimulus spreads through **many spinal segments at once**, so the whole cord fires — flexor spasms, sweating, a BP surge and **evacuation**[^ety-evacuation][^pre-e] **of bowel and bladder**.[^16]

![[Spinal Reflexes - mass reflex.png]]
*Mass reflex — in a paraplegic patient mild painful stimuli cause profuse sweating, systolic BP > 200 (mmHg) and bowel & bladder evacuation, because many spinal segments are activated together (cropped from the handwritten page).*

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Every muscle carries **two sensors with opposite jobs**: the **muscle spindle**, lying *in parallel* with the fibres, measures **length** and answers stretch with **contraction** (stretch reflex, one synapse, α ⊕); the **Golgi tendon organ**, lying *in series* in the tendon, measures **tension** and answers overload with **relaxation** (inverse stretch reflex, two synapses, α ⊖). The spindle keeps posture and tone; the tendon organ protects the muscle.
>
> The **γ motor neuron** is what makes the spindle usable during movement: by contracting the spindle's ends together with the α drive (**α–γ coactivation**) it keeps the spindle taut, so length is reported at every muscle length — and turning γ drive up (Jendrassik) or releasing it from upper-motor-neuron control (spasticity, clonus) is what makes reflexes brisk.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> A tendon tap stretches the spindle → Ia → one synapse → α motor neuron → a brisk but controlled jerk. Descending pathways keep γ drive moderate; overload is cut off by the Ib disynaptic inhibition, and painful stimuli trigger the polysynaptic withdrawal + crossed extensor pattern.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Partial loss of descending inhibition (early upper motor neuron lesion) raises γ and α excitability: reflexes become brisk, a few beats of clonus appear, the Babinski response may emerge. A weak reflex can still be "rescued" by the Jendrassik manoeuvre, showing the arc itself is intact.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Complete upper motor neuron lesion: clasp-knife spasticity (rising resistance then sudden give), sustained ankle clonus (≥ 5 beats). Complete cord transection: after spinal shock passes, a trivial stimulus below the level fires the whole isolated cord — the **mass reflex** with flexor spasms, sweating, BP > 200 mmHg and evacuation of bladder and bowel.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → upper motor neuron lesion → ↑ γ drive and loss of descending inhibition → hyperreflexia → spasticity + clonus → (transection) spinal shock → isolated cord → mass reflex
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Reflex grading localises the lesion: a lost jerk with wasting points to the arc (lower motor neuron, root, nerve); a brisk jerk with clonus and clasp-knife tone points above the arc (upper motor neuron). In a paraplegic, a mass reflex / autonomic dysreflexia episode is an emergency — find and remove the trigger (full bladder, impacted stool) and treat the BP.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Stretch (myotatic) reflex:** "Length sensor", "annulospiral / flower-spray endings", "Ia", "monosynaptic", "knee jerk" → muscle spindle → α motor neuron stimulated → contraction.
>
> **Inverse stretch reflex:** "Tension sensor", "Ib", "disynaptic", "lengthening reaction", "weight lifter drops the weight" → Golgi tendon organ → inhibitory interneuron → α motor neuron inhibited → relaxation.
>
> **α–γ coactivation:** "Spindle stays taut during contraction", "flabby spindle", "trail and plate endings" → γ motor neurons fired with α.
>
> **Jendrassik manoeuvre:** "Clench teeth / interlock fingers and pull before tapping" → reinforcement of a weak reflex via ↑ γ activity.
>
> **Clasp-knife spasticity:** "Resistance builds then suddenly gives way" → upper motor neuron lesion (stretch reflex then inverse stretch reflex).
>
> **Mass reflex:** "Paraplegic", "trivial stimulus", "sweating, BP > 200, bladder and bowel empty" → whole isolated cord firing together.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Myogram with no latency and fatigue = flexor (withdrawal) reflex; delayed rise with a long after-discharge = crossed extensor reflex. Spindle drawn "flabby" = α contraction without γ.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Number of synapses: 1 (stretch) < 2 (inverse stretch) < many (withdrawal). Clonus: a few unsustained beats can be normal; sustained (≥ 5 beats) = upper motor neuron lesion. Mass reflex systolic BP > 200 mmHg.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Swapping the receptors' arrangement**
>
> **What students think:** The spindle measures tension and the tendon organ measures length.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The spindle is *in parallel* with the extrafusal fibres, so it is stretched when the muscle lengthens — it is the **length** sensor. The tendon organ is *in series* in the tendon, so it is pulled whenever the muscle develops force — it is the **tension** sensor. Contraction actually unloads the spindle (makes it flabby) but loads the tendon organ.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**S**pindle — **S**ide by side — **S**tretch (length); **T**endon organ — in **T**andem — **T**ension."
>
> **Trap 2: Calling the inverse stretch reflex monosynaptic**
>
> **What students think:** Both muscle reflexes have a single synapse.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** An afferent can only *excite* its target directly; to *inhibit* the α motor neuron the Ib fibre must first excite an inhibitory interneuron, which adds a second synapse. Hence stretch reflex = monosynaptic, inverse stretch reflex = disynaptic, withdrawal = polysynaptic.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Inhibition needs an interneuron."
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking γ motor neurons make the muscle contract directly**
>
> **What students think:** γ motor neurons supply the extrafusal fibres too.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** γ fibres supply **only intrafusal** fibres, and only their **peripheral contractile ends**; the central part, carrying the sensory endings, has no myofibrils. γ activity contributes to force only *indirectly*, by stretching the spindle centre and triggering the stretch reflex.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "α = extrafusal (direct); γ = intrafusal ends (indirect, via the stretch reflex)."
>
> **Trap 4: Mixing up the dynamic and static γ endings**
>
> **What students think:** Plate endings and trail endings are interchangeable.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** On the page, dynamic γ → **end plates** on the **dynamic bag fibre**; static γ → **trail plates** (trail endings) on the **static bag fibre and nuclear chain fibres**. Ia endings sit on all three intrafusal types; group II endings mainly on static bag and chain fibres.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**D**ynamic = **D**BF only; static = everything else (SBF + NCF)."
>
> **Trap 5: Placing every postural reflex in the cord**
>
> **What students think:** All the "other reflexes" are spinal.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Only the withdrawal, crossed extensor, positive supporting and mass reflexes are integrated in the cord. Righting reflexes need the **midbrain**; placing and hopping reactions need the **cerebral cortex**.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Support = Spinal · Righting = midbRain · Placing/Hopping = cortex (they need to *see* and *feel* the table)."

> The cerebellum receives spindle and tendon-organ information through the spinocerebellar tracts; cerebellar lesions give the opposite picture — hypotonia and a pendular knee jerk.
>
> **Clinical Integration:**
> Deep tendon reflexes, clonus, tone and the Jendrassik reinforcement are the bedside tests of this chapter; together they separate lower from upper motor neuron lesions. In spinal injury, the mass reflex (autonomic dysreflexia) is a life-threatening hypertensive crisis triggered by bladder or bowel distension.
>
> **Exam Integration:**
> Expect one-liners on: receptor for stretch vs inverse stretch reflex, afferent fibre types (Ia, II, Ib), number of synapses, other names (myotatic reflex, lengthening reaction), γ endings (plate vs trail), the Jendrassik manoeuvre, clasp-knife spasticity, and the level of integration of righting (midbrain), placing and hopping (cortex).

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Reflex arc: stimulus → **receptor → afferent → centre → efferent → effector** → response.
> 2. **Stretch reflex** — stimulus passive stretch → **muscle spindle (length sensor)** → **Ia, II** → spinal cord → **α motor neuron stimulated** → **contraction**; **monosynaptic**, AKA **myotatic reflex**.
> 3. Intrafusal fibres: **nuclear bag (static SBF, dynamic DBF)** and **nuclear chain (NCF)**.
> 4. **Ia = annulospiral = primary** (SBF, **DBF**, NCF); **II = flower-spray = secondary** (SBF, NCF).
> 5. **γ motor neuron → intrafusal fibres**: **dynamic (end plates) → DBF**; **static (trail plates) → SBF, NCF**. **α motor neuron → extrafusal fibres.**
> 6. **α–γ coactivation** keeps the spindle taut during contraction (otherwise it goes **flabby**).
> 7. **Jendrassik maneuver** (clench teeth / interlock fingers) → **brisk knee jerk** via α–γ coactivation.
> 8. **Inverse stretch reflex** — **protective**; overstretch (weight lifting) → **Golgi tendon organ (tension sensor)** → **Ib** → inhibitory interneuron → **α motor neuron inhibited** → **relaxation**; **disynaptic**, AKA **lengthening reaction**.
> 9. **Clasp-knife spasticity**: ↑ resistance (stretch reflex) then ↓ (inverse stretch reflex); **ankle clonus** = cycle of stretch & inverse stretch reflex.
> 10. **Withdrawal reflex** = **polysynaptic**: affected leg flexion (flexor ⊕, extensor ⊖), balancing leg extension (**crossed extensor**).
> 11. Myogram: flexor reflex **no latency** + fatigue; crossed extensor **latency (+)** + long **after-discharge** (posture).
> 12. Integration: positive supporting (magnet) reaction — **spinal cord**; righting — **midbrain**; placing & hopping — **cerebral cortex**.
> 13. **Mass reflex** in a **paraplegic** → sweating, **systolic BP > 200**, **bowel & bladder evacuation** (many spinal segments at once).

[^1]: Textbooks list five obligatory parts — receptor, afferent neuron, centre (one or more synapses), efferent neuron, effector; the page adds the stimulus and the response to make seven numbered steps. Sir Charles Sherrington's work on spinal reflexes (Nobel Prize 1932) is the basis of this chapter.
[^2]: The knee jerk (patellar reflex) tests segments L2–L4 through the femoral nerve; the ankle jerk tests S1 (tibial nerve), the biceps jerk C5–C6 and the triceps jerk C7.
[^3]: Guyton: each spindle has 3–12 intrafusal fibres — typically 1–3 nuclear bag fibres and 3–9 nuclear chain fibres. The central (equatorial) region of each intrafusal fibre has few or no actin–myosin filaments and carries the sensory endings; only the two ends contract.
[^4]: The page writes "Static bag fiber NCF" and then "NCF" again in the group II cell. Standard texts: secondary (group II) endings lie mainly on nuclear chain fibres and partly on static bag fibres — they do not supply the dynamic bag fibre.
[^5]: Primary (Ia) endings give both a **dynamic** response (to the rate of stretch) and a **static** response (to length); secondary (II) endings give only the static response. Ia fibres are 12–20 µm Aα fibres (≈ 70–120 m/s); group II are 6–12 µm Aβ fibres.
[^6]: Ia fibres also excite, through an inhibitory interneuron, a disynaptic pathway to the **antagonist** muscle (reciprocal inhibition) — so the knee jerk relaxes the hamstrings while it contracts the quadriceps.
[^7]: About 30 % of the motor fibres in a ventral root are γ efferents (Guyton). Because α and γ are fired together, the spindle keeps signalling during contraction and can correct any mismatch between intended and actual shortening ("servo-assist").
[^8]: Ernő Jendrassik described the reinforcement in 1885. Remote muscle effort raises γ (fusimotor) drive and reduces presynaptic inhibition of Ia terminals, so the tapped reflex becomes brisker; it is used before declaring a reflex absent.
[^9]: Each Golgi tendon organ is a capsule of collagen strands at the musculotendinous junction, connected in series with about 10–15 extrafusal fibres; it is far more sensitive to active contraction than to passive stretch. Ib fibres are large Aα fibres like Ia.
[^10]: Also called **autogenic inhibition** (the muscle inhibits itself) or the **clasp-knife reflex**. Modern work shows that tendon organs signal tension continuously during normal movement; the "protective" all-or-none role is the classical teaching kept by the notes.
[^11]: Clasp-knife spasticity is velocity-dependent and typical of upper motor neuron (pyramidal) lesions, most marked in the antigravity muscles. Classical texts credit the sudden give to the Golgi tendon organ, as the page does; later studies also implicate group II and flexor-reflex afferents.
[^12]: Clonus beats at about 5–7 Hz. A few unsustained beats can be normal; sustained clonus (usually ≥ 5 beats) indicates an upper motor neuron lesion.
[^13]: The flexor (withdrawal) reflex uses the "flexor reflex afferents" (pain, cutaneous) and spreads through many interneurons; Guyton notes that the crossed extensor reflex begins 0.2–0.5 s after the painful stimulus and that both show after-discharge from reverberating interneuron circuits, longer for the crossed extensor.
[^14]: The positive supporting reaction (Magnus) is seen in spinal and decerebrate animals; it helps the limb bear weight. The "magnet reaction" name comes from the extended limb following the examiner's finger as it is withdrawn from the foot pad.
[^15]: Righting reflexes (labyrinthine, body and neck righting) persist in a midbrain animal but are lost after decerebration below the midbrain; placing and hopping reactions are lost after removal of the motor cortex.
[^16]: The mass reflex appears weeks after a complete cord transection, once spinal shock has passed. Lesions above T6 can cause **autonomic dysreflexia** — sympathetic overactivity below the lesion with very high BP and reflex bradycardia. The page writes "200 mg"; the unit is **mmHg**.

[^ety-spinal]: **spinal** — Latin *spina*, "thorn, backbone" + *-alis*, "relating to" → relating to the spine; the *spinal cord* is the cord inside the vertebral column.

[^ety-sensory]: **sensory** — Latin *sentire*, "to feel, perceive" → *sensus*, sense + *-ory*, "relating to" → carrying feeling. **Differentiate:** *sensory* (input to the CNS) · *motor* (Latin *movere*, to move — output to muscles).

[^ety-fusal]: **intrafusal / extrafusal** — Latin *fusus*, "spindle" + *intra-*, "within" / *extra-*, "outside" → fibres inside the spindle vs the ordinary fibres outside it. Same root as *fusiform* (spindle-shaped) and *fusimotor* (= γ motor).

[^ety-neuron]: **neuron** — Greek *neuron*, "sinew, cord, nerve" → the nerve cell; *motor neuron* = the nerve cell that drives muscle. α and γ are size classes: α large (Aα axons), γ small (Aγ axons).
[^ety-nucleus]: **nucleus / nuclear** — Latin *nucleus*, "kernel, little nut" (diminutive of *nux*, nut) → the kernel of a cell. *Nuclear bag* = nuclei crowded in a bulge; *nuclear chain* = nuclei in a row. In the CNS, *nucleus* also means a cluster of neuron cell bodies.
[^ety-annulospiral]: **annulospiral** — Latin *anulus*, "little ring" + *spira*, "coil" → the primary (Ia) ending winds in rings and spirals around the middle of the intrafusal fibre. *Flower-spray* (English) describes the branching secondary ending.
[^ety-synapse]: **synapse** — Greek *syn-*, "together" + *haptein*, "to fasten, clasp" → a clasping together; coined by Sherrington (1897). **Differentiate:** *monosynaptic* (one synapse — stretch reflex) · *disynaptic* (two — inverse stretch reflex) · *polysynaptic* (many — withdrawal reflex).
[^ety-coactivation]: **coactivation** — Latin *co-*, "together" + *activus*, "active" (*agere*, to drive) + *-ation*, the process → switching on together: α and γ motor neurons fired at the same time. **Differentiate:** *coactivation* (both together) · *reciprocal inhibition* (agonist on, antagonist off) · *co-contraction* (agonist and antagonist both contract — joint stiffening).
[^ety-jendrassik]: **Jendrassik manoeuvre** — Ernő Jendrassik (1858–1921), Hungarian neurologist in Budapest, who described this reinforcement of the knee jerk in 1885.
[^ety-maneuver]: **maneuver / manoeuvre** — French *manœuvre*, from Latin *manu operari*, "to work by hand" → a planned hand movement; in medicine a set procedure done by the examiner or patient.
[^ety-protective]: **protective** — Latin *pro-*, "in front" + *tegere*, "to cover" → covering in front, shielding; the inverse stretch reflex shields muscle and tendon from tearing.
[^ety-golgi]: **Golgi tendon organ** — Camillo Golgi (1843–1926), Italian histologist at Pavia, who described the organ in 1878; Nobel Prize 1906 (with Cajal). Same Golgi as the Golgi apparatus and the cerebellar Golgi cell.
[^ety-tendon]: **tendon** — Medieval Latin *tendo*, from *tendere*, "to stretch" (influenced by Greek *tenōn*, sinew) → the stretched cord joining muscle to bone. Same root as *tension* and *tone*.
[^ety-inhibition]: **inhibition** — Latin *in-*, "in, upon" + *habere*, "to hold" → *inhibere*, "to hold back" + *-ion* → the process of holding back a neuron's firing. **Differentiate:** *inhibition* (active suppression via an inhibitory synapse) · *disinhibition* (removing an inhibition = net excitation) · *fatigue* (falling response from exhaustion).
[^ety-relaxation]: **relaxation** — Latin *re-*, "back" + *laxare*, "to loosen" (*laxus*, loose) + *-ation* → loosening back; the muscle returns to its resting length. Opposite of *contraction*.
[^ety-contraction]: **contraction** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *trahere*, "to draw, pull" + *-ion* → a drawing together; the muscle's filaments slide together. **Differentiate:** *isotonic* contraction (shortens, same tension) · *isometric* (same length, tension rises) · *contracture* (a fixed, lasting shortening).
[^ety-myotatic]: **myotatic** — Greek *mys, myos*, "muscle" + *tasis*, "stretching" (*teinein*, to stretch) + *-ic* → relating to muscle stretch; *myotatic reflex* = stretch reflex.
[^ety-spasticity]: **spasticity** — Greek *spastikos*, "drawing, pulling" (*span*, to draw, to tear) + *-ity*, state → a state of being pulled tight. **Differentiate:** *spasticity* (velocity-dependent, clasp-knife — upper motor neuron) · *rigidity* (uniform lead-pipe or cogwheel — basal ganglia) · *spasm* (a sudden involuntary contraction).
[^ety-clonus]: **clonus** — Greek *klonos*, "violent, confused motion, turmoil" → rhythmic involuntary jerks. **Differentiate:** *clonus* (rhythmic, sustained by stretch) · *tonic* (sustained contraction — Greek *tonos*, tension) · *tonic–clonic* (the two phases of a generalised seizure) · *myoclonus* (single sudden muscle jerks).
[^ety-flexion]: **flexion / extension** — Latin *flectere*, "to bend" → bending a joint (reducing its angle); *ex-*, "out" + *tendere*, "to stretch" → stretching out (increasing the angle). *Crossed extensor* = extension on the opposite (crossed) side.
[^ety-myogram]: **myogram** — Greek *mys, myos*, "muscle" + *gramma*, "something written, a record" → a written record of muscle contraction against time.
[^ety-latency]: **latency** — Latin *latere*, "to lie hidden" + *-ency*, state → the hidden period between stimulus and response. **Differentiate:** *latency* (delay before response) · *after-discharge* (response continuing after the stimulus stops) · *reflex time* (total time through the arc).
[^ety-fatigue]: **fatigue** — Latin *fatigare*, "to tire out" (from *ad fatim*, "to bursting") → falling response with continued use; in reflexes it occurs at the synapses of the polysynaptic chain.
