# 🫀 Cardiac Output

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Cardiac output (CO)** is the volume of blood each ventricle pumps per minute: **CO = Heart rate (HR) × Stroke volume (SV)** (≈ 5 L/min at rest). Heart rate is set by the autonomic nerves; stroke volume by **preload, contractility and afterload** (mnemonic **CAP**). Output is measured by **Fick's principle**, shared out unequally between organs, and fails in **shock**.
> - ✅ What sets stroke volume, and which marker best measures each determinant?
> - ✅ How is CO measured, and which organ gets the most blood / uses the most O₂?
> - ✅ What are the types and stages of shock and the compensations that fail?

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Pump Fed by a Hose and Pushing Against a Nozzle

The heart is a **pump**: how much it delivers per stroke depends on **how much water reaches it** (preload — venous return fills the ventricle), **how hard it squeezes** (contractility), and **how tight the nozzle is** (afterload — the resistance it pushes against). Multiply by **how often it pumps** (HR) and you have the output.

```
             PRELOAD (EDV, venous return)   ↑ → ↑ SV  (Frank–Starling)
SV  ◄────────  CONTRACTILITY (EF, inotropy)  ↑ → ↑ SV  (digoxin)
             AFTERLOAD (TPR, aortic pressure) ↑ → ↓ SV  (∝ 1/CO)

CO = HR × SV   (sympathetic ↑HR ↑CO · parasympathetic ↓HR ↓CO)        BP = CO × TPR
MEASURE: Fick  CO = O₂ consumption / A–V O₂ difference = 5 L/min
FAILURE: shock → hypovolaemic (m/c) · neurogenic · cardiogenic · anaphylactic · septic (CO ↑)
```

## 🫀 Cardiac Output

- **Cardiac output (CO) = Heart rate (HR) × stroke volume (SV).**[^1]
  - **Sympathetic**[^ety-sympathetic][^pre-syn] **stimulation : ↑ HR → ↑ CO.**
  - **Parasympathetic**[^pre-para] **stimulation : ↓ HR → ↓ CO.**

![[Cardiac Output - pulmonary and systemic circuits.png]]
*The two circuits in series — the right heart (RA, RV) pumps through the pulmonary circuit to the lungs for oxygenation, the left heart (LA, LV) through the systemic circuit to the body; venous return brings blood back (red = O₂-rich, CO₂-poor; blue = O₂-poor, CO₂-rich) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

🧠 *Because the two sides are in series, the right and left ventricles must pump the same output over time — whatever the systemic veins return to the right heart is what the left heart can send out.*

## ⚙️ Determinants of Stroke Volume

### Factors affecting stroke volume — mnemonic **(CAP)**

| Feature | **Preload**[^ety-preload][^pre-pre] | **Contractility (inotropy)**[^ety-contractility][^pre-con][^ety-inotropy] | **Afterload** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Determined by** | **End diastolic volume (EDV)** · **Venous**[^ety-venous] **return (VR)** | **Ejection**[^ety-ejection][^pre-ex] **fraction (EF)** | **Total peripheral**[^ety-peripheral][^pre-peri] **resistance**[^ety-resistance][^pre-re] **(TPR)** · **Aortic**[^ety-aorta] **pressure** |
| **Best marker** | **EDV** | **EF** | **TPR** |
| **Features** | **Factors ↑ VR:** 1. **Calf muscle contraction** · 2. **One way valve** · 3. **Deep fascia**[^ety-fascia] **(prevents collapse of veins)** | **Frank–Starling**[^ety-frank-starling] **law (length–tension relationship) :** **↑ EDV → ↑↑ force of contraction within physiological limits** (then a **plateau**) | The aorta — the vessel the left ventricle must push against |
| **Relation with CO** | **∝ CO** | **∝ CO** | **∝ 1/CO** |
| **Applied aspect** | — | **Digoxin**[^ety-digoxin][^suf-in] — **positive inotropic agent (↑ contractility → ↑ EF)** · **used in Rx of heart failure** | **Heart & kidney form parallel circuit → removal of kidney → ↑ TPR (↑ afterload) → ↓ CO**[^2] |

![[Cardiac Output - preload contractility afterload features.png]]
*The "Features" row of the page's table — the skeletal-muscle (calf) pump with one-way venous valves (preload), the Frank–Starling curve rising to a plateau (contractility), and the aorta from ascending to abdominal (afterload) (cropped from the handwritten page).[^3]*

🧠 *Frank–Starling in one line: a ventricle that is filled more is stretched more, and a stretched muscle (up to its optimal length) contracts more forcefully — so the heart automatically pumps out whatever comes in.*

### Factors affecting force of contraction
- **Bowditch**[^ety-bowditch] **effect / force–frequency relationship / treppe**[^ety-treppe] **:**
  - **↑ HR → ↑ force of contraction.**[^4]
- **Anrep**[^ety-anrep] **effect : abrupt ↑ in afterload → ↑ force of contraction.**[^5]

## 📏 Measurement & Distribution

### Measurement

**1. Fick's**[^ety-fick] **principle :**
- **O₂ consumption = Arteriovenous**[^ety-arteriovenous] **oxygen difference × blood flow.**
  - **Cardiac output = O₂ consumption / AVO₂ difference = 5 L/min.**[^6]
  - **Heart has highest AVO₂ difference = 114 mL/L.**

**2. Echocardiography**[^ety-echocardiography][^suf-graphy] **(m/c) : clinically used.**

**3. Thermodilution**[^ety-thermodilution][^pre-thermo] **method** } **Theoretical.**[^7]
**4. Dye dilution method** } **Theoretical.**

### Distribution

**Distribution & oxygen consumption:**

| Organ | Distribution (blood flow) | O₂ consumption |
|---|---|---|
| **Liver**[^ety-hepatic] | **1500 mL/min (highest)** | **51 mL/min (highest)** |
| **Brain** | **750 mL/min** | **46 mL/min** |
| **Kidney**[^ety-renal] | **1250 mL/min** · **420 mL/100 g/min (highest)** | — |
| **Resting skeletal muscle** | **3 mL/100 g/min** | — |
| **Heart** | **250 mL/min (5 % of CO)** | **10 mL/100 g/min (highest)** |

🧠 *"Highest" depends on the unit: per organ per minute the **liver** wins both blood flow and O₂ use; per 100 g of tissue the **kidney** gets the most blood (it filters, it does not just feed itself) and the **heart** uses the most O₂ (it extracts almost all of it — hence the highest A–V O₂ difference).*[^8]

> [!note] Note
> **Cardiac index**[^ety-index] **ratio : cardiac output per m² body surface area.**
> **Normal cardiac index ratio = 3.2 L/min/m² BSA.**[^9]

## ❤️ Coronary Circulation

- **Heart receives majority of blood during diastole.**[^10]
- **Subendocardial**[^ety-subendocardial][^pre-sub][^pre-endo] **part : more prone to ischemia**[^ety-ischaemia][^suf-aemia].
- **Myocardial**[^ety-myocardium][^pre-myo] **oxygen demand ∝ HR, duration of systole, systolic BP.**

🧠 *During systole the contracting muscle squeezes its own vessels shut (most of all in the inner, subendocardial layer), so the left ventricle is perfused mainly in diastole. Tachycardia therefore hurts twice: it raises O₂ demand and shortens the diastole that delivers O₂.*

### Regulation of coronary blood flow
1. **Nervous control : minimal role.**
2. **Local factors : accumulation of adenosine**[^ety-adenosine][^suf-ine]**, NO, H⁺, metabolic**[^ety-metabolic][^pre-meta] **end product → ↑ coronary**[^ety-coronary] **blood flow.**[^11]
3. **Postural**[^ety-postural] **change :**[^12]
   - **Supine**[^ety-supine] **→ on immediate standing →**
   - **Transient fall in BP (BP = CO × TPR).**
   - **Parameters affected : VR ↓, CO ↓, TPR ↑, HR ↑, central venous pressure ↓.**
   - **Mechanism maintaining BP :**
     - **Baroreceptor**[^ety-baroreceptor][^pre-baro] **reflex.**
     - **↑ TPR (to counter ↓ CO).**

![[Cardiac Output - postural change supine to standing.png]]
*Postural change — supine to immediate standing: blood pools in the legs, venous return and cardiac output fall, and the baroreceptor reflex raises HR and TPR to restore BP (cropped from the handwritten page).*

```
Supine → standing
   ↓ gravity pools ~500 mL of blood in the legs
↓ VR → ↓ CVP → ↓ EDV → ↓ SV → ↓ CO → transient ↓ BP   (BP = CO × TPR)
   ↓ baroreceptor reflex
↑ HR  +  ↑ TPR (vasoconstriction)  →  BP restored
```

## 🚨 Shock[^ety-shock]

### Types

| Type | Seen in | Cardiac output |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolemic**[^ety-hypovolaemic][^pre-hypo] **(m/c)** | **RTA** | **↓** |
| **Neurogenic**[^ety-neurogenic][^suf-genic] | **General / spinal anesthesia**[^ety-anaesthesia][^pre-an] | **↓** (D/t **sympathetic inhibition → extensive vasodilation**[^ety-vasodilation][^pre-vaso] **→ ↓ TPR**) |
| **Cardiogenic** | **Cardiac disorders** · Eg: **cardiac tamponade**[^ety-tamponade] | **↓**[^13] |
| **Anaphylactic**[^ety-anaphylaxis][^pre-ana] | **Bee sting** | **↓** (D/t **histamine**[^ety-histamine] **release → ↑ capillary permeability**[^ety-permeability][^pre-per] **→ extensive vasodilation**) |
| **Septic**[^ety-septic] | **Bacterial infection** | **↑** (D/t **septicemia → ↑ body temperature → activation of SA node**)[^14] |

### Hypovolemic shock — stages

| Stage | **1. Non progressive stage** | **2. Progressive stage** | **3. Irreversible**[^ety-irreversible] **stage** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | **Compensatory**[^ety-compensatory] **mechanism present** | **Without Rx → death** | **Death inevitable** |
| **MOA** | Sympathetic activation + ADH (below) | ↓ coronary flow, cardiac deterioration (+ve feedback), microclots (below) | Loss of energy sources (below) |

**1. Non progressive stage — MOA:**

- **Sympathetic activation → norepinephrine**[^ety-norepinephrine][^pre-nor][^pre-epi] **release →**
  - **Heart : ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, ↑ CO, ↑ BP.**
  - **Kidney : vasoconstriction of afferent arteriole**[^ety-arteriole][^suf-ole] **→ diverts blood to heart & brain.**
- **Antidiuretic**[^ety-antidiuretic][^pre-anti][^pre-dia] **hormone release : ↑ blood volume (D/t ↑ water reabsorption).**

```
Blood loss → ↓ BP → sympathetic activation → norepinephrine
     ├─ heart: ↑HR, ↑contractility → ↑CO, ↑BP
     └─ kidney: afferent arteriole constricts → blood diverted to heart & brain
ADH ↑ → ↑ water reabsorption → ↑ blood volume
```

**2. Progressive stage — MOA:**

- **↓ coronary blood flow.**
- **Cardiac deterioration ⟲ positive feedback.**
- **Clot formation in small blood vessels : ↓ blood flow to heart.**[^15]

```
↓ coronary blood flow → cardiac deterioration → ↓ CO → ↓ BP → ↓ coronary blood flow …  (positive feedback)
+ clots in small vessels → ↓ blood flow to heart
```

**3. Irreversible stage — MOA:**

- **D/t loss of energy sources.**
- **ATP :**
  - **Adenosine regeneration ; slow process.**
- **Creatinine phosphate.**[^16]

🧠 *In the irreversible stage the cells have broken their ATP down so far (to adenosine, which diffuses away) that even if circulation is restored they cannot rebuild their energy stores fast enough — so death follows whatever is done.*

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **CO = HR × SV, and BP = CO × TPR.** Every topic in this chapter is one of these two equations being pushed or defended. Stroke volume rises with what fills the ventricle (preload — the Frank–Starling law makes the heart pump out whatever returns) and with how hard it squeezes (contractility), and falls with what it must push against (afterload). Because the heart cannot pump out more than the veins return, venous return — calf pump, valves, fascia, posture — is the hidden master of output.
>
> When a variable falls (standing up, blood loss), the baroreflex defends BP by raising HR and TPR; shock is the state where that defence is overwhelmed, and its progressive stage is a positive feedback loop (poor coronary flow → weaker heart → poorer coronary flow) that ends in an irreversible energy failure.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> CO ≈ 5 L/min (HR 72 × SV 70 mL), cardiac index 3.2 L/min/m². Venous return equals CO; the liver gets the most flow per organ, the kidney per 100 g; the heart extracts the most O₂ (A–V difference 114 mL/L) and is perfused mainly in diastole.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A fall in venous return (standing, mild blood loss) lowers EDV, SV and CO; the baroreceptor reflex raises HR and TPR, the kidney constricts its afferent arterioles and ADH retains water, so BP is held — the non-progressive stage of shock, with tachycardia and cool skin as the only signs.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> If loss continues, coronary flow falls, the heart deteriorates (positive feedback), microthrombi block small vessels and acidosis builds — the progressive stage, reversible only with treatment. Finally ATP stores are degraded to adenosine and cannot be regenerated — the irreversible stage, death inevitable.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → ↓ venous return / ↓ contractility / ↓ TPR → baroreflex + ADH compensation (non-progressive) → coronary under-perfusion and positive feedback (progressive) → energy failure (irreversible) → death
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The stage decides the outcome: fluids and blood in the non-progressive and progressive stages restore output; inotropes (digoxin, dobutamine) help a weak pump; vasoconstrictors help low-TPR shocks (neurogenic, septic, anaphylactic — adrenaline first); nothing reverses the irreversible stage.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Preload:** "EDV", "venous return", "calf muscle pump", "Frank–Starling", "length–tension" → best marker EDV.
>
> **Contractility:** "Inotropy", "ejection fraction", "digoxin" → best marker EF.
>
> **Afterload:** "TPR", "aortic pressure", "removal of kidney ↑ TPR" → best marker TPR; ∝ 1/CO.
>
> **Treppe / Bowditch:** "Force–frequency", "↑HR → ↑force" → staircase phenomenon.
>
> **Anrep effect:** "Abrupt ↑ afterload → ↑ force" → homeometric autoregulation.
>
> **Fick:** "O₂ consumption ÷ A–V O₂ difference" → CO 5 L/min.
>
> **Shock types:** "RTA" → hypovolaemic (m/c); "spinal anaesthesia, warm and slow" → neurogenic; "tamponade" → cardiogenic; "bee sting, histamine" → anaphylactic; "fever, ↑CO, warm" → septic.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> The Frank–Starling curve rising and then flattening to a plateau; the skeletal-muscle pump with open/closed venous valves; a person going from supine to standing = postural fall in BP.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Non-progressive (compensated, BP held) → progressive (without Rx → death) → irreversible (death inevitable). CO < ~2.2 L/min/m² (cardiac index) signals cardiogenic shock.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Highest blood flow" — which organ?**
>
> **What students think:** The kidney gets the most blood, full stop.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** It depends on the unit. Per organ per minute the liver gets the most (1500 mL/min) and uses the most O₂ (51 mL/min); per 100 g the kidney gets the most flow (420 mL/100 g/min); per 100 g the heart uses the most O₂ (10 mL/100 g/min) and has the highest A–V O₂ difference (114 mL/L).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Read the unit before choosing: "per minute → liver; per 100 g flow → kidney; per 100 g O₂ → heart".
>
> **Trap 2: Thinking afterload increases output**
>
> **What students think:** Higher aortic pressure means the heart is pumping more.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Afterload is the load the ventricle must overcome; raising it (↑TPR, hypertension, aortic stenosis, loss of the low-resistance renal circuit) reduces stroke volume — afterload ∝ 1/CO. The Anrep effect only partly compensates by raising force.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Preload and contractility push SV up; afterload pulls it down.
>
> **Trap 3: Septic shock has low cardiac output**
>
> **What students think:** All shocks have ↓ CO.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Early (warm, hyperdynamic) septic shock has ↑ CO — fever and sympathetic drive speed the SA node while TPR is very low from vasodilation. BP is low because TPR, not CO, has fallen.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Septic = warm, fast, high CO, low TPR" (until late cold sepsis).
>
> **Trap 4: Coronary flow is mainly neural**
>
> **What students think:** Sympathetic nerves control coronary flow.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page stresses nervous control plays a minimal role; local metabolites (adenosine, NO, H⁺, CO₂, K⁺) match flow to O₂ demand. And most left-coronary flow happens in diastole, so the subendocardium is the first layer to become ischaemic.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Coronary flow = metabolism + diastole."
>
> **Trap 5: Creatine vs creatinine**
>
> **What students think:** "Creatinine phosphate" is the energy store.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The high-energy phosphate store of muscle is **creatine phosphate** (phosphocreatine); creatinine is its waste product (cyclic anhydride) excreted by the kidney.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Creat-**ine** = store; creat-**inine** = waste.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **CO = HR × SV**; sympathetic ↑HR ↑CO, parasympathetic ↓HR ↓CO.
> 2. **SV determinants = CAP**: **Preload** (EDV, VR — best marker **EDV**, ∝ CO) · **Contractility** (EF — best marker **EF**, ∝ CO) · **Afterload** (TPR, aortic pressure — best marker **TPR**, ∝ 1/CO).
> 3. **Factors ↑ VR:** calf muscle contraction, one-way valves, deep fascia (prevents collapse of veins).
> 4. **Frank–Starling law (length–tension):** ↑EDV → ↑↑ force within physiological limits (plateau).
> 5. **Digoxin** = positive inotrope (↑contractility → ↑EF), used in heart failure.
> 6. **Heart & kidney in parallel → nephrectomy → ↑TPR (↑afterload) → ↓CO.**
> 7. **Bowditch effect = treppe = force–frequency** (↑HR → ↑force); **Anrep effect** (abrupt ↑afterload → ↑force).
> 8. **Fick:** CO = O₂ consumption / A–V O₂ difference = **5 L/min**; **heart has highest A–V O₂ difference (114 mL/L)**; echocardiography m/c clinically.
> 9. **Liver 1500 mL/min (highest flow) & 51 mL O₂/min (highest O₂)**; **kidney 420 mL/100 g/min (highest per g)**; **heart 250 mL/min (5 % CO), 10 mL O₂/100 g/min (highest per g)**; resting muscle 3 mL/100 g/min.
> 10. **Cardiac index = CO per m² BSA = 3.2 L/min/m².**
> 11. Coronary: **flow mostly in diastole**; **subendocardium most prone to ischaemia**; O₂ demand ∝ **HR, duration of systole, systolic BP**; regulated by **local factors (adenosine, NO, H⁺)**, nerves minimal.
> 12. Standing: **↓VR, ↓CO, ↓CVP, ↑TPR, ↑HR**; BP held by **baroreceptor reflex** + ↑TPR.
> 13. Shock: **hypovolaemic (m/c, RTA)** · **neurogenic** (spinal anaesthesia, ↓TPR) · **cardiogenic** (tamponade) · **anaphylactic** (bee sting, histamine) · **septic — CO ↑**.
> 14. Haemorrhagic shock stages: **non-progressive** (compensation: sympathetic, afferent arteriole constriction, ADH) → **progressive** (↓coronary flow, +ve feedback, microclots) → **irreversible** (energy loss, adenosine regeneration slow).

[^1]: Normal values: HR ≈ 72/min × SV ≈ 70 mL ≈ 5 L/min at rest; up to 20–35 L/min in strenuous exercise in trained athletes.
[^2]: The kidneys receive ~20–25 % of CO through a low-resistance bed in parallel with the other organs. Adding resistances in parallel lowers the total, so removing one parallel bed (nephrectomy) raises total peripheral resistance — the afterload of the left ventricle.
[^3]: The skeletal-muscle pump: contracting calf muscles squeeze the deep veins; the one-way valves let blood move only towards the heart; the tight deep fascia keeps the veins from bulging outwards and from collapsing, so the squeeze is transmitted to the blood. Respiration (the thoracoabdominal pump) also aids venous return.
[^4]: Treppe ("staircase") was first described in frog heart by Bowditch (1871): with a faster rate, each beat leaves more Ca²⁺ inside the cell (less time for extrusion), so successive contractions get stronger. Also called the positive force–frequency relationship.
[^5]: The Anrep effect (1912) is a slower intrinsic rise in contractility a few minutes after a sudden rise in aortic pressure (afterload) — "homeometric autoregulation", as distinct from Frank–Starling "heterometric autoregulation" (length-dependent).
[^6]: Worked example (Guyton): O₂ consumption 250 mL/min; arterial O₂ 200 mL/L, mixed venous (pulmonary artery) O₂ 150 mL/L → A–V difference 50 mL/L → CO = 250 ÷ 50 = 5 L/min. Mixed venous blood must be sampled from the pulmonary artery.
[^7]: The page calls thermodilution and dye dilution "theoretical". In practice the thermodilution method (cold saline injected through a pulmonary artery / Swan–Ganz catheter) is the standard invasive bedside method in intensive care, and the Stewart–Hamilton indicator-dilution principle underlies both; echocardiography is the commonest non-invasive method.
[^8]: The page's values match Ganong's table of resting organ blood flow and O₂ consumption (liver 1500 mL/min, 51 mL O₂/min; brain 750, 46; kidney 1260, 420 mL/100 g/min; heart 250 mL/min, ~84 mL/100 g/min blood flow, ~10 mL O₂/100 g/min; resting skeletal muscle ~2.7–3 mL/100 g/min).
[^9]: Cardiac index normal range ≈ 2.5–4.0 L/min/m² (Guyton's average for adults ≈ 3 L/min/m²; the page gives 3.2). A cardiac index < ~2.2 L/min/m² with high filling pressures defines cardiogenic shock.
[^10]: Left coronary flow falls almost to zero (or reverses) in early systole because the contracting myocardium compresses intramural vessels; ~70–80 % of left ventricular coronary flow occurs in diastole. Right coronary flow is less affected (lower RV pressure). Subendocardial vessels are compressed most — hence subendocardial infarction in hypotension or tachycardia.
[^11]: Adenosine (from ATP breakdown when O₂ supply falls), NO, H⁺, CO₂, K⁺ and lactate dilate coronary arterioles — metabolic autoregulation. Sympathetic stimulation has a small direct α-constrictor effect but a larger indirect dilator effect via ↑ metabolism.
[^12]: The page numbers postural change as item 3 straight after the two coronary regulators; it describes the effect of posture on venous return, CO and BP rather than coronary flow itself. It is kept where the page puts it.
[^13]: Cardiac tamponade is classified in most current texts as **obstructive** shock (the heart is compressed from outside and cannot fill), alongside massive pulmonary embolism and tension pneumothorax; the page groups it under cardiogenic. True cardiogenic shock = pump failure (large MI, myocarditis, arrhythmia).
[^14]: Early (warm, hyperdynamic) septic shock has a high CO with very low TPR; late (cold) septic shock has a falling CO from myocardial depression. Distributive shocks (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic) share low TPR from vasodilation.
[^15]: Guyton lists the positive-feedback loops of progressive shock: cardiac depression (↓coronary flow), vasomotor failure, blockage of very small vessels by sludged blood and microclots ("sludged blood"), increased capillary permeability, release of toxins (endotoxin) and generalised cellular deterioration.
[^16]: The page reads **creatinine phosphate**; the energy store of muscle is **creatine phosphate** (phosphocreatine). In irreversible shock high-energy phosphate stores are depleted — ATP is degraded to ADP, AMP and adenosine, which diffuses out of the cell and is converted to uric acid; regenerating the adenine nucleotide pool is slow (hours), so the cells cannot recover.
[^ety-sympathetic]: **sympathetic / parasympathetic** — Greek *syn-* (*sym-*), "together" + *pathos*, "feeling" → feeling together (Galen's idea of organs "sympathising"); *para-*, "beside" → the system beside the sympathetic. Sympathetic ↑HR and contractility; parasympathetic (vagus) ↓HR.
[^ety-preload]: **preload / afterload** — Latin *prae-*, "before" + English *load* → the load put on the muscle **before** it contracts (stretch from filling, EDV); *after-* + *load* → the load the muscle meets **after** it starts to shorten (the pressure it must overcome, TPR / aortic pressure). Terms borrowed from isolated-muscle experiments.
[^ety-contractility]: **contractility** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *trahere*, "to draw" + *-ility*, capacity → the intrinsic capacity to contract at a given length, independent of preload and afterload. **Differentiate:** *contractility* (capacity) · *contraction* (the act) · *contracture* (a fixed shortening).
[^ety-inotropy]: **inotropy** — Greek *is, inos*, "fibre, sinew" + *tropos*, "turn, change" → change in the force of the muscle fibre. **Differentiate:** *inotropy* (force) · *chronotropy* (rate) · *dromotropy* (conduction) · *lusitropy* (relaxation).
[^ety-venous]: **venous / vein** — Latin *vena*, "vein, a blood vessel" → *venous return* = blood flowing back to the heart through the veins. Same root as *venesection* and *venule* (a little vein).
[^ety-ejection]: **ejection** — Latin *e-* (*ex-*), "out" + *iacere*, "to throw" + *-ion* → throwing out; *ejection fraction* = the fraction of EDV thrown out per beat.
[^ety-peripheral]: **peripheral** — Greek *peri-*, "around" + *pherein*, "to carry" → *periphereia*, "the line carried around, circumference" → towards the outside; *total peripheral resistance* = the summed resistance of the systemic vessels away from the heart.
[^ety-resistance]: **resistance** — Latin *re-*, "back, against" + *sistere*, "to stand, cause to stand" → standing against; vascular resistance opposes flow (R = ΔP / Q). **Differentiate:** *resistance* (opposition to flow) · *compliance* (ease of stretch, ΔV/ΔP) · *impedance* (opposition to pulsatile flow).
[^ety-aorta]: **aorta / aortic** — Greek *aortē*, "that which is lifted, hung up" (*aeirein*, to lift) — Aristotle's name for the great artery from which the heart seems to hang; aortic pressure is the afterload of the left ventricle.
[^ety-fascia]: **fascia** — Latin *fascia*, "a band, bandage, strip" (same root as *fasces*, a bundle of rods) → the sheet of connective tissue wrapping muscles; the tight deep fascia of the leg turns calf contraction into a squeeze on the veins.
[^ety-frank-starling]: **Frank–Starling law** — **Otto Frank** (1865–1944), German physiologist, who described the pressure–volume behaviour of the frog heart (1895); and **Ernest Henry Starling** (1866–1927), English physiologist, who showed in the heart–lung preparation (1914–1918) that the energy of contraction is a function of the length of the muscle fibres. Starling also coined the word *hormone* (1905).
[^ety-digoxin]: **digoxin** — from *Digitalis* (foxglove, Latin *digitus*, "finger" — the flower fits over a fingertip) + *-oxin* (from *toxin* / *oxy-*, a glycoside name) → the cardiac glycoside from *Digitalis lanata*; William Withering described foxglove for dropsy in 1785. Inhibits Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → positive inotropy.
[^ety-bowditch]: **Bowditch effect** — **Henry Pickering Bowditch** (1840–1911), American physiologist at Harvard, who described the staircase (treppe) phenomenon and the all-or-none behaviour of heart muscle in 1871 in Carl Ludwig's laboratory in Leipzig.
[^ety-treppe]: **treppe** — German *Treppe*, "staircase, stairs" → the stepwise increase in the strength of successive contractions when stimulation frequency rises.
[^ety-anrep]: **Anrep effect** — **Gleb von Anrep** (1889–1955), Russian-born physiologist working in Starling's laboratory in London, who described it in 1912; later professor in Cairo.
[^ety-fick]: **Fick's principle** — **Adolf Eugen Fick** (1829–1901), German physiologist in Würzburg, who stated the principle for measuring cardiac output in 1870; also known for Fick's law of diffusion (1855).
[^ety-arteriovenous]: **arteriovenous** — Greek *artēria*, "windpipe, artery" (ancient anatomists found arteries empty after death and thought they carried air) + Latin *vena*, "vein" + *-ous* → between artery and vein; the A–V O₂ difference is the O₂ extracted on the way through a tissue.
[^ety-echocardiography]: **echocardiography** — Greek *ēchō*, "echo, a returned sound" + *kardia*, "heart" + *graphein*, "to write" → imaging the heart by reflected ultrasound. **Differentiate:** *echocardiography* (ultrasound) · *electrocardiography* (electrical) · *phonocardiography* (sounds).
[^ety-thermodilution]: **thermodilution** — Greek *thermē*, "heat" + Latin *dilutio*, from *di-* (*dis-*), "apart" + *luere*, "to wash" → a heat (cold) indicator washed out and diluted by blood; the temperature–time curve downstream gives the flow (CO).
[^ety-hepatic]: **hepatic / liver** — Greek *hēpar, hēpatos*, "liver" + *-ic* → of the liver; *liver* is Old English *lifer*. Same root as *hepatitis*.
[^ety-renal]: **renal / kidney** — Latin *ren, renis*, "kidney" + *-al* → of the kidney; Greek equivalent *nephros* (nephron, nephrectomy).
[^ety-index]: **index** — Latin *index*, "forefinger, pointer, indicator" → a value that points to something; the *cardiac index* corrects CO for body size (per m² body surface area).
[^ety-subendocardial]: **subendocardial** — Latin *sub-*, "under" + Greek *endon*, "within" + *kardia*, "heart" + *-al* → the layer just under the endocardium, the innermost myocardium; it is compressed most in systole and perfused last, so it is the first to become ischaemic.
[^ety-ischaemia]: **ischaemia** — Greek *ischein*, "to hold back" + *haima*, "blood" + *-ia* → blood held back from a tissue. **Differentiate:** *ischaemia* (insufficient flow, reversible) · *infarction* (tissue death) · *hypoxia* (low O₂ for any reason).
[^ety-myocardium]: **myocardium / myocardial** — Greek *mys*, "muscle" + *kardia*, "heart" + *-ium* → heart muscle. Its O₂ demand rises with heart rate, wall tension (systolic BP) and the time spent in systole.
[^ety-adenosine]: **adenosine** — Greek *adēn*, "gland" (adenine was first isolated from pancreas, 1885) + *-ose*, sugar (ribose) + *-ine*, chemical → adenine + ribose; the breakdown product of ATP and the main metabolic coronary vasodilator.
[^ety-metabolic]: **metabolic** — Greek *metabolē*, "change" (*meta-*, change + *ballein*, to throw) + *-ic* → of the chemical changes of life; metabolic end products (adenosine, H⁺, CO₂, K⁺) signal O₂ need. **Differentiate:** *metabolism* (all) · *anabolism* (*ana-*, up — building) · *catabolism* (*kata-*, down — breaking).
[^ety-coronary]: **coronary** — Latin *corona*, "crown, garland" + *-ary* → the arteries that encircle the heart like a crown.
[^ety-postural]: **postural** — Latin *positura*, "position, placing" (*ponere*, to place) + *-al* → relating to body position; *postural (orthostatic) hypotension* = a fall in BP on standing.
[^ety-supine]: **supine** — Latin *supinus*, "lying on the back, face up" → **Differentiate:** *supine* (face up) · *prone* (*pronus*, face down) · *orthostatic* (*orthos*, upright + *statos*, standing).
[^ety-baroreceptor]: **baroreceptor** — Greek *baros*, "weight, pressure" + Latin *recipere*, "to receive" + *-tor* → a pressure (stretch) receiver in the carotid sinus and aortic arch that drives the baroreflex.
[^ety-shock]: **shock** — French *choc*, "a violent blow, collision" → circulatory failure with inadequate tissue perfusion. **Types:** hypovolaemic (incl. haemorrhagic), cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic).
[^ety-hypovolaemic]: **hypovolaemic** — Greek *hypo-*, "under, deficient" + Latin *volumen*, "volume" + Greek *haima*, "blood" + *-ic* → too little blood volume. **Differentiate:** *hypovolaemia* (low volume) · *hypotension* (low pressure) · *hypoperfusion* (low tissue flow) — shock is the last.
[^ety-neurogenic]: **neurogenic** — Greek *neuron*, "nerve, sinew" + *-genēs*, "born of, produced by" → produced by the nervous system; *neurogenic shock* = loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone (spinal cord injury, spinal anaesthesia) → vasodilation with bradycardia.
[^ety-anaesthesia]: **anaesthesia** — Greek *an-*, "without" + *aisthēsis*, "sensation, feeling" + *-ia* → without sensation. **Differentiate:** *anaesthesia* (no sensation) · *analgesia* (*algos*, pain — no pain) · *paraesthesia* (abnormal sensation).
[^ety-vasodilation]: **vasodilation** — Latin *vas*, "vessel" + *dilatare*, "to spread out, widen" (*dis-*, apart + *latus*, wide) + *-ion* → widening of vessels → ↓ resistance. **Differentiate:** *vasodilation* (widening) · *vasoconstriction* (narrowing) · *venodilation* (veins — ↓ preload).
[^ety-tamponade]: **tamponade** — French *tampon*, "a plug, stopper" + *-ade*, an action → the action of plugging; in *cardiac tamponade* fluid in the pericardial sac compresses the heart and stops it filling (Beck's triad: hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds).
