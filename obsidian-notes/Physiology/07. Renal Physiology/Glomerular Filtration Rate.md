# 🫘 Glomerular Filtration Rate

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is the volume of plasma **filtered**[^ety-filtration] from the glomerular[^ety-glomerulus][^suf-ulus] capillaries into Bowman's capsule per minute — normally **125 mL/min = 180 L/day**. Everything in this chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **What stops proteins from being filtered?** — heparan sulphate (charge), fenestrated endothelium and the slit diaphragm (size)
> - ✅ **What pushes fluid out?** — the Starling forces: net filtration pressure = Pc − (πc + Pb) = **10 mm Hg**
> - ✅ **How is it measured and controlled?** — inulin (gold standard), creatinine, cystatin C; arteriolar tone, autoregulation (80–180 mm Hg) and mesangial cells

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Coffee Filter Between Two Taps

Picture the glomerulus as a **coffee filter sitting between two taps**. The filter paper (three layers plus a negative charge) lets water and small solutes through but holds back proteins. The **inlet tap** is the afferent arteriole, the **outlet tap** is the efferent arteriole: opening the inlet or squeezing the outlet raises the pressure on the filter (↑ GFR); closing the inlet or opening the outlet drops it (↓ GFR). Blood flow (RBF) only cares whether the *total* resistance went up or down.

```
INLET TAP (afferent)  ─►  FILTER (glomerulus, Pc 60)  ─►  OUTLET TAP (efferent)  ─►  peritubular capillaries
 dilate inlet   (PGE2)        → ↑ Pc → ↑ GFR   · ↑ RBF
 squeeze inlet  (NE, shock)   → ↓ Pc → ↓ GFR   · ↓ RBF
 squeeze outlet (Ang II)      → ↑ Pc → ↑ GFR (later ↓, as πc rises) · ↓ RBF
 open outlet    (ACE-I)       → ↓ Pc → ↓ GFR   · ↑ RBF
NFP = Pc − (πc + Pb) = 60 − (32 + 18) = 10 mm Hg   →   GFR 125 mL/min = 180 L/day   →   FF = GFR/RPF = 20 %
```

*The renal corpuscle as drawn in the notes — the thicker afferent arteriole, the glomerular capillaries ("only capillary in between 2 arterioles"), the efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, plasma/filtrate entering the tubule, the peritubular capillaries, and the four processes (filtration, reabsorption, secretion, excretion) with "Excretion = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion" (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Structure | What the page says |
|---|---|
| **Afferent arteriole**[^ety-afferent][^pre-ad][^suf-ole] | Brings blood in — **more thicker** (wider) than the efferent arteriole |
| **Efferent arteriole**[^pre-ex] | Carries blood out of the glomerulus |
| **Glomerular capillaries**[^ety-capillary] | **Only capillary in between 2 arterioles** (a capillary bed drained by an arteriole, not a venule) |
| **Bowman's capsule**[^ety-bowman] | Cup around the capillary tuft that receives the **plasma / filtrate** |
| **Peritubular capillaries**[^ety-peritubular][^pre-peri] | Second capillary bed, wrapped round the tubule — takes back what is reabsorbed |

**The four basic renal processes:**
1. **Filtration**
2. **Reabsorption**[^ety-reabsorption][^pre-re]
3. **Secretion**[^ety-secretion][^pre-se]
4. **Excretion**[^ety-excretion] → urinary excretion

> [!info] Renal physiology in one equation
> **Excretion = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion**
>
> 🧠 What leaves in the urine is what was filtered, minus what the tubule took back, plus what the tubule added.

### Nephron[^ety-nephron][^pre-nephro]

![[Glomerular Filtration Rate - nephron with glomerulus.png]]
*A nephron with its glomerulus — "Glomerulus → group of blood vessels" (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Glomerulus → group of blood vessels** (a tuft of capillaries).
- **Nephron:**
  - **Functional unit of kidney.**
  - **1 million / kidney.**[^1]
  - **Loss of nephron → other nephrons compensate.**

🧠 Because each kidney has about a million nephrons working in parallel, losing some of them only makes the survivors filter more — which is why GFR can fall a long way before creatinine rises.

### Ultrafiltrate[^ety-ultrafiltrate][^pre-ultra]

- **Filtered fluid: ultrafiltrate** (**proteins are not filtered, only plasma**[^ety-plasma]).
- **Normal GFR: 125 mL/min or 180 L/day.**[^2]

### Reason for proteins not being filtered

**1. Heparan sulphate**[^ety-heparan]**:**
- Lines the glomerular capillaries.
- **Negatively charged & repels proteins** (anions[^ety-anion][^pre-ana], −ve).
- In renal disease: **proteins filtered** (d/t loss of heparan sulphate).

**2. Fenestrated endothelium**[^ety-fenestrated][^ety-endothelium][^pre-endo]**:**
- **Small gap between cells** (4–8 mm).[^3]
- Restricts protein passage.

**3. Slit diaphragm**[^ety-diaphragm][^pre-dia]**:**
- **Foot process → reinforces the fenestrations → smaller gaps → slit diaphragm of podocytes**[^ety-podocyte][^suf-cyte].
- Restricts protein passage.

![[Glomerular Filtration Rate - slit diaphragm and fenestrated endothelium.png]]
*The filtration barrier as drawn in the notes — podocyte foot processes with the slit diaphragm between them, sitting on the fenestrated endothelium (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Podocyte morphology — foot processes wrapping a glomerular capillary, with slit diaphragms between them (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0b/Podocyte_morphology.jpg)
*Podocytes and their interdigitating foot processes around the glomerular capillary — the slit diaphragm spans the gaps between the feet (figure from a 2025 open-access Frontiers review, via Wikimedia Commons; licence on the file page).*

**Integrity of slit diaphragm is maintained by:**[^4]

| Protein | Mutation (leads to protein excretion) |
|---|---|
| 1. **Nephrin**[^ety-nephrin][^suf-in] | **Congenital Finnish type nephrotic syndrome**[^ety-nephrotic][^suf-otic][^ety-syndrome][^pre-syn] (**NPHS 1 gene**) |
| 2. **Podocin** | **Steroid resistant nephrotic syndrome**[^ety-steroid] |
| 3. **α-actinin**[^ety-actinin] | **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**[^ety-fsgs][^suf-osis] |

### Filtration forces

**Starling forces**[^ety-starling]**:**
- **Hydrostatic**[^ety-hydrostatic][^pre-hydro] **pressure in glomerular capillaries (Pc).**
- **Oncotic**[^ety-oncotic] **pressure in glomerular capillary (πc).**
- **Hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule (Pb).**

| Forces favouring filtration | Forces opposing filtration |
|---|---|
| **Pc = 60 mm Hg** | **πc = 32 mm Hg** |
| — | **Pb = 18 mm Hg** |

![[Glomerular Filtration Rate - Starling forces across the glomerulus.png]]
*Starling forces across the glomerulus — Pc = 60 mm Hg pushes fluid out; πc = 32 mm Hg and Pb = 18 mm Hg oppose it; the applied aspect (renal calculi raise Pb) is noted beside the tubule (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!warning] Applied aspect — **presence of renal calculi**[^ety-calculus]
> **↑ Pb → ↓ GFR.** A stone blocking the tubule or ureter dams fluid back into Bowman's capsule, so the pressure opposing filtration rises.

**Net filtration pressure:**[^5]

```
NFP = Forces that favour filtration − forces that oppose filtration
    = Pc − (πc + Pb)
    = 60 − (32 + 18)
    = 10 mm Hg
```

### Measurement of GFR

**Criteria of a substance used to measure GFR:**

| ✅ Must be | ✅ Must be |
|---|---|
| **Freely filtered** | **No secretion** |
| **No reabsorption** | **Non toxic** |

**Substances:**

**1. Inulin**[^ety-inulin]
- **Inulin clearance**[^ety-clearance] **rate by normal kidneys = 125 mL/min = normal GFR.**
- **Gold standard** (inulin filtered = inulin excreted).
- Procedure: **inulin clearance**.

```
IV inulin ──► Plasma ──(Filtered)──► Kidneys ──(Excreted)──► Urine
```

- Filtration × plasma concentration (P) = urine concentration (U) × volume (V).
- **GFR × P = U × V**

```latex
\text{GFR} = \frac{U \times V}{P}
```

- **↑ P → ↓ GFR.**[^6]

**2. Creatinine**[^ety-creatinine]
- **Plasma creatinine is used for estimation in clinical practice.**
- **↑ plasma creatinine → ↓ GFR.**[^7]

**3. Cystatin C**[^ety-cystatin]
- **Newer marker for GFR.**[^8]

**Applied aspect:**

| Relation with GFR | Substance |
|---|---|
| **Clearance = GFR** | **Inulin** |
| **Clearance > GFR** (New net secretion)[^9] | **Para amino hippuric acid (PAH)**[^ety-pah][^pre-para] |
| **Clearance < GFR** (New net reabsorption) | **Glucose**[^ety-glucose] |

🧠 Compare every substance with inulin: if more is cleared than was filtered, the tubule must have **added** some (secretion); if less, it must have **taken some back** (reabsorption).

## 🩸 Renal Circulation[^ety-circulation]

![Blood flow through the kidney — renal artery to segmental, interlobar, arcuate and cortical radiate arteries, afferent arterioles, glomeruli, efferent arterioles, peritubular capillaries and vasa recta (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/82/2612_Blood_Flow_in_the_Kidneys.jpg/800px-2612_Blood_Flow_in_the_Kidneys.jpg)
*The renal circulation from renal artery to renal vein, with the two capillary beds in series (glomerular → peritubular) that the page's corpuscle drawing shows (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY 3.0).*

**Renal blood flow (RBF):**
- **1250 mL/min (20–25 % of cardiac output).**[^10]

**Renal plasma flow:**
- **55 % of RBF = 625 mL/min.**[^11]
- **Measurement:**
  - Substance used: **PAH**.
  - Formula:

```latex
\text{Effective / estimated RPF} = \frac{U_{PAH} \times V}{P_{PAH}}
```

```latex
\text{True RPF} = \frac{\text{Effective RPF}}{\text{Extraction ratio}} = \frac{625}{0.9} = 700 \text{ mL/min}
```

- **Extraction ratio**[^ety-extraction] is **% of PAH excreted by kidneys = 90 %.**[^12]

**Filtration fraction**[^ety-fraction]**:**

```latex
\text{FF} = \frac{\text{GFR}}{\text{Renal plasma flow}} = \frac{125}{625} = 20\%
```

- **1/5th of renal plasma is filtered at any point of time.**

### Regulation of GFR & RBF

#### 1. Constriction of afferent & efferent arteriole

| Substance | Effect on arteriole / capillaries | Effect on GFR | Effect on RBF |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Norepinephrine**[^ety-norepinephrine][^pre-epi] (in shock, sympathetic[^ety-sympathetic] stimulation) | **Constriction of afferent arteriole** → ↓ blood flow to glomerular capillaries → ↓ Pc | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Angiotensin II**[^ety-angiotensin][^pre-angio] | **Constriction of efferent arteriole: biphasic**[^ety-biphasic][^pre-bi] **response** — 1. **Initial phase (stronger):** blood pooling in glomerular capillaries → ↑ Pc → ↑ GFR · 2. **Later phase:** ↑ protein in glomerular capillaries → ↑ πc → ↓ GFR | ↑ GFR, **later ↓ GFR** | ↓ (**in both phases**) |
| **PGE2** (AKA **renal prostaglandin**[^ety-prostaglandin]): produced by **renal medullary interstitial**[^ety-interstitial][^pre-inter] **cells (RMIC)** | **Dilatation**[^ety-dilatation] **of afferent arteriole:** ↑ blood flow to **renal cortex**[^ety-cortex-medulla], ↓ blood flow to **renal medulla** | ↑ | ↑ |
| **ACE inhibitors** | **Dilatation of efferent arteriole:** blood moves quickly from glomerular capillaries → ↓ Pc | ↓ | ↑ |

> [!note] Note
> **Efferent arteriolar constriction → ↑ πc in peritubular capillaries.**
>
> 🧠 Squeezing the outlet keeps more water in the glomerulus but lets the proteins through to the peritubular capillaries at a higher concentration — that higher oncotic pressure then pulls reabsorbed fluid back into the blood from the proximal tubule.[^13]

#### 2. Autoregulation[^ety-autoregulation][^pre-auto]

- **Despite perfusion**[^ety-perfusion][^pre-per] **pressure change → no change in GFR & RBF.**
- **Occurs between 80–180 mm Hg.**
- **Doesn't need neural influence (occurs in denervated**[^ety-denervated][^pre-de] **kidney).**

**Mechanism:**

**• Myogenic**[^ety-myogenic][^pre-myo][^suf-genic]**: vascular.**

```
↑ RBF → vessel wall stretch → opens Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx → vasoconstriction → ↓ RBF
```

**• Tubulo glomerular feedback (TGF):**
- **Negative feedback mechanism.**

```
↑ GFR → ↑ NaCl filtration → activates macula densa (GFR sensor) → release adenosine
      → constricts afferent arteriole → ↓ GFR
```

The sensor is the **macula densa**[^ety-macula-densa] of the distal tubule; its messenger is **adenosine**[^ety-adenosine][^suf-ine], which (unusually) **constricts** the afferent arteriole.[^14]

- **Applied aspect:**
  - a. **Proteins require Na⁺ for their absorption.**
  - b. **High protein diet → Na⁺ reaches macula densa → ↑ GFR.**[^15]

#### 3. Mesangial[^ety-mesangial][^pre-meso] cell contraction & relaxation

| ↓ GFR | ↑ GFR |
|---|---|
| **Endothelin**[^ety-endothelin], **angiotensin II**, **norepinephrine** | **NO**, **dopamine**[^ety-dopamine], **cAMP**, **natriuretic peptide**[^ety-natriuretic] |
| ↓ | ↓ |
| **Mesangial cell contraction** | **Mesangial cell relaxation** |
| ↓ | ↓ |
| ↓ blood flow to capillaries | **Expansion of capillaries** |
| ↓ | ↓ |
| **↓ GFR** | **↑ RBF & ↑ GFR** |

![[Glomerular Filtration Rate - mesangial cell.png]]
*The mesangial cell drawn as a star-shaped cell wrapped round a glomerular capillary — its contraction shrinks the filtering surface, its relaxation expands it (cropped from the handwritten page).*

🧠 Mesangial cells sit between the capillary loops like the strings of a purse: contraction pulls the loops together and reduces the filtering surface area (Kf), relaxation lets them spread out.

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> GFR is a **pressure-driven ultrafiltration through a charge- and size-selective sieve**, and every fact in this chapter is either about the **sieve** (heparan sulphate, fenestrae, slit diaphragm — keep proteins in) or the **pressure** (Pc 60 against πc 32 + Pb 18 → NFP 10 mm Hg). The kidney controls that pressure with **two resistors in series** — the afferent and efferent arterioles: resistance *before* the glomerulus lowers Pc, resistance *after* it raises Pc, while RBF falls whenever *either* resistance rises.
>
> Measurement follows the mass-balance rule **Excretion = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion**: a substance that is only filtered (inulin) measures GFR; one that is filtered and almost fully secreted (PAH) measures renal plasma flow; their ratio is the filtration fraction (20 %).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> RBF 1250 mL/min (20–25 % of cardiac output), RPF 625 mL/min, GFR 125 mL/min, FF 20 %. Autoregulation (myogenic + TGF) keeps GFR and RBF flat while mean arterial pressure moves between 80 and 180 mm Hg, and the filtration barrier keeps the ultrafiltrate protein-free.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> When perfusion falls (dehydration, early shock, renal artery stenosis), angiotensin II squeezes the efferent arteriole to hold Pc and GFR up, and PGE2 dilates the afferent arteriole to protect flow. Nephron loss in early CKD is hidden because the remaining nephrons hyperfilter, so plasma creatinine stays in the normal range despite a real fall in GFR. Early podocyte injury gives only microalbuminuria.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Remove the compensations and GFR collapses: an ACE inhibitor in bilateral renal artery stenosis (efferent dilated → Pc falls), an NSAID in a volume-depleted patient (PGE2 lost → afferent constricts), or intense sympathetic tone in shock (afferent constriction → ↓ GFR, ↓ RBF → prerenal AKI). A stone raises Pb and stops filtration upstream. Loss of heparan sulphate or a mutant nephrin / podocin / α-actinin opens the barrier → nephrotic-range proteinuria.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → ↓ perfusion / nephron loss / barrier injury → Ang II efferent constriction, PGE2 afferent dilatation, hyperfiltration → drug or disease removes the compensation → ↓ Pc or ↑ Pb or barrier failure → AKI, rising creatinine, nephrotic syndrome
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Knowing which arteriole a drug acts on predicts its effect on GFR: ACE inhibitors and ARBs lower GFR (and creatinine rises a little — expected), NSAIDs lower RBF and GFR in patients who depend on prostaglandins, and the combination "ACE-I + NSAID + diuretic" is the classic triple whammy for AKI.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Congenital nephrotic syndrome of the Finnish type:** "Massive proteinuria from birth", "large placenta", "NPHS1 / nephrin" → nephrin is the backbone of the slit diaphragm; without it albumin pours through from fetal life.
>
> **Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome:** "Child with nephrotic syndrome not responding to steroids", "podocin (NPHS2)" → a structural slit-diaphragm defect, not an immune one, so steroids cannot fix it.
>
> **FSGS:** "Focal segmental sclerosis", "α-actinin-4", "nephrotic syndrome in an adult / HIV / heroin user" → podocyte cytoskeleton fails, foot processes efface, segments of some glomeruli scar.
>
> **Obstructive uropathy / renal calculi:** "Stone", "hydronephrosis", "falling GFR" → ↑ Pb opposes filtration.
>
> **Prerenal AKI from drugs:** "ACE inhibitor started, creatinine jumped", "bilateral renal artery stenosis" → efferent dilatation removes the Ang II prop on Pc.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Electron microscopy showing **effaced (fused) podocyte foot processes** = slit-diaphragm failure; a star-shaped cell between capillary loops = mesangial cell.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> GFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for > 3 months = CKD; GFR < 15 = kidney failure. Plasma creatinine rises steeply only after about half the nephrons are lost. Autoregulation fails below a mean pressure of ~80 mm Hg.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Angiotensin II always lowers GFR because it is a vasoconstrictor"**
>
> **What students think:** Any vasoconstrictor lowers GFR.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Angiotensin II acts mainly on the **efferent** arteriole. Squeezing the outlet dams blood in the glomerulus and raises Pc, so GFR first **rises** (stronger, initial phase). Only later, as the filtered blood becomes protein-rich, does πc rise and GFR fall. RBF falls in both phases because total resistance is higher.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Ask "which arteriole, and does Pc go up or down?" — afferent constriction ↓ Pc; efferent constriction ↑ Pc (biphasic).
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up GFR and RBF changes for ACE inhibitors**
>
> **What students think:** ACE inhibitors reduce both GFR and RBF.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** By dilating the efferent arteriole they **lower** resistance, so RBF **rises**; but blood runs out of the glomerulus faster, Pc falls and GFR **falls**. That divergence (↓ GFR, ↑ RBF) is the exam point.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** RBF follows total resistance; GFR follows Pc.
>
> **Trap 3: "Creatinine clearance is the gold standard for GFR"**
>
> **What students think:** Creatinine is the reference marker.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Creatinine is used for *estimation* in clinical practice because it is endogenous, but it is slightly secreted by the tubule, so its clearance overestimates GFR. Inulin is freely filtered, neither reabsorbed nor secreted, and non-toxic — inulin filtered = inulin excreted — so inulin clearance is the gold standard.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Gold standard = inulin; clinical estimate = creatinine; newer marker = cystatin C.
>
> **Trap 4: Calling adenosine a vasodilator in the kidney**
>
> **What students think:** Adenosine dilates arterioles everywhere (as in the heart).
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In tubuloglomerular feedback the macula densa releases adenosine, which **constricts the afferent arteriole** through A1 receptors and so lowers GFR — the kidney is the classic exception.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "TGF: ↑ NaCl at the macula densa → adenosine → afferent squeeze → ↓ GFR."
>
> **Trap 5: Thinking autoregulation needs the nerves**
>
> **What students think:** Constant GFR is maintained by sympathetic reflexes.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Autoregulation is intrinsic — myogenic stretch of the arteriolar wall and TGF — and works in a **denervated (transplanted) kidney** between 80 and 180 mm Hg.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Auto = self": no neural influence needed.

> eGFR from creatinine (and cystatin C) stages CKD and doses drugs; ACE inhibitors are renoprotective in diabetic nephropathy precisely because they lower Pc; NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors are avoided in volume depletion; stones and obstruction lower GFR through Pb.
>
> **Exam Integration:**
> Expect the NFP calculation (60 − 32 − 18 = 10), clearance comparisons (inulin = GFR, PAH > GFR, glucose < GFR), RPF = 625 and true RPF = 700 via extraction ratio 0.9, FF = 20 %, and the GFR/RBF arrows for NE, Ang II, PGE2 and ACE inhibitors.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Excretion = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion**; nephron = functional unit, **1 million / kidney**.
> 2. Glomerular capillaries = the **only capillary bed between two arterioles**; the **afferent arteriole is thicker**.
> 3. **Normal GFR = 125 mL/min = 180 L/day**; filtrate = protein-free **ultrafiltrate**.
> 4. Protein barrier: **heparan sulphate (negative charge)**, **fenestrated endothelium**, **slit diaphragm of podocytes**.
> 5. Slit-diaphragm proteins: **nephrin → congenital Finnish nephrotic syndrome (NPHS1)**, **podocin → steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome**, **α-actinin → FSGS**.
> 6. **NFP = Pc − (πc + Pb) = 60 − (32 + 18) = 10 mm Hg**; **renal calculi → ↑ Pb → ↓ GFR**.
> 7. GFR marker criteria: **freely filtered, no reabsorption, no secretion, non-toxic** → **inulin = gold standard**; **creatinine = clinical estimate**; **cystatin C = newer marker**.
> 8. **Clearance = GFR → inulin; > GFR → PAH (net secretion); < GFR → glucose (net reabsorption).**
> 9. **RBF 1250 mL/min (20–25 % of CO)**; **RPF = 55 % of RBF = 625 mL/min (PAH)**; **true RPF = 625 / 0.9 = 700 mL/min**; **FF = 125/625 = 20 %**.
> 10. **NE → afferent constriction → ↓ GFR ↓ RBF**; **Ang II → efferent constriction → ↑ then ↓ GFR, ↓ RBF**; **PGE2 → afferent dilatation → ↑ ↑**; **ACE-I → efferent dilatation → ↓ GFR ↑ RBF**.
> 11. **Autoregulation 80–180 mm Hg**, works in the **denervated kidney**: **myogenic** (stretch → Ca²⁺ → vasoconstriction) and **TGF** (macula densa → **adenosine** → afferent constriction).
> 12. **High protein diet → ↑ GFR** (via TGF).
> 13. Mesangial **contraction** (endothelin, Ang II, NE) → ↓ GFR; **relaxation** (NO, dopamine, cAMP, natriuretic peptide) → ↑ RBF & GFR.

[^1]: Textbooks give about 0.8–1.5 million nephrons per kidney (Guyton: ~1 million), with wide variation by birth weight; numbers fall with age (~10 % per decade after 40) and the remaining nephrons hyperfilter.
[^2]: 125 mL/min × 1440 min/day = 180 L/day. Since plasma volume is ~3 L, the whole plasma is filtered about 60 times a day; ~99 % of the filtrate is reabsorbed, leaving ~1–1.5 L of urine.
[^3]: ⚠ The page reads "4–8 mm"; the unit must be **nm** (millimetres are impossible for a cell gap). Guyton gives endothelial fenestrae of ~70–100 nm (they stop cells, not albumin) and the slit pores between foot processes ~25 nm; the effective size cut-off of the whole barrier is a molecular radius of ~4 nm (≈ 8 nm diameter), which is probably what "4–8 nm" refers to. Albumin (radius ~3.6 nm) is held back mainly by its negative charge.
[^4]: Genes: nephrin = *NPHS1* (19q13; congenital nephrotic syndrome of the Finnish type, typically presenting at birth with a large placenta); podocin = *NPHS2* (autosomal recessive steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome of childhood); α-actinin-4 = *ACTN4* (autosomal dominant familial FSGS).
[^5]: Guyton's figures: Pc 60, Pb 18, πc averaging 32 mm Hg (≈ 28 at the afferent end, rising to ≈ 36 at the efferent end as protein-free fluid leaves), oncotic pressure in Bowman's capsule ≈ 0. GFR = Kf × NFP = 12.5 mL/min/mm Hg × 10 mm Hg = 125 mL/min.
[^6]: Read "↑ P → ↓ GFR" as: for a substance produced at a steady rate and excreted only by filtration (U × V constant), its plasma level rises when GFR falls — P and GFR are inversely related. This is why a rising plasma creatinine signals a falling GFR.
[^7]: Creatinine (from muscle creatine) is freely filtered and slightly secreted by the PCT, so creatinine clearance overestimates GFR by ~10–20 %; plasma creatinine also depends on muscle mass. Clinical eGFR uses equations (MDRD, CKD-EPI) from plasma creatinine, age and sex.
[^8]: Cystatin C is a small protein (13 kDa) made at a constant rate by all nucleated cells, freely filtered and then reabsorbed and degraded in the PCT (not returned to blood); it is independent of muscle mass, so it is useful in the elderly, cirrhotics and amputees and is combined with creatinine in the CKD-EPI 2021 equation.
[^9]: The page writes "New net secretion" / "New net reabsorption"; "New" appears to be a slip for **net** — i.e. clearance > GFR means **net secretion**, clearance < GFR means **net reabsorption**. The page's wording is kept in the table.
[^10]: Guyton: ~1100–1250 mL/min (about 22 % of a 5 L/min cardiac output) for kidneys weighing only ~0.4 % of body weight — the flow far exceeds their metabolic needs because it serves filtration.
[^11]: 55 % because haematocrit is ~45 %: RPF = RBF × (1 − Hct) = 1250 × 0.55 ≈ 690; the notes' 625 mL/min is the PAH-measured (effective) RPF, and correcting for extraction gives the true RPF of ~700 mL/min.
[^12]: PAH extraction ratio = (arterial PAH − renal venous PAH) ÷ arterial PAH ≈ 0.9: about 10 % of PAH escapes because some blood perfuses parts of the kidney (capsule, medulla, perirenal fat) that do not secrete PAH. 625 ÷ 0.9 = 694 ≈ 700 mL/min.
[^13]: Peritubular capillary forces: after efferent constriction, the hydrostatic pressure in peritubular capillaries falls and their oncotic pressure rises (filtration fraction ↑), both favouring reabsorption of fluid from the interstitium — the "glomerulotubular balance" link between filtration and PCT reabsorption.
[^14]: Adenosine acts on A1 receptors of the afferent arteriole to constrict it (most other beds, e.g. coronary, dilate to adenosine via A2 receptors). The macula densa also lowers renin release when NaCl delivery is high.
[^15]: Mechanism in standard texts: amino acids are reabsorbed in the PCT by Na⁺-coupled cotransport, so a protein meal increases proximal Na⁺ reabsorption → **less** NaCl reaches the macula densa → TGF dilates the afferent arteriole (less adenosine) → GFR rises (by ~20–30 % within 1–2 h). The page's "Na⁺ reaches macula densa" is best read as "less Na⁺ reaches"; the page wording is kept in the body.
[^ety-filtration]: **filtration** — Medieval Latin *filtrum*, "felt" (felt was used to strain liquids) + *-ation*, the process → straining through felt. **Differentiate:** *filtration* (driven by hydrostatic pressure through a sieve) · *ultrafiltration* (through a very fine sieve that holds back proteins) · *diffusion* (down a concentration gradient) · *osmosis* (water down its own gradient).
[^ety-glomerulus]: **glomerulus** — Latin *glomus*, "a ball of yarn" + *-ulus*, "little" → a little ball of yarn — the tangled capillary tuft. Plural *glomeruli*; adjective *glomerular*. Do not confuse with *glomus* bodies (carotid, glomus tumour).
[^ety-afferent]: **afferent / efferent** — Latin *ad-* (assimilated to *af-*), "towards" + *ferre*, "to carry" → carrying towards (the glomerulus); *ex-* (assimilated to *ef-*), "out of" + *ferre* → carrying away. **Memory:** **A**fferent **A**rrives, **E**fferent **E**xits. *Arteriole* = Latin *arteria* + diminutive *-ola*, a small artery.
[^ety-capillary]: **capillary** — Latin *capillaris*, "hair-like" (*capillus*, a hair) → a vessel as fine as a hair. **Differentiate:** *arteriole* (small artery with muscle — sets resistance) · *capillary* (single endothelial layer — exchange) · *venule* (small vein — collects).
[^ety-bowman]: **Bowman's capsule** — Sir William Bowman (1816–1892), English surgeon, ophthalmologist and histologist, who described the capsule around the glomerulus in 1842. *Capsule* = Latin *capsula*, "a little box" (*capsa*, box + *-ula*, small).
[^ety-peritubular]: **peritubular** — Greek *peri-*, "around" + Latin *tubulus*, "a small tube" (*tubus*, tube + *-ulus*, little) + *-ar*, "relating to" → the capillaries that run around the tubules. [^ety-reabsorption]: **reabsorption** — Latin *re-*, "back, again" + *ab-*, "away, from" + *sorbere*, "to suck in" + *-tion*, the process → sucking back in what was already filtered (tubule → blood). **Differentiate:** *absorption* (gut lumen → blood, first time) · *reabsorption* (tubular fluid → blood, taking back) · *adsorption* (*ad-*, onto — molecules sticking to a surface).
[^ety-secretion]: **secretion** — Latin *se-*, "apart, aside" + *cernere*, "to sift, separate" (past participle *cretus*) + *-ion* → setting apart; in the kidney, moving a substance from the peritubular blood **into** the tubular fluid (PAH, H⁺, K⁺). **Differentiate:** *secretion* (blood → tubule, active) vs *reabsorption* (tubule → blood) vs *excretion* (out of the body in urine).
[^ety-excretion]: **excretion** — Latin *ex-*, "out" + *cernere*, "to sift" → sifting out, discharging from the body. Same root as *secretion*; the prefix decides the direction: *se-* (set aside, into the tubule) vs *ex-* (out of the body). **Differentiate:** *excretion* (waste leaving the body) vs *egestion* (undigested food leaving the gut).
[^ety-nephron]: **nephron** — Greek *nephros*, "kidney" + *-on*, a unit (as in *neuron*) → the kidney's unit; coined in the 20th century. Latin equivalent root *ren-* (renal). **Differentiate:** *nephron* (glomerulus + tubule, the functional unit) · *glomerulus* (only the capillary tuft) · *renal corpuscle* (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule).
[^ety-ultrafiltrate]: **ultrafiltrate** — Latin *ultra-*, "beyond" + *filtrare*, to strain + *-ate*, the product → a filtrate that has gone beyond ordinary filtering (proteins held back). Plasma minus proteins ≈ glomerular filtrate.
[^ety-plasma]: **plasma** — Greek *plasma*, "something moulded or formed" (*plassein*, to mould) → the fluid matrix of blood (Purkinje's 19th-century usage). **Differentiate:** *plasma* (fluid with clotting factors) vs *serum* (fluid after clotting — no fibrinogen).
[^ety-heparan]: **heparan sulphate** — Greek *hēpar*, "liver" (heparin was first isolated from liver, 1916) + *-an*, a polysaccharide → a heparin-like glycosaminoglycan; *sulphate* from Latin *sulfur*. Its sulphate and carboxyl groups carry the negative charge that repels albumin.
[^ety-anion]: **anion** — Greek *ana-*, "up" + *ion*, "going" (*ienai*, to go) → the ion that goes up to the anode (positive pole), i.e. a negatively charged ion; named by Faraday (1834). **Differentiate:** *anion* (−, goes to the anode) vs *cation* (+, *kata-* "down", goes to the cathode).
[^ety-fenestrated]: **fenestrated** — Latin *fenestra*, "window" + *-ated*, "provided with" → provided with windows; fenestrated endothelium has pores through the cell. **Differentiate:** *continuous* (no pores — muscle, brain) · *fenestrated* (pores — glomerulus, endocrine glands, gut villi) · *sinusoidal / discontinuous* (large gaps — liver, spleen, marrow).
[^ety-endothelium]: **endothelium** — Greek *endon*, "within" + *thēlē*, "nipple" + *-ium*, a tissue → originally "inner covering over papillae" (by analogy with *epithelium*); now the single cell layer lining vessels. **Differentiate:** *endothelium* (lines vessels) · *epithelium* (covers surfaces, lines tubules) · *mesothelium* (lines body cavities).
[^ety-diaphragm]: **diaphragm** — Greek *dia-*, "across, through" + *phragma*, "a fence, partition" → a partition stretched across. The *slit diaphragm* is the thin protein membrane stretched across the slit between two podocyte feet, just as the thoracic diaphragm is stretched across the trunk.
[^ety-podocyte]: **podocyte** — Greek *pous, podos*, "foot" + *kytos*, "hollow vessel, cell" → foot cell; its foot processes (pedicels) interdigitate around the capillary. Same root as *podiatry*, *pseudopod*.
[^ety-nephrin]: **nephrin / podocin** — Greek *nephros*, "kidney" + *-in*, protein suffix → the kidney protein (identified 1998 by Tryggvason's group, Finland); *podocin* = *podo-* (foot) + *-in*, the podocyte protein (2000). Both are slit-diaphragm proteins.
[^ety-nephrotic]: **nephrotic** — Greek *nephros*, "kidney" + *-otic*, "pertaining to a condition" (from *-osis*) → relating to a kidney disorder; *nephrotic syndrome* = proteinuria > 3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia. **Differentiate:** *nephrotic* (protein leaks — podocyte/barrier) vs *nephritic* (*-itis*, inflammation — haematuria, hypertension, oliguria).
[^ety-syndrome]: **syndrome** — Greek *syn-*, "together" + *dromos*, "a running" → things that run together; a set of signs and symptoms occurring together. **Differentiate:** *syndrome* (a recognisable cluster, cause may vary) vs *disease* (a defined cause and pathology) vs *sign* (found by the examiner) vs *symptom* (felt by the patient).
[^ety-steroid]: **steroid** — Greek *stereos*, "solid" (from *cholesterol*, "solid bile") + *-oid*, "like" → like a sterol; the four-ring lipid family including glucocorticoids. *Steroid-resistant* nephrotic syndrome = no remission after 4–8 weeks of prednisolone.
[^ety-actinin]: **α-actinin** — Greek *aktis, aktinos*, "ray" (→ *actin*, the thread-like protein) + *-in* → the protein that cross-links actin filaments; in podocytes, α-actinin-4 anchors the foot-process cytoskeleton.
[^ety-fsgs]: **focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** — Latin *focus*, "hearth" → *focal*, only some glomeruli; *segmentum*, "a piece cut off" → *segmental*, only part of each affected tuft; *glomerulus* + Greek *sklērōsis*, "hardening" (*sklēros*, hard + *-osis*, condition) → scarring. **Differentiate:** *focal* (some glomeruli) vs *diffuse* (most glomeruli); *segmental* (part of a tuft) vs *global* (the whole tuft).
[^ety-starling]: **Starling forces** — Ernest Henry Starling (1866–1927), British physiologist at University College London, who in 1896 described the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across capillaries (he also co-discovered secretin and gave his name to the Frank–Starling law of the heart).
[^ety-hydrostatic]: **hydrostatic** — Greek *hydōr*, "water" + *statikos*, "causing to stand" → the pressure of a fluid standing (at rest) — in a vessel, the pressure pushing fluid outward. **Differentiate:** *hydrostatic* pressure (pushes fluid out) vs *oncotic* pressure (pulls fluid in) vs *osmotic* pressure (total solute pull).
[^ety-oncotic]: **oncotic** — Greek *onkos*, "bulk, mass, swelling" + *-tic* → the bulk of the large molecules (plasma proteins) — the colloid osmotic pressure. Same root as *oncology* (a "mass" = tumour). **Differentiate:** *oncotic* (proteins only, ~25–32 mm Hg) vs *osmotic* (all solutes, ~5400 mm Hg across a semipermeable membrane).
[^ety-calculus]: **calculus (calculi)** — Latin *calculus*, "a small pebble" (diminutive of *calx*, limestone; pebbles were used for counting → *calculate*) → a stone in a hollow organ. *Nephrolithiasis* (Greek *lithos*, stone) is the Greek synonym.
[^ety-inulin]: **inulin** — Latin *Inula helenium* (elecampane, the plant it was first extracted from, 1804) + *-in* → a fructose polymer (~5 kDa). Do not confuse with **insulin** (Latin *insula*, island — the islets of Langerhans).
[^ety-clearance]: **clearance** — Old French *cler*, from Latin *clarus*, "clear, bright" + *-ance*, the state → the volume of plasma completely cleared of a substance per minute: C = U × V ÷ P. **Differentiate:** *clearance* (a volume per time) vs *excretion rate* (an amount per time, U × V) vs *filtered load* (GFR × P).
[^ety-creatinine]: **creatinine** — Greek *kreas, kreatos*, "flesh, meat" → *creatine* (found in meat, 1832) + *-ine* → the anhydride of creatine, a waste product of muscle. **Differentiate:** *creatine* (energy store, creatine phosphate) vs *creatinine* (its waste, used for GFR).
[^ety-cystatin]: **cystatin C** — *cyst(eine)* + Greek *-statin*, "stopping" (*histanai*, to stand) → a cysteine-protease inhibitor; *C* marks the third member described. Do not confuse with *statins* (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors) — same suffix, "stopper".
[^ety-pah]: **para-aminohippuric acid (PAH)** — Greek *para-*, "beside, opposite" (the 1,4 position on the benzene ring) + *amino* (from *ammonia*) + Greek *hippos*, "horse" + *ouron*, "urine" → **hippuric** acid was first found in horse urine (Liebig, 1829). Filtered and avidly secreted, so ~90 % is removed in one pass → measures renal plasma flow.
[^ety-glucose]: **glucose** — Greek *gleukos*, "sweet wine, must" + *-ose*, the sugar suffix → the sweet sugar; named by Dumas (1838). **Differentiate the -oses:** glucose, fructose, galactose (monosaccharides) · sucrose, lactose, maltose (disaccharides).
[^ety-circulation]: **circulation** — Latin *circulari*, "to form a circle" (*circulus*, a small circle) + *-ation* → going round in a circle; William Harvey's *De Motu Cordis* (1628). The renal circulation is unusual for having two capillary beds in series.
[^ety-extraction]: **extraction ratio** — Latin *ex-*, "out" + *trahere*, "to draw, pull" + *-tion* → the fraction of a substance pulled out of the blood in one pass through an organ: (arterial − venous) ÷ arterial. *Ratio* = Latin "reckoning".
[^ety-fraction]: **filtration fraction** — Latin *fractio*, "a breaking" (*frangere*, to break) → the broken-off part; FF = GFR ÷ RPF = the part of the plasma flow that is filtered (20 %). Same root as *fracture*.
[^ety-norepinephrine]: **norepinephrine (noradrenaline)** — *nor-* (German *N ohne Radikal*, "nitrogen without a methyl radical") + Greek *epi-*, "upon" + *nephros*, "kidney" + *-ine* → the demethylated "upon-the-kidney" hormone; Latin equivalent *ad-* + *ren* → *adrenaline*. Both names mean "from the gland on top of the kidney".
[^ety-sympathetic]: **sympathetic** — Greek *syn-* (*sym-* before *p*), "together" + *pathos*, "feeling" → feeling together (Galen thought organs "sympathised" through these nerves). In the kidney, sympathetic fibres constrict the afferent arteriole and stimulate renin release (β1).
[^ety-angiotensin]: **angiotensin** — Greek *angeion*, "vessel" + Latin *tensio*, "stretching, tension" (*tendere*, to stretch) + *-in* → the vessel-tensing protein; the name merges *angiotonin* and *hypertensin* (1958). **Differentiate:** *angiotensinogen* (liver precursor) → *angiotensin I* (renin) → *angiotensin II* (ACE, active).
[^ety-biphasic]: **biphasic** — Latin *bi-*, "two" + Greek *phasis*, "appearance" (*phainein*, to show) + *-ic* → showing two phases; here an initial ↑ GFR and a later ↓ GFR from the same stimulus. **Differentiate:** *biphasic* (two opposite phases in time) vs *bimodal* (two peaks in a distribution).
[^ety-prostaglandin]: **prostaglandin** — *prostate* + *gland* + *-in* → named by Ulf von Euler (1935), who first found these lipids in seminal fluid and thought they came from the prostate (they actually come mainly from the seminal vesicles). Made from arachidonic acid by COX — the reason NSAIDs lower RBF.
[^ety-interstitial]: **interstitial** — Latin *inter-*, "between" + *sistere*, "to stand" + *-ial* → standing between; the tissue (and fluid) between cells or tubules. Renal medullary interstitial cells sit between the tubules of the medulla and make PGE2.
[^ety-dilatation]: **dilatation** — Latin *dilatare*, "to spread out, widen" (*dis-*, apart + *latus*, wide) + *-ation* → widening. *Dilatation* and *dilation* are used interchangeably (*dilation* is a later back-formation); *vasodilatation* is the widening of a vessel. Opposite *constriction* (Latin *con-* + *stringere*, to draw tight).
[^ety-cortex-medulla]: **cortex / medulla** — Latin *cortex*, "bark, rind" → the outer layer of an organ; *medulla*, "marrow, pith" → the inner part. In the kidney the cortex holds the glomeruli; the medulla holds the loops, collecting ducts and vasa recta. Same pair in the adrenal gland and the brain.
[^ety-autoregulation]: **autoregulation** — Greek *autos*, "self" + Latin *regula*, "a rule, ruler" + *-ation* → self-ruling; the organ keeps its own flow constant without nerves or hormones. **Differentiate:** *autoregulation* (intrinsic — myogenic, TGF) vs *extrinsic regulation* (sympathetic nerves, hormones).
[^ety-perfusion]: **perfusion** — Latin *per-*, "through" + *fundere*, "to pour" + *-sion* → pouring through; the flow of blood through a tissue. **Differentiate:** *perfusion* (blood through tissue) · *infusion* (*in-*, into — fluid into a vein) · *transfusion* (*trans-*, across — blood from one person to another) · *diffusion* (*dis-*, apart — spreading out).
[^ety-denervated]: **denervated** — Latin *de-*, "removal, away from" + *nervus*, "sinew, nerve" + *-ated* → with the nerves removed — e.g. a transplanted kidney, which still autoregulates. **Differentiate:** *denervation* (nerves removed) vs *innervation* (nerves supplied) vs *enervation* (loss of energy — no relation to nerves in modern usage).
[^ety-myogenic]: **myogenic** — Greek *mys, myos*, "muscle" (literally "mouse", for the way a muscle ripples) + *-genēs*, "born of, produced by" + *-ic* → arising in the muscle itself; the arteriolar smooth muscle contracts when stretched (Bayliss effect, 1902). **Differentiate:** *myogenic* (from the muscle) vs *neurogenic* (from nerves) vs *metabolic* (from local chemicals).
[^ety-macula-densa]: **macula densa** — Latin *macula*, "spot, stain" + *densa*, "thick, crowded" → the dense spot: a plaque of tall, crowded cells in the wall of the distal tubule where it touches its own glomerulus; it senses NaCl delivery. Same *macula* as the macula of the retina and *macule* of the skin.
[^ety-adenosine]: **adenosine** — Greek *adēn*, "gland" (adenine was first isolated from pancreas, 1885) + *-osine*, from *ribose* → adenine + ribose; a nucleoside released when ATP is used. In the kidney it constricts the afferent arteriole (A1 receptors).
[^ety-mesangial]: **mesangial** — Greek *mesos*, "middle" + *angeion*, "vessel" + *-al* → in the middle of the vessels; mesangial cells sit between the glomerular capillary loops, are contractile and phagocytic, and make matrix. *Extraglomerular* mesangial (lacis) cells belong to the juxtaglomerular apparatus.
[^ety-endothelin]: **endothelin** — *endothelium* + *-in* → the peptide made by endothelial cells (Yanagisawa, 1988); the most potent vasoconstrictor known. Contrast **NO** (nitric oxide, also endothelial — the dilator).
[^ety-dopamine]: **dopamine** — *DOPA* (*d*ihydr*o*xy*p*henyl*a*lanine) + *amine* → the amine made from DOPA. At low ("renal") doses it dilates renal vessels via D1 receptors and relaxes mesangial cells.
[^ety-natriuretic]: **natriuretic** — Latin *natrium*, "sodium" (from Egyptian/Greek *natron*, soda — hence the symbol Na) + Greek *ourēsis*, "urination" + *-tic* → causing sodium to be passed in the urine. **Differentiate:** *natriuresis* (↑ Na⁺ excretion) · *diuresis* (↑ urine volume) · *aquaresis* (↑ free-water excretion without salt).
[^pre-ad]: **ad- (af-, ac-, ag- …)** — to, towards; the *d* turns into the next consonant: afferent, adrenal, adsorption. Don't confuse with **ab-** away from (absorption) · **a- / an-** without (anuria).
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (ef-, e-)** — out of, away: efferent, excretion, extraction. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exocrine) · **extra-** beyond (extraglomerular, extracellular).
[^suf-ole]: **-ole / -ola / -ule / -ulus** — small (Latin diminutive): arteriole (small artery), venule, tubule, glomerulus, capsule. Don't confuse with **-ol** an alcohol (cholesterol, ethanol) · **-ose** a sugar (glucose).
[^suf-ulus]: **-ulus / -ula / -ulum** — little (diminutive): glomerulus (little ball), tubulus (little tube), capsula (little box), macula (spot). Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation (glomerulonephritis) · **-osis** a condition (glomerulosclerosis).
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: peritubular, pericardium, perirenal fat. Don't confuse with **para-** beside (para-aminohippuric) · **per-** through (perfusion) · **pre-** before.
[^pre-re]: **re-** — back, again: reabsorption (sucking back), reflex, regulation of reabsorption. Don't confuse with **ren- / reno-** kidney (Latin: renal, renin) · **retro-** backwards, behind (retroperitoneal) · **pre-** before.
[^pre-se]: **se-** — apart, aside: secretion (set apart), segregate, select. Don't confuse with **semi-** half · **sub-** under · **ex-** out (excretion — same root *cernere*, opposite direction).
[^pre-nephro]: **nephro- / nephr-** — kidney (Greek): nephron, nephrin, nephrotic, nephrology. Don't confuse with **neuro-** nerve · **ren- / reno-** kidney (the Latin equivalent: renal, renin, adrenal).
[^pre-ultra]: **ultra-** — beyond, extremely: ultrafiltrate, ultrasound, ultrastructure. Don't confuse with **supra- / super-** above · **hyper-** over, excessive (Greek) · **infra-** below.
[^pre-ana]: **ana- / an-** — up, back, again (Greek): anion (goes up to the anode), anabolism, anatomy. Don't confuse with **an- / a-** without (anuria, anaemia) · **ante-** before · **anti-** against.
[^pre-endo]: **endo-** — within: endothelium, endothelin, endocytosis. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside · **epi-** upon (epithelium, epinephrine) · **meso-** middle (mesangial).
[^pre-dia]: **dia-** — through, across, apart: diaphragm (partition across), diuresis (urine through), diabetes, dialysis. Don't confuse with **di-** two (dipeptide) · **dys-** bad, difficult (dysuria).
[^suf-cyte]: **-cyte / cyto-** — cell: podocyte, erythrocyte, leucocyte. Don't confuse with **-cyst / cysto-** bladder, sac (cystitis, cystometrogram) · **-cytosis** an increase in cells (leucocytosis) or a cellular process (endocytosis).
[^suf-in]: **-in / -ine** — names a protein or chemical substance: nephrin, podocin, actinin, inulin, angiotensin, endothelin, creatinine, adenosine. Don't confuse with **-ase** an enzyme (ATPase) · **-ogen** a precursor (angiotensinogen).
[^suf-otic]: **-otic / -osis** — a state or condition (often abnormal): nephrotic, sclerosis, osmotic. Don't confuse with **-itic / -itis** inflammation (nephritic, nephritis) · **-tic** of, relating to (oncotic, hydrostatic).
[^pre-syn]: **syn- / sym- (before b, p, m)** — together, with: syndrome, sympathetic, synapse. Don't confuse with **semi-** half · **sin-** (Latin *sinister*, left).
[^suf-osis]: **-osis** — a condition, process or state, often abnormal: glomerulosclerosis, osmosis, nephrolithiasis (*-iasis*, the same idea). Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation · **-oma** a tumour · **-asis / -iasis** a diseased condition (lithiasis).
[^pre-hydro]: **hydro- / hydr-** — water: hydrostatic, hydronephrosis, hydrolysis. Don't confuse with **hygro-** moisture (hygroma) · **hydroxy-** an OH group (chemistry).
[^pre-para]: **para-** — beside, alongside; in chemistry the 1,4 (opposite) position on a benzene ring: para-aminohippuric acid, paracrine. Don't confuse with **peri-** around · **pre-** before · **post-** after.
[^pre-epi]: **epi-** — upon, over: epinephrine (on the kidney), epithelium, epididymis. Don't confuse with **endo-** within · **hypo-** under · **ecto-** outside.
[^pre-angio]: **angio-** — vessel (Greek *angeion*): angiotensin, angiography, angioedema. Don't confuse with **angina** (Latin *angere*, to choke — chest pain) · **vaso-** vessel (the Latin equivalent: vasoconstriction).
[^pre-bi]: **bi-** — two, twice (Latin): biphasic, bicarbonate, bilateral. Don't confuse with **di-** two (Greek: dipeptide) · **dia-** through · **bio-** life.
[^pre-inter]: **inter-** — between: interstitial, intercalated (cells "inserted between"), interlobar. Don't confuse with **intra-** within (intracellular) · **infra-** below.
[^pre-auto]: **auto-** — self: autoregulation, autonomic, autoimmune. Don't confuse with **allo-** other (allograft) · **hetero-** different.
[^pre-per]: **per-** — through, thoroughly: perfusion, permeable, percutaneous. Don't confuse with **peri-** around (peritubular) · **pre-** before · **para-** beside.
[^pre-de]: **de-** — down, away, removal, reversal: denervated, depolarisation, dehydration. Don't confuse with **di-** two · **dis-** apart (diffusion) · **dys-** bad.
[^pre-myo]: **myo- / my-** — muscle: myogenic, myocardium, myofibroblast. Don't confuse with **myelo-** marrow, spinal cord (myeloma, myelin) · **myco-** fungus.
[^suf-genic]: **-genic / -genesis** — producing or produced by: myogenic, nephrogenic (diabetes insipidus), angiotensinogen (*-ogen*, the precursor). Don't confuse with **-gen** a precursor substance · **-gnosis** knowing (diagnosis).
[^suf-ine]: **-ine / -osine** — a chemical, often a nitrogen base or amine: adenosine, dopamine, creatinine, norepinephrine. Don't confuse with **-in** a protein (nephrin) · **-ose** a sugar · **-ase** an enzyme.
[^pre-meso]: **meso- / mes-** — middle: mesangial, mesoderm, mesentery, mesonephros. Don't confuse with **meta-** after, change (metanephros, metabolism) · **pro-** before (pronephros).
