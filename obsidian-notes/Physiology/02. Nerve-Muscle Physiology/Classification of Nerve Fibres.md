# 🗂 Classification of Nerve Fibres

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Nerve fibres are sorted by **how thick and how myelinated** they are, because that decides **how fast they conduct** and **what they carry**. The chapter then asks how much stimulus a fibre needs to fire (**strength–duration curve**) and what happens when a fibre is **injured** and tries to **regenerate**:
> - ✅ **Erlanger–Gasser** (A α β γ δ, B, C — based on myelin; for all fibres) vs **Lloyd–Hunt** (I–IV — sensory fibres only)
> - ✅ **Rheobase** (minimum strength) and **chronaxie** (time at twice rheobase) — chronaxie ∝ 1 / excitability
> - ✅ **Nerve injury** → **Wallerian (distal) and proximal degeneration** → **regeneration 1–3 mm/day**, graded by **Seddon** and **Sunderland**

## 🧠 Mental Model — Roads, a Heavy Door and a Cut Cable

```
FIBRES = ROADS        A (myelinated, thick)  = expressway  → Aα 120 m/s, proprioception + α motor
                      B (myelinated, thin)   = state road  → preganglionic autonomic
                      C (unmyelinated)       = village lane → 2 m/s, slow pain + postganglionic
STIMULUS = DOOR       push hard for a short time OR gently for a long time
                      weakest push that ever opens it = RHEOBASE · time needed at 2 × rheobase = CHRONAXIE
INJURY = CUT CABLE    distal piece dies (Wallerian, < 24 h) · cell body reacts (chromatolysis, 36 h)
                      regrowth 1–3 mm/day (Tinel sign advances) · Seddon: praxia → axonotmesis → neurotmesis
```

## 🗂 Classification of Nerve Fibers

**Types:**
1. **Erlanger–Gasser**[^ety-erlanger] **classification.**
2. **Lloyd–Hunt**[^ety-lloyd] **classification.**

### Erlanger Gasser classification
- **Erlanger and Gasser received the Nobel Prize.**
- Classified based on **myelin**[^ety-myelin].

```
                              MYELIN
                    ┌───────────┴───────────┐
                 PRESENT                  ABSENT
            ┌───────┴───────┐               │
            A               B               C
   ┌──────┬─┴────┬──────┐
   α      β      γ      δ

Diameter         :  Largest (maximum thickest) ─────────────────►  Smallest (thinnest)
Conduction vel.  :  Maximum  120 m/s          ─────────────────►  Minimum  2 m/s
AP spike duration:  Least                      ─────────────────►  Most
```

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - Erlanger Gasser classification tree.png]]
*Erlanger–Gasser classification based on myelin — present (A: α, β, γ, δ; B) or absent (C); diameter, conduction velocity (120 → 2 m/s) and spike duration change in opposite directions along the row (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!tip] 🧠 Reading the three arrows
> Going from **Aα to C** the fibres get **thinner and lose myelin**, so they conduct **slower** — and their action potential (spike) lasts **longer**. Thick + myelinated = fast + brief spike; thin + bare = slow + long spike.[^1]

### Functions of nerve fibers
Fibers are **sensory**[^ety-sensory] **(S)**, **motor (M)**, or **both**.

| Type | Function |
|---|---|
| **Aα** | **S:** **Proprioception**[^ety-proprioception][^pre-pro] (joint position sense) |
| | **M:** Alpha motor neuron (spinal cord) |
| **Aβ** | **S:** Touch sensation |
| **Aγ** | **M:** Muscle spindle[^ety-spindle] (gamma motor neuron in spinal cord: regulate tone) |
| **Aδ** | **S:** Fast pain (1st pain) |
| **B** | **Preganglionic**[^ety-ganglionic][^pre-pre] **autonomic**[^ety-autonomic][^pre-auto] **nerve fibers** |
| **C** | **S:** Slow pain (2nd pain) |
| | **Postganglionic**[^pre-post] **autonomic nerve fibers** |

> [!note] Note
> - **Susceptible to compression**[^ety-compression][^pre-com]**:** **Aα (largest) > Aβ.**
> - **Susceptible to hypoxia**[^ety-hypoxia][^pre-hypo][^suf-ia]**:**
>   - **Most: B.**
>   - **Least: C.**[^2]

### Lloyd–Hunt classification
- AKA **sensory classification**: **only for sensory fibres**.
- **Numerical classification:**

| Type | Corresponding to Erlanger–Gasser classification |
|---|---|
| **I** — Ia: muscle spindles · Ib: Golgi[^ety-golgi] tendon[^ety-tendon] organ | **Aα** |
| **II** | **Aβ** |
| **III** | **Aδ** |
| **IV** | **C** |

> [!info] 🧠 Why two systems?
> Erlanger–Gasser letters cover **every** fibre (motor, sensory, autonomic). Lloyd–Hunt Roman numerals were made for **sensory (afferent) fibres from muscle** — so spindle and tendon-organ afferents are named Ia and Ib, while the motor Aγ and the autonomic B have no Roman numeral.[^3]

## ⚡ Action Potential

### Characteristics
- For generating a response: a **stimulus**[^ety-stimulus] of a particular **strength** and **duration**.

### Strength–duration curve

**Rheobase**[^ety-rheobase]**:**
- **Minimum strength of current** required to produce a response.

**Chronaxie**[^ety-chronaxie]**:**
- **Minimum time** required to produce a response with **twice rheobase strength**.

**Importance:**
- **Chronaxie ∝ 1 / Excitability**[^ety-excitability][^pre-ex].

| | **Nerve** | **Muscle** |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronaxie** | ↓ | ↑ |
| **Excitability** | ↑ | ↓ |

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - strength duration curve.png]]
*Strength–duration graph — stimulus voltage (y-axis; the page's "Voltage" label sits under a watermark and was cut off) against duration (ms): the curve flattens at the rheobase; at 2 × rheobase the duration read off the x-axis is the chronaxie (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!tip] 🧠 Reading the curve
> A short pulse must be strong; a long pulse can be weak — but never weaker than the **rheobase**, the floor of the curve. Because the floor is hard to measure, the **chronaxie** (duration needed at double the rheobase) is used to compare tissues: **nerve has a short chronaxie (more excitable), muscle a long one (less excitable).**[^4]

### Response of action potential — on repeated stimulus

```
                     ON REPEATED STIMULUS
              ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
        SUBTHRESHOLD                   SUPRATHRESHOLD
              ↓                              ↓
        ACCOMMODATION                   ADAPTATION
     (rising stimuli, no AP)    (threshold stimulus → AP; then repeated strong stimuli → no AP)
```

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - accommodation and adaptation.png]]
*Response on repeated stimulus — subthreshold stimuli of rising strength give no AP (accommodation); a threshold stimulus gives an AP; repeated suprathreshold stimuli give no AP (adaptation) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Subthreshold**[^pre-sub] → **Accommodation**[^ety-accommodation][^pre-ad][^suf-ation].
- **Suprathreshold**[^pre-supra] → **Adaptation**[^ety-adaptation].[^5]

## 🩹 Nerve Injury

- **m/c cause:** **Bone fractures**[^ety-fracture].[^6]
- **m/c part susceptible to injury:** **Axon**[^ety-axon].
- **Based on the nature of injury:**
  - **Degeneration**[^ety-degeneration][^pre-de].
  - **Regeneration**[^ety-regeneration][^pre-re].

### Degeneration

**Distal**[^ety-distal] **Degeneration:**
- AKA **Wallerian**[^ety-waller] **Degeneration.**
- **Earliest (within 24 hours of injury).**
- **Changes:**
  - a) **Axonal degeneration.**
  - b) **Myelin degeneration.**
  - c) **Phagocytosis**[^ety-phagocytosis][^suf-cyte][^suf-osis] **of debris:** clearing the site of injury.[^7]

**Proximal**[^ety-proximal] **Degeneration:**
- **36 hours after injury.**
- **Changes:**
  - **Nucleus:** pushed to the **periphery**[^ety-periphery][^pre-peri].
  - **Nissl body**[^ety-nissl]**:** **Chromatolysis**[^ety-chromatolysis][^suf-lysis] (broken down).[^8]

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - distal and proximal degeneration.png]]
*Axon injury — above: proximal cell body with central nucleus and intact Nissl body, the injured axon with its myelin and distal synapse; below: proximal degeneration — nucleus peripheral, Nissl body broken up (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| | **Distal (Wallerian) degeneration** | **Proximal degeneration** |
|---|---|---|
| **Where** | Axon + myelin **distal** to the cut | Cell body (and axon up to the first node) |
| **When** | **Earliest — within 24 h** | **36 h** after injury |
| **Changes** | Axonal + myelin degeneration, phagocytosis of debris | Nucleus → periphery; Nissl body → chromatolysis |

### Regeneration
- **90 hours after injury.**
- **Slow process: 1–3 mm/day.**
- **Tinel**[^ety-tinel] **sign:**
  - **Procedure:** tapping at the site of the injured nerve.
  - **Inference:** tingling sensation (electric-shock type) felt → indicates **good prognosis**[^ety-prognosis][^suf-gnosis].[^9]

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - Tinel sign.png]]
*Tinel sign — tapping over the injured (regenerating) nerve produces tingling in its territory (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!note] Note — Encapsulating structures
> - **Epineurium**[^ety-neurium][^pre-epi]**:** outermost layer.
> - **Perineurium:** encloses bundles, axon.
> - **Endoneurium**[^pre-endo]**:** encloses axons.

![[Classification of Nerve Fibres - coverings of nerve.png]]
*Coverings of neuron — epineurium (outermost, around the whole nerve), perineurium (around each bundle/fascicle) and endoneurium (around each axon) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Classification of nerve injury
- **To clinically assess prognosis and plan treatment.**

**1) Seddon's**[^ety-seddon] **classification:**

| | **Neuropraxia**[^ety-neuropraxia][^pre-a] | **Axonotmesis**[^ety-tmesis][^suf-tmesis] | **Neurotmesis** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Features of injury** | • Due to **compression** · • **Intact nerve** · • **Local conduction block (+)** · • Eg: **Saturday night palsy**[^ety-palsy] | • **Axonal injury** · • **Wallerian degeneration** · • **Regeneration: 1–3 mm/day** | **Complete transection**[^ety-transection][^pre-trans] |
| **Recovery** | **Spontaneous**[^ety-spontaneous] | Possible | — |
| **Prognosis** | Good | Intermediate | Worst |

**2) Sunderland**[^ety-sunderland] **classification:**

| Grade | Types of injury |
|---|---|
| **I** | Neuropraxia |
| **II** | Axonotmesis |
| **III** | Axonotmesis + **endoneurium disrupted** |
| **IV** | Axonotmesis + **endoneurium and perineurium disrupted** |
| **V** | Neurotmesis (axon + epi + peri + endo disrupted): **worst prognosis** |

- **In Grade IV & V: surgical intervention.**[^10]

> [!info] 🧠 How the two systems line up
> Seddon has three steps; Sunderland splits Seddon's axonotmesis by **how many sheaths are torn**. The more connective-tissue tubes survive, the better the regrowing axon is guided back to its target — so grades I–III usually recover on their own, while IV–V (tubes gone) need the surgeon.

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **Size and myelin decide everything about a nerve fibre.** A thick, heavily myelinated fibre (Aα) has low internal resistance and saltatory conduction, so it is fast (up to 120 m/s), has a brief spike and is the first to fail under **pressure**; a thin, unmyelinated C fibre is slow (≈ 2 m/s), has a long spike and survives hypoxia best — while the thin myelinated B fibre is the most hypoxia-sensitive. That is why the classifications line up with function: proprioception and α motor in the fastest fibres, slow pain and postganglionic autonomic in the slowest.
>
> The same physics governs excitability (a more excitable tissue needs less time at twice the rheobase — a short chronaxie) and recovery after injury: the axon is the part that dies, the connective-tissue tubes are what guide it back, and the number of tubes torn (Seddon, Sunderland) predicts the outcome.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> A mixed nerve carries A, B and C fibres side by side, each conducting at its own velocity (compound action potential). All fire when a stimulus of adequate strength and duration (above rheobase, around chronaxie) arrives; slowly rising stimuli are ignored (accommodation) and sustained strong ones fade (adaptation).
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Compression of the nerve (sleeping with the arm over a chair) blocks the large Aα and Aβ fibres first — proprioception and touch go, pain and autonomic function survive. This is **neuropraxia**: the axon is intact, there is only a local conduction block, and recovery is spontaneous within days to weeks.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> If the axon is crushed or cut, the distal segment undergoes **Wallerian degeneration within 24 h** and the cell body shows **chromatolysis** (36 h). Regrowth starts around 90 h and creeps at 1–3 mm/day, signalled by an advancing **Tinel sign**. If the endoneurial and perineurial tubes are destroyed (Sunderland IV–V, Seddon neurotmesis) the axon cannot find its way and surgical repair is required.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → trauma / fracture / compression → conduction block (neuropraxia) → axon loss with Wallerian degeneration (axonotmesis) → sheath disruption (Sunderland III–IV) → complete transection (neurotmesis) → no recovery without surgery
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The grade decides management: neuropraxia is observed, axonotmesis is followed with serial Tinel signs and nerve-conduction studies, and grade IV–V injuries are explored and repaired. The fibre type that fails first tells you the mechanism (pressure → A fibres; hypoxia → B fibres).

> 120 m/s (Aα) → 2 m/s (C); Wallerian degeneration < 24 h, proximal degeneration 36 h, regeneration from 90 h at 1–3 mm/day; Sunderland I–III spontaneous potential, IV–V surgical.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "C fibres are the most sensitive to hypoxia because they are the smallest"**
>
> **What students think:** The thinnest fibre fails first under every insult.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Each insult has its own order. Pressure blocks the largest (Aα > Aβ) first; hypoxia blocks **B** most and **C least**; local anaesthetics block the small fibres (C, then B) before the large ones.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Pressure → big first; hypoxia → B first, C last; local anaesthetic → small first.
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up rheobase and chronaxie**
>
> **What students think:** Chronaxie is the minimum time at rheobase strength.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Rheobase is a **strength** (the minimum current that works with an infinitely long pulse); chronaxie is a **time** — the minimum duration at **twice** the rheobase. A shorter chronaxie means **greater** excitability (nerve > muscle).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Rheo = current strength; chron = time; chronaxie ∝ 1/excitability."
>
> **Trap 3: Accommodation vs adaptation**
>
> **What students think:** They are synonyms.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** On the page, **subthreshold** repeated (slowly rising) stimuli → **accommodation** (threshold rises, no AP); **suprathreshold** repeated stimuli → **adaptation** (response declines though the stimulus continues).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Below threshold the nerve **accommodates** — it raises its threshold so a slowly creeping weak stimulus never catches up; above threshold it **adapts** — it fires, then its response wanes to the repeated strong stimulus.
>
> **Trap 4: Which segment degenerates in Wallerian degeneration**
>
> **What students think:** The proximal stump degenerates first.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Wallerian degeneration is the **distal** segment, separated from its cell body (its protein factory), and it starts **earliest — within 24 h**. The proximal reaction (chromatolysis, peripheral nucleus) comes later (36 h).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Waller = cut-off end dies."
>
> **Trap 5: Neuropraxia needs surgery**
>
> **What students think:** Any nerve palsy after injury needs exploration.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In neuropraxia (Sunderland I) the axon is intact with only a local conduction block — recovery is spontaneous and prognosis good. Surgery is for Sunderland IV & V (neurotmesis, sheaths disrupted).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Ask "what is still intact?" — axon intact = wait; tubes destroyed = operate.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Erlanger–Gasser** (Nobel Prize) — based on **myelin**: present → **A (α, β, γ, δ), B**; absent → **C**.
> 2. Aα → C: diameter **largest → smallest**, velocity **120 m/s → 2 m/s**, spike duration **least → most**.
> 3. **Aα:** proprioception + α motor · **Aβ:** touch · **Aγ:** muscle spindle (γ motor, tone) · **Aδ:** fast (1st) pain · **B:** preganglionic autonomic · **C:** slow (2nd) pain + **postganglionic autonomic**.
> 4. **Compression: Aα > Aβ**; **hypoxia: most B, least C**.
> 5. **Lloyd–Hunt** = sensory only: **Ia (spindle) & Ib (Golgi tendon organ) = Aα, II = Aβ, III = Aδ, IV = C**.
> 6. **Rheobase** = minimum **strength**; **chronaxie** = minimum **time at 2 × rheobase**; **chronaxie ∝ 1/excitability** → nerve: chronaxie ↓, excitability ↑; muscle: the reverse.
> 7. Repeated **subthreshold → accommodation**; repeated **suprathreshold → adaptation**.
> 8. Nerve injury: m/c cause **bone fractures**; m/c part **axon**.
> 9. **Distal = Wallerian degeneration — earliest, within 24 h** (axon, myelin, phagocytosis); **proximal — 36 h** (nucleus to periphery, **chromatolysis** of Nissl bodies).
> 10. **Regeneration — 90 h, 1–3 mm/day; Tinel sign** (tingling on tapping) → **good prognosis**.
> 11. **Epineurium** outermost · **perineurium** bundles · **endoneurium** axons.
> 12. **Seddon:** neuropraxia (compression, intact nerve, conduction block, **Saturday night palsy**, spontaneous, good) · axonotmesis (Wallerian, 1–3 mm/day, intermediate) · neurotmesis (complete transection, worst).
> 13. **Sunderland:** I neuropraxia · II axonotmesis · III + endoneurium · IV + endo + perineurium · V neurotmesis (worst) → **IV & V need surgery**.

[^1]: Ganong's table (approximate): **Aα** 12–20 µm, 70–120 m/s, spike 0.4–0.5 ms · **Aβ** 5–12 µm, 30–70 m/s · **Aγ** 3–6 µm, 15–30 m/s · **Aδ** 2–5 µm, 12–30 m/s · **B** < 3 µm, 3–15 m/s, spike 1.2 ms · **C** 0.4–1.2 µm (unmyelinated), 0.5–2 m/s, spike 2 ms. The page gives the two extremes (120 and 2 m/s).
[^2]: Ganong's order of susceptibility: **hypoxia** B > A > C; **pressure** A > B > C; **local anaesthetics** C > B > A. A crossed leg "going to sleep" first loses touch and position sense (A fibres), while pain (C) persists — and a spinal anaesthetic blocks autonomic (B) and pain (C) before motor (A) function.
[^3]: Group Ia = primary (annulospiral) endings of muscle spindles; Ib = Golgi tendon organs; II = secondary (flower-spray) spindle endings and touch/pressure receptors; III = Aδ (fast pain, cold); IV = C (slow pain, warmth, itch). Erlanger–Gasser letters are used mainly for cutaneous and motor fibres, Lloyd–Hunt numerals for muscle afferents.
[^4]: The term *chronaxie* was introduced by Louis Lapicque (1909). In electrodiagnosis a denervated muscle has a much longer chronaxie (it responds only to long pulses), so its strength–duration curve moves to the right; reinnervation shifts it back.
[^5]: Accommodation: when a stimulus rises slowly, Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ channels open while it rises, so the threshold climbs ahead of the stimulus and no AP fires. Adaptation is the fall in firing (or response) during a sustained or repeated suprathreshold stimulus — the property that divides receptors into rapidly and slowly adapting types ([[Sensory Receptors]]).
[^6]: The classic example is the radial nerve in a fracture of the shaft of the humerus (spiral groove); other common causes of peripheral nerve injury are penetrating trauma, traction (brachial plexus) and compression.
[^7]: Augustus Waller described the process in 1850 in cut frog glossopharyngeal and hypoglossal nerves. Schwann cells and invading macrophages clear the debris; the Schwann cells then proliferate inside the preserved endoneurial tubes, forming the bands of Büngner that guide the regrowing axon.
[^8]: This cell-body response (the "axon reaction") — swelling of the soma, displacement of the nucleus to the periphery and dissolution of Nissl substance (chromatolysis) — reflects the switch from neurotransmitter production to synthesis of structural proteins for regrowth; standard texts describe it beginning within the first days and peaking at 1–3 weeks. The page's "36 hours" is kept in the body.
[^9]: Named after Jules Tinel (1915), and described the same year by Paul Hoffmann (hence "Hoffmann–Tinel sign"). Regenerating axon sprouts are mechanosensitive, so tapping over their tips gives tingling; a Tinel sign that moves distally over weeks shows regeneration is progressing. The same sign is used to detect median nerve compression in carpal tunnel syndrome. Regrowth is often quoted as ~1 mm/day (≈ 1 inch a month).
[^10]: In Sunderland III the endoneurial tubes are disrupted but the perineurium holds the fascicles together, so recovery is possible but incomplete (misdirected axons). In IV the fascicles are disrupted and only the epineurium remains (a neuroma-in-continuity), in V the nerve is divided — both need resection and repair or grafting. Mackinnon later added a "grade VI" for mixed injuries.
[^ety-erlanger]: **Erlanger–Gasser classification** — Joseph Erlanger (1874–1965) and Herbert Spencer Gasser (1888–1963), American physiologists, who recorded the compound action potential with the cathode-ray oscilloscope and shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1944) for discoveries on the functions of single nerve fibres.
[^ety-lloyd]: **Lloyd–Hunt classification** — named after the neurophysiologists David P. C. Lloyd and Carlton C. Hunt, whose work on muscle and skin afferents in the 1940s–50s gave the Roman-numeral groups I–IV for sensory fibres.
[^ety-myelin]: **myelin** — Greek *myelos*, "marrow" + *-in* → the fatty "marrow" of nerves; coined by Rudolf Virchow (1854). Do not confuse with *myo-* (muscle); *myelitis* is inflammation of the spinal cord, *myeloid* refers to bone marrow.
[^ety-sensory]: **sensory** — Latin *sentire*, "to feel, perceive" + *-ory* → carrying feeling towards the CNS. **Differentiate:** *sensory* = afferent (to the CNS) vs *motor* (Latin *movere*, to move) = efferent (from the CNS).
[^ety-proprioception]: **proprioception** — Latin *proprius*, "one's own" + *-ception* (*capere*, "to take, receive") → perception of one's own body — joint position and movement sense. **Differentiate:** *exteroception* (from outside: touch, sight) · *interoception* (from viscera) · *proprioception* (from muscles and joints).
[^ety-spindle]: **muscle spindle** — Old English *spinel*, the tapering rod used in spinning → the spindle-shaped stretch receptor lying among muscle fibres; its sensitivity is set by γ motor neurons (Aγ).
[^ety-ganglionic]: **preganglionic / postganglionic** — Latin *pre-*, "before" / *post-*, "after" + Greek *ganglion*, "a knot, swelling" → autonomic fibres before (B, myelinated) or after (C, unmyelinated) the synapse in the autonomic ganglion.
[^ety-autonomic]: **autonomic** — Greek *autos*, "self" + *nomos*, "law" + *-ic* → self-governing; the nervous system that runs viscera without voluntary control (term of J. N. Langley, 1898).
[^ety-compression]: **compression** — Latin *com-*, "together" + *premere*, "to press" + *-sion* → pressing together. The largest myelinated fibres fail first under pressure.
[^ety-hypoxia]: **hypoxia** — Greek *hypo-*, "under, deficient" + *oxy(gen)* + *-ia*, "condition" → too little oxygen at the tissue. **Differentiate:** *hypoxia* (low tissue O₂) · *hypoxaemia* (low blood O₂) · *anoxia* (no O₂) · *ischaemia* (low blood flow — hypoxia plus lack of nutrients and waste removal).
[^ety-golgi]: **Golgi tendon organ** — Camillo Golgi (1843–1926), Italian histologist, who invented the silver ("black reaction") stain and shared the 1906 Nobel Prize with Ramón y Cajal; he described the tension receptor at the muscle–tendon junction (and the Golgi apparatus).
[^ety-tendon]: **tendon** — Medieval Latin *tendo*, from *tendere*, "to stretch" (influenced by Greek *tenōn*, sinew) → the stretched cord joining muscle to bone; the tendon organ senses its tension.
[^ety-stimulus]: **stimulus** — Latin, "a goad, a pointed stick for driving cattle" → anything that prods an excitable tissue into a response; plural *stimuli*.
[^ety-rheobase]: **rheobase** — Greek *rheos*, "a current, stream" + *basis*, "base, foundation" → the base (minimum) current; the floor of the strength–duration curve.
[^ety-chronaxie]: **chronaxie** — Greek *chronos*, "time" + *axia*, "value" → the time value; Louis Lapicque's 1909 index of excitability: the duration needed at twice the rheobase.
[^ety-excitability]: **excitability** — Latin *ex-*, "out" + *citare*, "to set in motion, rouse" + *-ability* → the capacity to be roused into an action potential. **Differentiate:** *excitability* (ability to respond) · *conductivity* (ability to propagate) · *contractility* (muscle's ability to shorten).
[^ety-accommodation]: **accommodation** — Latin *ad-* (*ac-*), "to" + *commodus*, "fitting, convenient" + *-ation*, the process → the process of fitting oneself to a change; the nerve "gets used to" a slowly rising current and raises its threshold. **Differentiate:** accommodation of a **nerve** (rising threshold to a slowly rising stimulus) vs accommodation of the **eye** (lens changing focus) vs **adaptation** (declining response to a sustained stimulus).
[^ety-adaptation]: **adaptation** — Latin *ad-*, "to" + *aptare*, "to fit" (*aptus*, fitted) + *-ation* → fitting to the circumstances; the response wanes although the strong stimulus continues (rapidly vs slowly adapting receptors).
[^ety-fracture]: **fracture** — Latin *fractura*, "a breaking" (*frangere*, to break) → a break in a bone; same root as *fragment* and *refractory*.
[^ety-axon]: **axon** — Greek *axōn*, "axis, axle" → the long process that carries impulses away from the cell body; the part of the neuron most exposed to injury.
[^ety-degeneration]: **degeneration** — Latin *de-*, "down, away from" + *genus*, "kind, race" + *-ation* → falling away from one's kind; tissue losing its normal structure and function. **Differentiate:** *degeneration* (structural decay of a living part) · *necrosis* (cell death in living tissue) · *atrophy* (shrinkage of a part that is still alive).
[^ety-regeneration]: **regeneration** — Latin *re-*, "again" + *generare*, "to beget, produce" + *-ation* → producing again; regrowth of the axon from the proximal stump. **Differentiate:** *regeneration* (the same tissue regrows) vs *repair / fibrosis* (replacement by scar).
[^ety-distal]: **distal** — Latin *distare*, "to stand apart" + *-al* → farther from the origin (here, from the cell body). Opposite *proximal*.
[^ety-waller]: **Wallerian degeneration** — Augustus Volney Waller (1816–1870), English physiologist, who described in 1850 the degeneration of nerve fibres separated from their cell bodies.
[^ety-phagocytosis]: **phagocytosis** — Greek *phagein*, "to eat" + *kytos*, "cell" + *-osis*, "process" → cell-eating; coined after Élie Metchnikoff's work (1880s). **Differentiate:** *phagocytosis* (engulfing particles) · *pinocytosis* (*pinein*, to drink — engulfing fluid) · *exocytosis* (expelling vesicle contents).
[^ety-proximal]: **proximal** — Latin *proximus*, "nearest" + *-al* → nearer the origin (the cell body). Opposite *distal*.
[^ety-periphery]: **periphery** — Greek *peri-*, "around" + *pherein*, "to carry" → the line carried around; the outer edge — the nucleus is pushed to the edge of the swollen soma.
[^ety-nissl]: **Nissl body** — Franz Nissl (1860–1919), German neuropathologist, whose basic-dye stain shows the rough endoplasmic reticulum of neurons as blue granules (Nissl substance).
[^ety-chromatolysis]: **chromatolysis** — Greek *chrōma*, "colour" + *lysis*, "loosening, dissolution" → dissolution of the stainable (coloured) material — the Nissl substance disperses so the soma stains pale. **Differentiate:** *chromatolysis* (Nissl loss in an injured neuron, reversible) vs *karyolysis* (fading of a dead cell's nucleus) vs *haemolysis* (red-cell rupture).
[^ety-tinel]: **Tinel sign** — Jules Tinel (1879–1952), French neurologist, who described the "tingling sign" of regenerating nerves in 1915 from war injuries (Paul Hoffmann described it independently the same year).
[^ety-prognosis]: **prognosis** — Greek *pro-*, "before" + *gnōsis*, "knowledge" → fore-knowing the outcome. **Differentiate:** *prognosis* (likely outcome) vs *diagnosis* (*dia-*, through — knowing what the disease is).
[^ety-neurium]: **epineurium / perineurium / endoneurium** — Greek *neuron*, "nerve" + *-ium*, a layer, with *epi-* "upon" (the outer coat of the whole nerve), *peri-* "around" (around each fascicle — it forms the blood–nerve barrier) and *endo-* "within" (around each axon). The same pattern as epimysium / perimysium / endomysium in muscle.
[^ety-seddon]: **Seddon's classification** — Sir Herbert Seddon (1903–1977), British orthopaedic surgeon, who classified nerve injuries into neurapraxia, axonotmesis and neurotmesis from wartime injuries (1942–43).
[^ety-neuropraxia]: **neuropraxia (neurapraxia)** — Greek *neuron*, "nerve" + *a-*, "not" + *praxis*, "action, doing" → a nerve that does not act (non-function) although it is anatomically intact. **Differentiate:** *neurapraxia* (block, intact axon) · *axonotmesis* (axon cut, sheath intact) · *neurotmesis* (whole nerve cut).
[^ety-tmesis]: **axonotmesis / neurotmesis** — Greek *axōn*, "axis" or *neuron*, "nerve" + *tmēsis*, "a cutting" → cutting of the axon only vs cutting of the whole nerve.
[^ety-palsy]: **palsy** — Middle English *palesie*, from Old French *paralisie*, from Greek *paralysis* (*para-*, "beside, faulty" + *lyein*, "to loosen") → loss of motor power. *Saturday night palsy* — radial nerve compression in the spiral groove when a person (often intoxicated) sleeps with the arm hung over a chair back.
[^ety-transection]: **transection** — Latin *trans-*, "across" + *secare*, "to cut" + *-tion* → a cut straight across. **Differentiate:** *transection* (cut across) · *resection* (cut out a part) · *dissection* (cut apart to separate).
[^ety-spontaneous]: **spontaneous** — Latin *sponte*, "of one's own accord" → happening without outside treatment.
[^ety-sunderland]: **Sunderland classification** — Sir Sydney Sunderland (1910–1993), Australian anatomist, who refined Seddon's scheme into five grades in 1951 according to which connective-tissue layers are disrupted.
[^pre-pro]: **pro-** — before, forward, on behalf of: proprioception (*proprius*, "one's own", usually derived from *pro privo*, "for the individual"), prognosis, prophase. Don't confuse with **proto-** first · **pre-** before (Latin) · **post-** after.
[^pre-pre]: **pre-** — before: preganglionic, prevertebral. Don't confuse with **pro-** forward · **para-** beside · **peri-** around · **post-** after.
[^pre-post]: **post-** — after, behind: postganglionic, postsynaptic, postpartum. Don't confuse with **pre-** before · **posterior** (the back side, same root).
[^pre-auto]: **auto-** — self: autonomic, autoimmune, autoantibody. Don't confuse with **allo-** other · **hetero-** different.
[^pre-com]: **com- / con- / co-** — together: compression, compound, conduction. Don't confuse with **contra-** against · **counter-** opposite.
[^pre-hypo]: **hypo-** — under, below, deficient: hypoxia, hypotension, hypothalamus. Don't confuse with **hyper-** over · **hypno-** sleep · **sub-** (Latin twin).
[^suf-ia]: **-ia** — a condition or state: hypoxia, neuralgia, anaemia. Don't confuse with **-iasis** a disease from an agent · **-ism** a state or doctrine.
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (e-, ef-)** — out of, outward: excitability, efferent, excretion. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek) · **extra-** beyond.
[^pre-sub]: **sub-** — below, under, less than: subthreshold, subclavian, subacute. Don't confuse with **supra- / super-** above · **hypo-** (Greek twin) · **infra-** beneath.
[^pre-supra]: **supra-** — above, beyond: suprathreshold, supraclavicular, suprarenal. Don't confuse with **super-** over, excessive · **sub-** below · **hyper-** (Greek twin).
[^pre-ad]: **ad- (ac-, af-, ag-…)** — to, towards; the *d* turns into the next consonant: accommodation, adaptation, afferent. Don't confuse with **ab-** away from · **a- / an-** without.
[^suf-ation]: **-ation / -ition / -ion** — the process or result of an action: accommodation, adaptation, degeneration, regeneration. Don't confuse with **-osis** a condition or process of disease · **-ity** a quality.
[^pre-de]: **de-** — down, away, removal: degeneration, demyelination, depolarisation. Don't confuse with **dis-** apart · **dys-** abnormal.
[^pre-re]: **re-** — again, back: regeneration, repolarisation, recovery. Don't confuse with **retro-** backwards, behind · **pre-** before.
[^suf-cyte]: **-cyte / cyto-** — cell: phagocytosis, monocyte, astrocyte. Don't confuse with **-cytosis** used for a process (phagocytosis) or an excess of cells (leucocytosis) · **-blast** an immature cell.
[^suf-osis]: **-osis** — a process, condition or state: phagocytosis, sclerosis, necrosis. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation · **-iasis** infestation.
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: periphery, perineurium, perimysium. Don't confuse with **para-** beside · **epi-** upon · **pre-** before.
[^suf-lysis]: **-lysis / -lytic** — loosening, dissolution, breakdown: chromatolysis, haemolysis, glycolysis. Don't confuse with **-stasis** standing still · **-ptosis** falling (drooping).
[^suf-gnosis]: **-gnosis** — knowledge, recognition: prognosis, diagnosis, agnosia. Don't confuse with **-genesis** origin, production · **-nomy** law.
[^pre-epi]: **epi-** — upon, over, outer: epineurium, epimysium, epidermis. Don't confuse with **endo-** within · **peri-** around · **hypo-** under.
[^pre-endo]: **endo-** — within: endoneurium, endomysium, endothelium. Don't confuse with **epi-** upon · **exo-** outside · **ecto-** outer.
[^pre-a]: **a- / an-** — not, without: neurapraxia (no action), anoxia, anaemia, atrophy. Don't confuse with **ad- (a-)** towards (*a*scend) · **ab-** away from · **ana-** up, again (anabolism).
[^suf-tmesis]: **-tmesis / -tomy** — cutting: axonotmesis, neurotmesis; *-tomy* = a surgical cutting (neurotomy). Don't confuse with **-ectomy** cutting out (neurectomy) · **-stomy** making an opening · **-tome** a cutting instrument or segment (dermatome).
[^pre-trans]: **trans-** — across, through: transection, transmission, transport. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **dia-** through · **per-** through (Latin).
