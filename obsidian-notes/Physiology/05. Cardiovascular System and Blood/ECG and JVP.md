# 📈 ECG and JVP

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The **electrocardiogram (ECG)**[^ety-electrocardiography][^pre-electro][^suf-graphy] records, from the skin, the **potential difference between a (+) and a (−) electrode**[^ety-electrode]; as the wave of depolarisation and repolarisation moves through the heart, each lead "sees" it coming (upward **deflection**[^ety-deflection][^pre-de]) or going (downward deflection). The **JVP**[^ety-jugular] is the pressure wave of the right atrium seen in the internal jugular vein.
> - ✅ Which lead looks at which wall (and which artery)?
> - ✅ What does each wave, segment and interval mean, and what are their normal values?
> - ✅ What is the cardiac axis, and what do the a, c, v waves and x, y descents of the JVP stand for?

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Twelve Cameras Around a Moving Wave

Think of the heart's electrical wave as a runner and each lead as a **camera** with a positive end. A runner coming **towards** the camera's + end → **upward** blip; running **away** → **downward** blip; running across → small or biphasic. Twelve cameras (6 in the frontal plane, 6 around the chest) let you rebuild where the runner went — and a wall that has died (infarct) shows up in the cameras that face it.

```
12 LEADS
 ├─ 6 LIMB (frontal plane)
 │    ├─ Bipolar  I, II, III    (Einthoven: II = I + III)
 │    └─ Unipolar aVR, aVL, aVF (Goldberger: augmented ×1.5)
 └─ 6 CHEST V1–V6 (horizontal plane)

ECG  P → PR seg (AV delay) → QRS (ventricular depol) → ST (isoelectric) → T (ventricular repol) → U
JVP  a (RA contraction) · c (tricuspid bulge) · x (RA relaxation) · v (RA filling) · y (RA emptying)
```

## 📈 Electrocardiography (ECG)

### 🔌 ECG leads

- **Measures potential difference b/w (+) & (−) electrodes which causes deflections**
  - → **Represented as waves in ECG.**

```
                         12 leads
            ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
       6 limb leads                    6 chest leads
     ┌──────┴────────┐                  • V1 – V6
  Bipolar      Unipolar (Augmented)
  • Lead I     • aVR
  • Lead II    • aVL
  • Lead III   • aVF
```

- **Bipolar**[^ety-bipolar][^pre-bi] **limb leads:** Lead I, Lead II, Lead III.
- **Unipolar**[^pre-uni] **(augmented**[^ety-augmented]**) limb leads:** aVR, aVL, aVF.
- **Chest leads:** V1 – V6.[^1]

### 🦾 Limb leads

#### Bipolar leads
- **Einthoven's triangle**[^ety-einthoven]
- **Einthoven's law : Electric potential of lead II = lead I + lead III.**[^2]

| Lead | (−) electrode | (+) electrode |
|---|---|---|
| **Lead I** | Right arm | Left arm |
| **Lead II** | Right arm | Left foot |
| **Lead III** | Left arm | Left foot |

#### Augmented unipolar leads
- **AKA Goldberger's**[^ety-goldberger] **leads.**
- **One limb : (+)**
- **Remaining 2 limbs : (−)**
- **Potentials augmented by 1.5 times.**[^3]

| Lead | (+) limb | (−) limbs combined |
|---|---|---|
| **aVR** | Right arm | Left arm + left foot |
| **aVL** | Left arm | Right arm + left foot |
| **aVF** | Left foot | Right arm + left arm |

![Goldberger's augmented unipolar leads aVR, aVL and aVF (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/ECG-Goldberger.svg/800px-ECG-Goldberger.svg.png)

### 🩺 Clinical significance

- Each lead **represents the cardiac activity** of the surface it faces.

![[ECG and JVP - leads viewing the heart.png]]
*The limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF) and chest leads (V1–V6) drawn over the heart — each lead represents the cardiac activity of the wall it faces (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Leads | Surface | Blood supply |
|---|---|---|
| **V3, V4** | **Anterior**[^ety-anterior] **wall** | **Left anterior descending artery (LAD)** |
| **V1, V2** | **Septal**[^ety-septum] **wall** | **Left anterior descending artery (LAD)** |
| **aVL, I, V5, V6** | **Lateral wall** | **Left coronary**[^ety-coronary] **artery (LCA) – circumflex**[^ety-circumflex][^pre-circum] **branch** |
| **II, III, aVF** | **Inferior wall** | **Right coronary artery (RCA)** |

> [!danger] LAD = **"widow maker's**[^ety-widow-maker] **artery"** (m/c artery causing **MI**[^ety-infarction][^pre-in])
> The LAD supplies both the anterior and the septal wall (V1–V4), so its occlusion kills the largest mass of left ventricle.[^4]

#### Waveform

| Part of the tracing | Represents (page) | Normal value (figure) |
|---|---|---|
| **P wave** | **Atrial depolarisation**[^ety-depolarisation][^suf-ation] | P-wave duration ~0.1 s ⚠[^6] |
| **PR segment**[^ety-segment] | **A-V nodal delay** | — |
| **PR interval**[^ety-interval][^pre-inter] | Start of P → start of QRS | **0.12 – 0.22 s** |
| **QRS complex** | **Ventricular depolarisation** | **QRS duration < 0.12 s** |
| **J point** | Junction of QRS and ST segment | — |
| **ST segment** | Isoelectric — all ventricle depolarised | — |
| **T wave** | **Ventricular repolarisation**[^ety-repolarisation][^pre-re] | — |
| **U wave** | **Late repolarisation of Purkinje**[^ety-purkinje] **fibres, papillary**[^ety-papillary] **muscles** | — |
| **QT interval** | **Total ventricular activity (TVA) = ventricular depolarisation + repolarisation** | **0.40 s** |
| **RR interval** | Distance between R waves (one cardiac cycle) | — |

**Colour key on the page:** 🟡 yellow → **waves** · 🔵 blue → **segments : isoelectric**[^ety-isoelectric][^pre-iso] **lines (no current flow)** · 🟢 green → **intervals**.

🧠 *A **segment** is a flat stretch between two waves (no wave inside it); an **interval** always contains at least one wave plus a segment. So the PR **segment** is only the AV-nodal pause, while the PR **interval** is atrial depolarisation + that pause.*

![[ECG and JVP - normal ECG waveform.png]]
*The page's labelled waveform — P (atrial depolarisation), PR segment (A-V nodal delay), QRS (ventricular depolarisation), J point, T (ventricular repolarisation), U (late repolarisation of Purkinje fibres and papillary muscles), QT interval = total ventricular activity; yellow waves, blue segments, green intervals (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Normal sinus rhythm ECG with P wave, PR interval, QRS complex, ST segment, T wave and QT interval labelled (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9e/SinusRhythmLabels.svg/800px-SinusRhythmLabels.svg.png)
*Schematic normal sinus rhythm with every wave, segment and interval labelled — a clean version of the page's waveform (Wikimedia Commons, public domain).*

| Segment / interval | Applied aspect |
|---|---|
| **ST segment : elevation / depression** | Detects **ischemic**[^ety-ischaemia][^suf-aemia] **/ infarcted tissue** (D/t current flow)[^7] |
| **PR interval** | **> 0.20 s → heart block**[^8] |
| **QT interval** | Changes with heart rate, therefore **Bazett's**[^ety-bazett] **formula** : **QTc (corrected QT) = QT interval / √RR interval**[^9] |
| **RR interval** | Used to calculate heart rate (HR) : **HR = 1500 / no. of small squares b/w 2 successive R waves**[^10] |

### 🧭 Cardiac axis[^ety-axis]

#### Direction of current flow

**Whole heart:**

```
         Depolarisation  ───────►
   (L)                               (R)
         ◄───────  Repolarisation

   Septum  ⇄  Endocardium  ⇄  Epicardium
   (depolarisation →, repolarisation ← — opposite directions)
```

**Individual myocardial**[^ety-myocardium][^pre-myo] **cell:**

```
         Depolarisation  ───────►
   (L)                               (R)
         Repolarisation  ───────►

   Septum  ⇉  Endocardium  ⇉  Epicardium
   (depolarisation → and repolarisation → — same direction)
```

🧠 *In the whole heart the wave of depolarisation runs from **endocardium**[^pre-endo][^suf-cardium] **to epicardium**[^pre-epi], but repolarisation runs back from **epicardium to endocardium** (the outer layer recovers first). A wave of repolarisation moving away from a lead gives the same (upright) deflection as depolarisation moving towards it — which is why the normal T wave is upright like the QRS. In a single cell both run the same way, so the repolarisation deflection is opposite to the depolarisation one.*[^11]

#### Direction of overall electrical activity

- **Leads directed towards mean QRS axis** → **(+) deflection in ECG.**
- **Direction away from mean QRS axis** (aVR) → **(−) deflection on ECG.**
- **Cardiac axis / mean QRS axis : directed towards (left ventricle — ↑ muscle mass)** — drawn at **59°** on the page.[^12]

![[ECG and JVP - cardiac axis.png]]
*The page's axis wheel — leads pointing towards the mean QRS axis (59°) record a (+) deflection, aVR at −150° points away and records a (−) deflection (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Hexaxial reference system — leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF and their angles (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/56/Hexaxial_reference_system.svg/800px-Hexaxial_reference_system.svg.png)

- **Recorded along with routine ECG.**
- **Significance : cardiac conduction blocks → intervals prolonged.**

## 🫀 Jugular Venous Pressure (JVP)

- **Recorded in internal jugular vein.**[^14]

🧠 *The internal jugular vein has no valve between it and the right atrium, so it works like a manometer: every pressure change in the right atrium shows up as a ripple in the neck.*

| Component | Type | Cause (page) |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | Wave | **Right atrial contraction** |
| **c** | Wave | **Right ventricular contraction + bulging of tricuspid**[^ety-tricuspid][^pre-tri] **valve into right atrium**[^ety-atrium] |
| **x** | Descent | **Right atrial relaxation** |
| **v** | Wave | **Right atrial filling** |
| **y** | Descent | **Right atrial emptying** |

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> An ECG lead is a **voltmeter with a direction**: a wave of depolarisation moving **towards its positive pole** writes an upward deflection, moving away writes a downward one, and an isoelectric line means **no current is flowing** (the whole muscle is in the same state). Every rule in this chapter follows — the axis points to the left ventricle because that is where the biggest mass of muscle depolarises; aVR is negative because it looks at the heart from the opposite side; leads facing a dead wall show the injury current (ST shift) of that wall.
>
> Time is read off the paper (0.04 s per small square at 25 mm/s), so the intervals become clinical numbers: PR > 0.20 s = AV block, QRS ≥ 0.12 s = bundle-branch block, QT must be corrected for rate (Bazett). The JVP is the same idea for pressure: a valveless vein shows every right-atrial event as a wave.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Sinus P before every QRS, PR 0.12–0.20 s, QRS < 0.12 s, isoelectric ST, upright T, QT ~0.40 s; mean axis about +59° (normal −30° to +90°). JVP shows a, c, v waves with x and y descents, height ≤ 3–4 cm above the sternal angle.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Changes appear in intervals before symptoms: a long PR (first-degree block), a widening QRS, a long QTc from drugs or hypokalaemia (with prominent U waves); ischaemia gives ST depression in the leads facing the under-perfused wall; a mild axis shift follows ventricular hypertrophy.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> ST elevation in a contiguous lead group localises an infarct — V1–V4 (LAD, anterior/septal), I/aVL/V5–V6 (circumflex, lateral), II/III/aVF (RCA, inferior); complete heart block dissociates P from QRS and produces cannon a waves in the JVP; right heart failure raises the JVP.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → ischaemia / conduction disease / electrolyte change → interval or ST changes (compensated) → infarction or complete block → arrhythmia, pump failure, raised JVP
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The lead group with ST elevation names the occluded artery and decides which vessel the cath lab opens; PR and QTc numbers decide whether a drug is safe; the JVP is the bedside gauge of right atrial pressure and fluid status.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Einthoven's law:** "Lead II = lead I + lead III", "bipolar triangle" → the three standard limb leads.
>
> **Goldberger's leads:** "Augmented 1.5 times", "one limb positive, two negative" → aVR, aVL, aVF.
>
> **Anterior / septal MI:** "ST elevation V1–V4", "widow maker" → LAD occlusion.
>
> **Lateral MI:** "I, aVL, V5, V6" → circumflex branch of LCA.
>
> **Inferior MI:** "II, III, aVF" → RCA.
>
> **Heart block:** "PR > 0.20 s" → first-degree AV block; His bundle ECG localises it (AH = AV node, HV = infra-Hisian).
>
> **Long QT:** "QTc by Bazett", "QT changes with heart rate" → correct before judging.
>
> **U wave:** "Late repolarisation of Purkinje fibres / papillary muscles" → prominent in hypokalaemia.
>
> **JVP:** "a wave = atrial contraction", "c = tricuspid bulge", "x = atrial relaxation", "v = atrial filling", "y = atrial emptying".
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> A flat line between waves = isoelectric (no current flow); an upright deflection = wave towards the positive pole; aVR negative in normal ECG = it looks away from the mean axis.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Small square 0.04 s / large 0.20 s; PR > 0.20 s = block; QRS ≥ 0.12 s = wide (bundle-branch block); QTc > ~0.44–0.46 s = long QT; HR = 1500 / small squares (300 / large squares).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Segment vs interval**
>
> **What students think:** PR segment and PR interval are the same.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** A segment is an isoelectric line between waves (PR segment = AV nodal delay only); an interval includes waves (PR interval = P wave + PR segment = atrial depolarisation + AV delay). Heart block is judged on the PR **interval** (> 0.20 s).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Segment = flat line only; interval = wave + line."
>
> **Trap 2: Thinking the T wave should be inverted**
>
> **What students think:** Repolarisation is the reverse of depolarisation, so T should point opposite to QRS.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In the whole heart repolarisation travels the opposite way (epicardium → endocardium). A repolarisation wave moving away from the positive electrode gives an upright deflection, so T is upright in the same leads as the QRS. Only a single cell gives opposite deflections.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Opposite direction × opposite sign = same deflection.
>
> **Trap 3: Mixing the lead groups and arteries**
>
> **What students think:** Inferior wall = LAD because it is "the big artery".
>
> **Why this is wrong:** II, III, aVF look at the inferior wall supplied (in ~85 % — right-dominant hearts) by the RCA; the LAD feeds V1–V4 (septal + anterior); the circumflex feeds I, aVL, V5, V6 (lateral).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "SAIL": Septal V1–2, Anterior V3–4, Inferior II/III/aVF, Lateral I/aVL/V5–6.
>
> **Trap 4: Reading the QT without correcting it**
>
> **What students think:** A QT of 0.40 s is always normal.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** QT shortens as heart rate rises; Bazett's QTc = QT/√RR normalises it to a rate of 60/min. A QT of 0.40 s at HR 120 (RR 0.5 s) gives QTc ≈ 0.57 s — dangerously long.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Always divide by √RR (RR in seconds).
>
> **Trap 5: The JVP c wave and x descent**
>
> **What students think:** The c wave is caused by carotid pulsation alone, or the x descent is ventricular relaxation.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page's c wave is right-ventricular contraction bulging the closed tricuspid valve into the atrium (carotid transmission may add to it); x is atrial relaxation (plus the descent of the tricuspid ring during ejection), y is atrial emptying after the tricuspid valve opens.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Waves = pressure rising (contraction, bulge, filling); descents = pressure falling (relaxation, emptying).

> [!tip]+ **🔗 Related Concepts & Cross-Topic Connections**
>
> Understanding how this topic connects to other areas of medicine deepens your knowledge and helps you see the bigger picture. These connections also help you answer integrated questions on exams.
>

> The ECG is the sum of these potentials: P = atrial phase 0, PR segment = AV nodal delay (0.1 s, 0.04 m/s), QRS = ventricular phase 0, ST = plateau, T = phase 3.
>

>

>

>

>
> **Clinical Integration:**
> The 12-lead ECG localises infarcts by lead group, measures conduction (PR, QRS, QT), estimates rate (1500 rule) and axis; the His bundle study separates AV-nodal from infra-Hisian block (which decides pacing); the JVP tracks right-sided filling pressure at the bedside.
>
> **Exam Integration:**
> Expect: Einthoven's law, Goldberger augmentation 1.5×, lead–wall–artery table, widow maker = LAD, small/large square values, PR > 0.20 s, Bazett's formula, HR = 1500/small squares, normal axis and its 59° mean, what U wave represents, and the cause of each JVP wave.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. ECG = potential difference b/w **(+) and (−) electrodes** → deflections → waves.
> 2. **12 leads** = 6 limb (bipolar **I, II, III** + augmented unipolar **aVR, aVL, aVF**) + 6 chest (**V1–V6**).
> 3. **Einthoven's law: lead II = lead I + lead III.**
> 4. **Goldberger's leads:** one limb (+), other two (−); potentials **augmented 1.5 times**.
> 5. **V3–V4 anterior & V1–V2 septal → LAD; aVL, I, V5, V6 lateral → LCx; II, III, aVF inferior → RCA.**
> 6. **LAD = widow maker's artery** (m/c artery causing MI).
> 7. ECG paper: **small square 0.04 s / 0.1 mV; large square (25 small) 0.20 s / 0.5 mV.**
> 8. P = atrial depolarisation · **PR segment = A-V nodal delay** · QRS = ventricular depolarisation · T = ventricular repolarisation · **U = late repolarisation of Purkinje fibres & papillary muscles** · **QT = total ventricular activity.**
> 9. Waves (yellow) · **segments = isoelectric lines (no current flow)** (blue) · intervals (green).
> 10. **ST elevation/depression → ischaemia/infarction · PR > 0.20 s → heart block · QTc = QT/√RR (Bazett) · HR = 1500/small squares.**
> 11. Whole heart: depolarisation endo → epi, **repolarisation epi → endo** (opposite); single cell: same direction.
> 12. **Mean QRS axis points to the left ventricle (↑ muscle mass)** — 59° on the page; leads towards it (+), away (aVR) (−).
> 13. His bundle ECG: **A = AV nodal, H = His bundle, V = ventricular**; conduction blocks → **intervals prolonged** (AH, HV).
> 14. JVP (internal jugular vein): **a** RA contraction · **c** RV contraction + tricuspid bulge · **x** RA relaxation · **v** RA filling · **y** RA emptying.

PAPER: small 0.04 s/0.1 mV · large 0.20 s/0.5 mV · HR = 1500/small squares · QTc = QT/√RR · PR > 0.20 = block
WAVES: P atria · PR seg AV delay · QRS ventricles · T repol · U Purkinje/papillary · QT = total ventricular activity
AXIS → LV (59°) · towards = + · away (aVR) = − · whole heart repol epi→endo
HIS ECG: A–H–V (AH, HV prolonged in block) · JVP: a c x v y

[^1]: Standard chest-lead positions: V1 4th intercostal space right sternal border · V2 4th ICS left sternal border · V3 midway between V2 and V4 · V4 5th ICS mid-clavicular line · V5 anterior axillary line at V4 level · V6 mid-axillary line at V4 level. The chest leads are unipolar, measured against Wilson's central terminal.
[^2]: Einthoven's law follows from Kirchhoff's voltage law: lead I = LA − RA, lead III = LL − LA, so I + III = LL − RA = lead II. It is used to check that the limb electrodes have been placed correctly.
[^3]: Goldberger (1942) removed the explored limb's own electrode from the reference terminal; the recorded voltage is then 1.5 times (50 % larger than) the voltage measured against Wilson's central terminal — hence "augmented" (aV = augmented Voltage).
[^4]: "Widow maker" usually means a proximal LAD occlusion (often a critical left main or proximal LAD lesion); the LAD's septal and diagonal branches supply the anterior wall, the anterior two-thirds of the septum and the apex. V5–V6 may also reflect the LAD's diagonal branches.
[^5]: Standard ECG settings: paper speed 25 mm/s and calibration 10 mm = 1 mV. At these settings one 1-mm small square = 0.04 s and 0.1 mV; the 10-mm tall rectangle at the start of each strip is the 1 mV calibration (standardisation) pulse.
[^6]: ⚠ The small label next to "P-wave duration" in the page's figure is barely legible; it seems to read 0.1 s. Standard texts give a normal P-wave duration of < 0.12 s (about 0.08–0.10 s).
[^7]: Injured (ischaemic or infarcted) muscle stays partially depolarised and so carries a "current of injury" between it and normal muscle during the isoelectric periods; this shifts the baseline and appears as ST elevation (transmural injury) or ST depression (subendocardial ischaemia).
[^8]: Normal PR interval 0.12–0.20 s (the page's figure prints 0.12–0.22 s; the applied table uses > 0.20 s for block). PR > 0.20 s with every P conducted = first-degree AV block.
[^9]: Bazett's formula (1920): QTc = QT / √RR, with both in seconds. Upper limit of normal QTc ≈ 0.44 s (men ~0.45 s, women ~0.46–0.47 s). It over-corrects at high heart rates; Fridericia (cube root) is an alternative.
[^10]: At 25 mm/s there are 1500 small squares in one minute (60 s ÷ 0.04 s), so HR = 1500 ÷ small squares between two R waves; equivalently 300 ÷ large squares. Example: 20 small squares → HR 75/min.
[^11]: The epicardium repolarises first because epicardial cells have a shorter action potential (a larger transient outward K⁺ current) and because the endocardium is compressed by high intraventricular pressure during systole, delaying its recovery. The page shows this with opposite red (depolarisation) and green (repolarisation) arrows for the whole heart.
[^12]: Normal mean QRS axis −30° to +90° (some texts to +100° or +110°); Guyton's illustrated average is +59°. Left axis deviation < −30° (left ventricular hypertrophy, left anterior hemiblock); right axis deviation > +90° (right ventricular hypertrophy, left posterior hemiblock, thin tall people).
[^13]: Typical normal values: PA (intra-atrial conduction) ~25–55 ms · AH (AV nodal conduction) ~50–120 ms · HV (His–Purkinje conduction) ~35–55 ms. A long AH localises block to the AV node (usually benign, atropine-responsive); a long HV localises it below the His bundle (infra-Hisian — risk of complete block, often needs pacing).
[^14]: The right internal jugular vein is used: it runs in a straight line to the right atrium through the superior vena cava with no valves. JVP is read with the patient at 45° as the vertical height above the sternal angle; normal ≤ 3–4 cm (≈ ≤ 8–9 cm H₂O above the right atrium).
[^15]: Clinical JVP patterns (standard texts): absent a wave — atrial fibrillation · giant a — tricuspid stenosis, pulmonary hypertension · cannon a — complete heart block (atrium contracts against a closed tricuspid valve) · large v — tricuspid regurgitation · steep y — constrictive pericarditis · absent y — cardiac tamponade.

Abbreviation ECG; EKG from Dutch/German *Elektrokardiogramm*.
[^ety-electrode]: **electrode** — Greek *ēlektron*, "amber → electricity" + *hodos*, "way, path" → the path by which current enters or leaves; coined by Michael Faraday (1834). The (+) electrode is the "exploring" pole of a lead.
[^ety-deflection]: **deflection** — Latin *de-*, "away, down" + *flectere*, "to bend" + *-ion* → a bending away (of the recording line) from the baseline. **Differentiate:** *deflection* (bending away) · *reflection* (bending back) · *inflection* (a change of curvature).
[^ety-jugular]: **jugular** — Latin *iugulum*, "the collarbone, the throat" (literally "little yoke", from *iugum*, yoke) + *-ar*, pertaining to → the vein of the neck. Same root as *conjugate* (yoked together).
[^ety-bipolar]: **bipolar / unipolar** — Latin *bi-*, "two" + Greek *polos*, "pole" → a lead with two active poles (+ and −); *uni-*, "one" → one active (exploring) pole measured against a combined reference.

[^ety-einthoven]: **Einthoven's triangle / law** — **Willem Einthoven** (1860–1927), Dutch physiologist (Leiden), who built the string galvanometer ECG (1901–1903), named the P, Q, R, S, T waves, described the three-lead triangle and won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1924.
[^ety-goldberger]: **Goldberger's leads** — **Emanuel Goldberger** (1913–1994), American cardiologist in New York, who introduced the augmented unipolar limb leads aVR, aVL, aVF in 1942.

[^ety-amplitude]: **amplitude** — Latin *amplus*, "large, ample" + *-itudo*, state → largeness; the height of a deflection, read on the y-axis in mV.
[^ety-depolarisation]: **depolarisation** — Latin *de-*, "removing" + Greek *polos*, "pole" + *-isation*, the process → removing the membrane's polarity (inside becomes less negative). [^ety-segment]: **segment** — Latin *segmentum*, "a piece cut off" (*secare*, to cut) → a piece of baseline cut off between two waves (PR, ST); it contains no wave. Same root as *section* and *sector*.
[^ety-interval]: **interval** — Latin *inter-*, "between" + *vallum*, "rampart, palisade" → the space between two ramparts; on the ECG a stretch that includes at least one wave (PR, QRS, QT, RR). **Differentiate:** *segment* (flat line only) vs *interval* (wave + segment).
