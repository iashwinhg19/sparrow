# 🧩 Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The **cerebellum**[^ety-cerebellum][^suf-ellum] and the **basal ganglia**[^ety-ganglia] are the two side-loops of the motor system: neither sends fibres straight to the spinal cord, both act **through the thalamus and cortex**.
> - ✅ **Cerebellum** — **timing & coordination**[^ety-coordination][^pre-co] **of motor movement** (start–stop signals); lesion → **ataxia**, intention tremor, past pointing.
> - ✅ **Basal ganglia** — **planning** of movement, **cognition** and **inhibition of unwanted movements**; lesion → **involuntary movements** (resting tremor, chorea, athetosis, hemiballismus) or poverty of movement (Parkinsonism).

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — The Conductor and the Gatekeeper

The **cerebellum is the conductor** of an orchestra: it does not choose the music, but it starts each instrument on the beat, stops it on time and smooths the timing — without it, every note comes too early, too late or overshoots (ataxia, past pointing, rebound). The **basal ganglia are the gatekeeper** at the door of the thalamus: the gate is shut by default (GABA brake on the thalamus); the **direct pathway opens** it for the movement you want, the **indirect pathway keeps it shut** for everything else, and **dopamine** oils both hinges.

```
CEREBELLUM     sensory input ──► cerebellum ──► motor output        (timing · coordination · balance)
               start: climbing fibres ⊕ deep nuclei ⊕ brainstem/thalamus ⊕ cortex → MOVEMENT STARTS
               stop : climbing fibres ⊕ Purkinje ⊖ deep nuclei                    → MOVEMENT STOPS
BASAL GANGLIA  cortex ⊕ striatum ─ direct (D1)   ─► GPi ⊖ lost ─► thalamus ⊕ ─► cortex → MOVEMENT
                                 └ indirect (D2) ─► GPe ⊖ lost ─► STN ⊕ GPi ─► thalamus ⊖ → NO MOVEMENT
LESIONS        cerebellum → ataxia, intention tremor (ipsilateral)
               basal ganglia → ↓ DA = Parkinsonism · ↓ caudate GABA = chorea · GP = athetosis · STN = hemiballismus
```

## 🌳 Cerebellum

**Function:** **Timing & coordination of motor movement.**[^1]

### Anatomy

#### Histology[^ety-histology]

| Layer (outside → in) | Cells in it |
|---|---|
| **1. External molecular**[^ety-molecular] **layer** | **Basket cell**, **Stellate**[^ety-stellate] **cell (star shape)** |
| **2. Purkinje**[^ety-purkinje] **cell layer** | **Purkinje cell** — **largest cell in cerebellum**; receives the **climbing fibre input** |
| **3. Inner granular**[^ety-granule] **layer** | **Granule cell**, **Golgi**[^ety-golgi] **cell** |

| Cell type | Cells | Neurotransmitter |
|---|---|---|
| **Inhibitory**[^ety-inhibition][^pre-in] **cells** | Basket, stellate, **Purkinje**, Golgi | **GABA**[^ety-gaba] |
| **Excitatory**[^ety-excitation][^pre-ex] **cell** | **Granule cell** (the only one) | **Glutamate**[^ety-glutamate] |

![[Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia - cerebellar cortex layers and cells.png]]
*Cerebellar cortex — external molecular layer (basket and stellate cells), Purkinje cell layer (Purkinje cell, the largest cell, with its climbing fibre input) and inner granular layer (granule and Golgi cells); inhibitory cells release GABA, the excitatory granule cell releases glutamate (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Plain words:* the Purkinje cell is the **only output** of the cerebellar cortex, and it is **inhibitory** — so the cortex of the cerebellum works by braking its own deep nuclei.[^2]

### Physiology

- **Sensory input → Cerebellum → Motor output.**

#### Start–stop signal circuit

**Start pathway:**

```
Inferior olivary nucleus → Climbing fibres ─⊕─► Deep cerebellar nuclei ─⊕─► Brain stem & thalamus ─⊕─► Cerebral cortex → MOVEMENT STARTS
```
- **Eg:** when the **archer pulls the string** of the bow to release the arrow.

**Stop pathway:**

```
Inferior olivary nucleus → Climbing fibres ─⊕─► Purkinje cells ─⊖─► Deep cerebellar nuclei → MOVEMENT STOPS
```
- **Eg:** when the **archer releases his fingers** from the string (**D/t inhibition of DCN**).

**Pre-start pathway:**

```
                       ┌─ Dorsal SCT  (Inferior cerebellar peduncle) ─┐
Spinal cord → Mossy fibres                                             ├─► Granule cells → Parallel fibres ─⊕─┬─ Basket cells  ┐
                       └─ Ventral SCT (Superior cerebellar peduncle) ─┘          ▲                            ├─ Stellate cells ┴─⊖─► Purkinje cells
                                                                                  │                            └─ Golgi cells             │
                                                                                  └──────── ⊖ Feedback inhibition ◄──┘                     ▼
                                                                                                               Deep cerebellar nuclei inhibition LOST
                                                                                                                              ▼
                                                                                                  New movement can start in the start pathway
```
- **Eg:** for the **archer to pull the string again**, the **inhibition on the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN) has to be removed**.[^4]
- 🧠 *Read it as:* climbing fibres from the **inferior olivary**[^ety-olivary] **nucleus** both start (via the deep nuclei) and stop (via Purkinje cells) a movement; **mossy fibres** from the cord, entering through the **peduncles**[^ety-peduncle], reach Purkinje cells through granule cells and their parallel fibres, and through basket/stellate cells **take the Purkinje brake off** so the next movement can begin. Golgi cells give **feedback inhibition** to the granule cells.[^5]

#### Functions

| Division | Nucleus | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Spinocerebellum** | • **Emboliform** • **Fastigial** • **Globose** | **Coordination of motor movements** |
| **Vestibulocerebellum** | **Vestibular** | • **Maintaining equilibrium**[^ety-equilibrium] **/ balance** • **Regulating eye movements** |
| **Neocerebellum (Recently evolved)** | **Dentate** | **Planning motor movements** |

> [!note] Note
> The **vestibular apparatus** helps in maintaining balance **along with the cerebellum**.[^6]

### Signs of cerebellar lesions

| No. | Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Truncal ataxia**[^ety-ataxia][^pre-a] | Unsteady trunk when sitting or standing |
| 2 | **Wide based stance** | Feet kept apart for balance |
| 3 | **Titubation**[^ety-titubation] | **Tremors of head** |
| 4 | **Ataxic / drunken gait** | Staggering, reeling walk |
| 5 | **Nystagmus**[^ety-nystagmus] | Jerky to-and-fro eye movements |
| 6 | **Decomposition**[^ety-decomposition][^pre-de] **of movements** | **Breakdown into individual parts** |
| 7 | **Past pointing** | **Finger–nose test** — the finger overshoots the target |
| 8 | **Kinetic / intentional tremors**[^ety-tremor] | **Seen with activity (absent at rest)** |
| 9 | **Dysarthria**[^ety-dysarthria][^pre-dys] | **Scanning speech** |
| 10 | **Pendular knee jerk** | Knee swings to and fro after the tap |
| 11 | **Dysdiadochokinesia**[^ety-dysdiadochokinesia][^suf-kinesia] | **Inability to perform alternating supination & pronation movements** |
| 12 | **Rebound**[^pre-re] **phenomenon** | **Unable to stop the movement** |

- 🧠 *One rule explains them all:* the cerebellum controls **timing** — so movements start late, overshoot (past pointing, rebound), break into pieces (decomposition), cannot alternate quickly (dysdiadochokinesia) and wobble more as they approach a target (intention tremor). Signs are on the **same side** as the lesion.[^7]

### Physiology

**Functions:**
1. **Planning of motor movements.**
2. **Cognition**[^ety-cognition] **(caudate nucleus).**
3. **Inhibition of voluntary & involuntary**[^ety-voluntary] **movements (even at rest).**

**Significance:** **Basal ganglia lesions → Involuntary movements.**

![[Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia - basal ganglia in the brain.png]]
*The basal ganglia (red) deep inside the cerebral hemisphere, above the brainstem and cerebellum (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Neural pathway

```
                                   CORTEX
                       GLU ⊕ │              │ GLU ⊕
 Substantia nigra ── DA ⊖ ──► STRIATUM (Caudate + Putamen) ◄── ⊕ DA ── Substantia nigra
      (D2 receptor)          │  INDIRECT side  │  DIRECT side   (D1 receptor)
                    GABA ⊖   ▼                 │ GABA ⊖
                        GP externa             │
                 GABA ⊖ ✗ (inhibition lost)    │
                             ▼                 ▼
                    Subthalamic nuclei ─GLU ⊕─► GP INTERNA
                                                │
                              GABA ⊖ (indirect) │ GABA ⊖ ✗ (inhibition lost — direct)
                                                ▼
                               Ventral anterior nucleus: THALAMUS
                                                │ GLU ⊕
                                                ▼
                                             CORTEX
                              NO MOVEMENT ◄─────┴─────► MOVEMENT
```

| | **Indirect pathway** | **Direct pathway** |
|---|---|---|
| Net effect | **Inhibits motor movements** | **Facilitates motor movement** |
| Dopamine receptor on striatum | **D₂** (DA ⊖) | **D₁** (DA ⊕) |
| Route | Striatum ⊖ GP externa → its GABA on the subthalamic nuclei is lost → STN ⊕ (GLU) GP interna → GP interna ⊖ thalamus | Striatum ⊖ GP interna → its GABA on the thalamus is lost → thalamus ⊕ cortex |
| Result at the cortex | **No movement** | **Movement** |

- **Dopamine facilitates direct & indirect pathway** (page).[^8]
- 🧠 *Plain words:* the GP interna is a brake permanently pressed on the thalamus. The **direct pathway lifts the brake** (inhibits the inhibitor = **disinhibition**) → movement; the **indirect pathway presses it harder** (via the subthalamic nucleus) → no movement.

![[Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia - direct and indirect pathways.png]]
*Neural pathway of the basal ganglia — indirect pathway (left: D₂, GP externa, subthalamic nuclei, inhibits movement) and direct pathway (right: D₁, GP interna inhibition lost, facilitates movement), both returning to the cortex through the ventral anterior nucleus of the thalamus (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Disorders

#### Parkinsonism[^ety-parkinson][^suf-ism]

- **Pathology:** **↓ dopamine in substantia nigra (pars compacta).**
- **Clinical features:**
  1. **Bradykinesia**[^ety-bradykinesia][^pre-brady] — **paucity (↓) of movements.**
  2. **Tremors** — **pill rolling tremors (resting tremors).**[^9]
- **Treatment:**
  1. **Medical:** **L-DOPA**[^ety-ldopa] **→ crosses BBB.**
     - Over time becomes **ineffective** (D/t **progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons**).[^10]
  2. **Surgical therapies:**

```
                        SURGICAL THERAPIES
             ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
  a. STEREOTACTIC ABLATION                b. DEEP BRAIN STIMULATION
     • Thalamus:                             • Basal ganglia:
       – Ventral anterior nucleus              – Subthalamic nucleus
       – Ventral lateral nucleus                      ↓
             ↓                                Improves movements
     Alleviates symptoms
```

- **a. Stereotactic**[^ety-stereotactic] **ablation**[^ety-ablation][^pre-ab] — destroying the thalamic **ventral anterior** and **ventral lateral** nuclei → **alleviates symptoms**.
- **b. Deep brain stimulation**[^ety-stimulation] — electrode in the **subthalamic nucleus** → **improves movements**.[^11]

> [!warning] Note
> **Dopamine can't cross BBB** — so the precursor **L-DOPA** is given and converted to dopamine inside the brain.[^12]

#### Huntington's disease[^ety-huntington]

**Pathogenesis:**

```

       │                       • Anti apoptotic
       │                       • Produces brain derived neurotrophic factor
       ↓
   Mutation
       ↓

       ↓
↑ Polyglutamine levels
       ↓
```

- The normal **Huntington's gene** product is **neuroprotective**[^pre-neuro], **anti-apoptotic**[^ety-apoptosis][^pre-anti][^suf-osis] and **produces brain derived neurotrophic**[^ety-trophic] **factor**; its **mutation**[^ety-mutation] — an expanded **trinucleotide**[^pre-tri] **repeat (CAG)** — gives ↑ **polyglutamine**[^pre-poly] → **excitotoxicity**[^ety-excitotoxicity] → loss of the GABA cells of the caudate.[^13]

**Clinical features:**
- **Chorea**[^ety-chorea] — **dance-like involuntary movements.**
- **Memory abnormality** — **cognition is affected.**

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Both loops **modulate** movement rather than produce it, and both do it largely by **inhibition**. The cerebellum's only cortical output, the Purkinje cell, is GABAergic: climbing-fibre input excites the deep nuclei to **start** a movement and, through Purkinje cells, inhibits them to **stop** it on time — so a cerebellar lesion ruins **timing** (overshoot, past pointing, rebound, intention tremor) on the **same side**.
>
> The basal ganglia hold the thalamus under a tonic GABA brake from GP interna/SN reticulata. The **direct pathway (D₁) releases** the brake for the wanted movement, the **indirect pathway (D₂, via the subthalamic nucleus) tightens** it for unwanted ones, and dopamine from the pars compacta tilts the balance toward movement. Too little dopamine → too much brake → **Parkinsonism** (hypokinetic); loss of the indirect pathway's caudate GABA cells or of the subthalamic nucleus → too little brake → **chorea / hemiballismus** (hyperkinetic).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Cerebellum compares intended with actual movement and times agonist/antagonist bursts; basal ganglia select the wanted motor programme (direct) and suppress competitors (indirect) under dopamine tone from the pars compacta.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Early Parkinson's: dopaminergic neurons die silently until roughly 60–80 % are lost, because surviving neurons and receptor up-regulation compensate; early signs are subtle — reduced arm swing, mild unilateral resting tremor. Early Huntington's: CAG carriers have only clumsiness, irritability and subtle cognitive change before chorea appears. Small cerebellar lesions are compensated by the other hemisphere and cortex.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Parkinsonism: bradykinesia, pill-rolling resting tremor, rigidity, postural instability; L-DOPA works at first but loses effect as dopaminergic neurons keep dying — then stereotactic thalamic ablation or subthalamic deep brain stimulation. Huntington's: chorea, dementia, caudate atrophy. Cerebellar disease: truncal ataxia, drunken gait, scanning speech, nystagmus, dysdiadochokinesia.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → neuronal loss (SNc dopamine / caudate GABA / cerebellar cortex) → compensation (receptor up-regulation, other circuits) → threshold crossed → hypokinetic or hyperkinetic syndrome / ataxia → drug failure → surgery (ablation, DBS)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Whether a movement disorder is hypokinetic or hyperkinetic tells you which pathway failed and which drugs help (dopamine replacement for Parkinsonism; dopamine blockers or depleters for chorea). Cerebellar signs localise to the same side and to vermis (truncal) vs hemisphere (limb) lesions.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Cerebellar lesion:** "Intention tremor", "past pointing on finger–nose test", "scanning speech", "dysdiadochokinesia", "pendular knee jerk", "rebound phenomenon", "wide-based drunken gait" → ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere or vermis lesion.
>
> **Vermis / flocculonodular lesion:** "Truncal ataxia", "titubation", "cannot sit or stand unsupported", "nystagmus" → midline cerebellum (e.g. medulloblastoma in children, alcohol in adults).
>
> **Parkinsonism:** "Pill-rolling resting tremor that disappears on movement", "bradykinesia", "mask-like face", "cogwheel rigidity" → ↓ dopamine in substantia nigra pars compacta.
>
> **Huntington's disease:** "Dance-like movements", "autosomal dominant", "CAG repeat", "anticipation", "dementia", "caudate atrophy" → loss of striatal GABA neurons.
>
> **Athetosis:** "Slow writhing, snake-charmer hands" → globus pallidus (classically cerebral palsy / kernicterus).
>
> **Hemiballismus:** "Sudden violent flinging of one arm" → contralateral subthalamic nucleus (lacunar stroke).
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Loss of the caudate bulge with box-shaped lateral ventricles = Huntington's; pale substantia nigra on pathology with Lewy bodies = Parkinson's; cerebellar atrophy on MRI in chronic alcoholism (vermis first).
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Parkinson's motor signs appear after ~60–80 % SNc dopaminergic loss; Huntington's CAG ≥ 40 repeats = full penetrance, longer repeats = earlier onset.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Mixing up the two tremors**
>
> **What students think:** All tremor means Parkinson's.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Basal ganglia (Parkinsonian) tremor is a **resting** tremor that lessens with action; cerebellar tremor is an **intention (kinetic)** tremor, absent at rest and worst near the target, because the cerebellum fails to time the braking of the movement.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Rest = basal ganglia; reaching = cerebellum."
>
> **Trap 2: Thinking the Purkinje cell is excitatory because it is the output**
>
> **What students think:** The main output neuron must excite its target.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Purkinje cells release GABA and inhibit the deep cerebellar nuclei; the only excitatory neuron of the cerebellar cortex is the granule cell (glutamate). Movement is stopped by Purkinje inhibition and restarted by removing it (basket/stellate inhibition of Purkinje cells).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "In the cerebellar cortex everyone inhibits except the granule cell."
>
> **Trap 3: Getting the cerebellar deep nuclei order wrong**
>
> **What students think:** The page's D, E, F, G is the anatomical order.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page lists the four letters, but its own figure numbers them 1 dentate, 2 emboliform, 4 globose, 3 fastigial — from lateral to medial the sequence is **dentate → emboliform → globose → fastigial**. The fastigial nucleus is the most medial (vermis), the dentate the most lateral (hemisphere).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Don't Eat Greasy Food" — lateral to medial.
>
> **Trap 4: Believing dopamine excites both pathways**
>
> **What students think:** "Dopamine facilitates the direct and indirect pathway" means it excites both.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Dopamine **excites** direct-pathway neurons through **D₁** and **inhibits** indirect-pathway neurons through **D₂**. Both actions favour movement, which is why the page says it "facilitates" both — but a question asking the effect on the indirect pathway wants "inhibits".
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "D₁ = Do (go); D₂ = Don't go is silenced."
>
> **Trap 5: Giving dopamine itself for Parkinson's disease**
>
> **What students think:** Replace the missing dopamine directly.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Dopamine cannot cross the blood–brain barrier; its precursor L-DOPA is carried across by the large neutral amino acid transporter and decarboxylated in the brain (given with carbidopa to block peripheral conversion).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Give the precursor, not the product."

> [!tip]+ **🔗 Related Concepts & Cross-Topic Connections**

> GABA, glutamate and dopamine (D₁/D₂ receptors) are the transmitters of every arrow in the basal ganglia circuit.
>
> **Related Topic 4: [[CSF, CBF and BBB]]**
> Dopamine cannot cross the blood–brain barrier but L-DOPA can — the basis of Parkinson's treatment.
>
> **Related Topic 5: [[Homeostasis]]**
> The cerebellum is the textbook feedforward (anticipatory) motor controller — braking on seeing a cow on the highway.
>
> **Related Topic 6: [[Hearing, Smell and Taste]]**
> The vestibulocerebellum works with the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear to keep balance and steady the eyes.
>
> **Clinical Integration:**
> Ataxia work-up (alcohol, stroke, tumour, multiple sclerosis), Parkinsonism (drugs, idiopathic), Huntington's (genetic counselling) and post-stroke hemiballismus all hinge on knowing which loop and which nucleus is involved.
>
> **Exam Integration:**

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Cerebellum — **timing & coordination of motor movement**.
> 2. Layers: **molecular** (basket, stellate) → **Purkinje** (largest cell, climbing fibre input) → **granular** (granule, Golgi).
> 3. Inhibitory (GABA): **basket, stellate, Purkinje, Golgi**; excitatory (glutamate): **granule cell** only.
> 4. Deep nuclei lateral → medial: **dentate (largest)**, emboliform, globose (emboliform + globose = **interpositus**), **fastigial**.
> 5. **Vermis + intermediate zone = spinocerebellum** (coordination); **lateral hemisphere = neocerebellum** (dentate — planning); **flocculonodular = vestibulocerebellum** (balance, eye movements).
> 6. Start: **olive → climbing fibres ⊕ DCN** → thalamus → cortex; stop: **climbing fibres ⊕ Purkinje ⊖ DCN**; pre-start: mossy → granule → parallel fibres → basket/stellate ⊖ Purkinje → DCN freed.
> 7. Cerebellar signs: truncal ataxia, wide stance, **titubation**, drunken gait, nystagmus, decomposition, **past pointing**, **intention tremor**, **scanning speech**, **pendular knee jerk**, **dysdiadochokinesia**, **rebound**.
> 8. Basal ganglia: **striatum (caudate + putamen) = input**; **GPi and SN pars reticulata = output**; **subthalamic = excitatory (glutamate)**; SN pars compacta = dopamine.
> 9. Functions: **planning**, **cognition (caudate)**, **inhibition of voluntary & involuntary movements**; lesion → **involuntary movements**.
> 10. **Direct (D₁) → movement; indirect (D₂, GPe, STN) → no movement**; thalamic relay = **ventral anterior nucleus**.
> 11. **Parkinsonism = ↓ DA in SN pars compacta** → bradykinesia, **pill-rolling resting tremor**; **L-DOPA crosses BBB, dopamine can't**; surgery: **stereotactic ablation of thalamus (VA, VL)**, **DBS of subthalamic nucleus**.
> 12. **Huntington's** — **CAG** trinucleotide repeat → ↑ polyglutamine → excitotoxicity → loss of **GABA cells of caudate** → **chorea**, memory loss.
> 13. **Chorea = caudate; athetosis = globus pallidus; hemiballismus = subthalamic nucleus.**

[^1]: The cerebellum makes up about 10 % of brain volume but contains more than half of all its neurons. It controls the **same side** of the body (its outputs cross twice), which is why cerebellar signs are ipsilateral to the lesion.
[^2]: Each Purkinje cell receives about 200 000 parallel-fibre synapses but only **one** climbing fibre, which wraps around its dendrites and fires a powerful "complex spike". Purkinje axons are the only fibres leaving the cerebellar cortex; they inhibit the deep nuclei (and, from the flocculonodular lobe, the vestibular nuclei).
[^3]: The page writes the mnemonic as "D, E, F, G" and numbers the nuclei 1 dentate, 2 emboliform, 3 fastigial, 4 globose on its figure. Anatomically the order from lateral to medial is dentate, emboliform, globose, fastigial — the common mnemonic is "Don't Eat Greasy Food".
[^4]: The page's start–stop scheme is a simplified version of Guyton's "turn-on / turn-off" signals: input fibres excite the deep nuclei directly (turn-on), and the same input reaching the Purkinje cells a few milliseconds later inhibits the deep nuclei (turn-off), so each movement is given a precise start and end.
[^5]: Climbing fibres come **only** from the inferior olivary nucleus; mossy fibres come from everywhere else (spinocerebellar tracts, pontine nuclei, vestibular nuclei, reticular formation) and end on granule cells in glomeruli. The dorsal spinocerebellar tract enters through the inferior cerebellar peduncle and the ventral spinocerebellar tract through the superior peduncle.
[^6]: Textbooks also call the vestibulocerebellum the **archicerebellum** (oldest), the spinocerebellum the **paleocerebellum**, and the neocerebellum the **cerebrocerebellum**. The flocculonodular lobe projects mainly to the vestibular nuclei, which is why the page lists "vestibular" as its nucleus.
[^7]: The common bedside mnemonic is **DANISH** — Dysdiadochokinesia, Ataxia, Nystagmus, Intention tremor, Scanning (slurred) speech, Hypotonia. Vermis lesions give truncal ataxia and titubation; hemisphere lesions give limb ataxia. Romberg's test is classically negative in cerebellar ataxia (positive in sensory ataxia). The rebound phenomenon is also called Holmes' rebound.
[^8]: Strictly, dopamine **excites** the direct pathway (D₁ receptors, Gs) and **inhibits** the indirect pathway (D₂ receptors, Gi); both effects promote movement, which is the sense of the page's "facilitates both". Loss of dopamine therefore weakens the "go" signal and strengthens the "stop" signal.
[^9]: The classic tetrad is **TRAP** — resting **T**remor (4–6 Hz, pill-rolling), **R**igidity (lead-pipe, or cogwheel when tremor is superimposed), **A**kinesia/bradykinesia and **P**ostural instability; signs usually appear after about 60–80 % of the nigral dopaminergic neurons are lost.
[^10]: L-DOPA is given with **carbidopa**, a peripheral DOPA-decarboxylase inhibitor that does not cross the BBB, so more L-DOPA reaches the brain and peripheral side effects (nausea, hypotension) fall. With disease progression, fewer neurons can store dopamine, giving "wearing-off" and "on–off" fluctuations and dyskinesias.
[^11]: Stereotactic thalamotomy (ventrolateral thalamus) relieves mainly tremor; pallidotomy (GPi) relieves rigidity and dyskinesia. Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus (or GPi) is reversible and adjustable and is now preferred over ablation for advanced Parkinson's disease.
[^12]: L-DOPA, an amino acid, is carried across the BBB by the large neutral amino acid transporter (LAT1); dopamine, a charged catecholamine, has no such carrier. Dietary protein competes with L-DOPA for the same transporter.
[^13]: Huntington's disease is autosomal dominant; the *HTT* gene on chromosome 4p16.3 normally carries fewer than 27 CAG repeats; 36–39 repeats give reduced penetrance and ≥ 40 full penetrance. Repeats tend to expand down the generations, especially through the father (anticipation). Imaging shows caudate atrophy with box-shaped frontal horns of the lateral ventricles.
[^14]: Athetosis is classically linked to the globus pallidus (also striatal damage in kernicterus and cerebral palsy). Hemiballismus is almost always due to a lesion — usually a small stroke — of the **contralateral** subthalamic nucleus.

[^ety-bradykinesia]: **bradykinesia** — Greek *bradys*, "slow" + *kinēsis*, "movement" + *-ia* → slowness of movement. **Differentiate:** *bradykinesia* (slow) · *hypokinesia* (reduced amplitude/amount — the page's "paucity") · *akinesia* (absence, difficulty starting).
[^pre-brady]: **brady-** — slow: bradykinesia, bradycardia, bradypnoea. Don't confuse with **tachy-** fast · **brachy-** short (brachydactyly) · **baro-** pressure.
[^ety-ldopa]: **L-DOPA (levodopa)** — **L**- = laevo-, "left" (the left-handed optical isomer, the biologically active form) + **DOPA** = **d**ihydr**o**xy**p**henyl**a**lanine → the amino-acid precursor of dopamine.
[^ety-stereotactic]: **stereotactic** — Greek *stereos*, "solid, three-dimensional" + *taxis*, "arrangement" + *-ic* → arranged in three dimensions: targeting a deep brain point with a 3-D coordinate frame. Same *taxis* root as *ataxia*.
[^ety-ablation]: **ablation** — Latin *ab-*, "away" + *latus*, "carried" (*auferre*, to carry away) + *-ion* → carrying away: surgical destruction or removal of tissue. **Differentiate:** *ablation* (destroy) · *stimulation* (DBS — switch on/modulate, reversible) · *resection* (cut out).
[^pre-ab]: **ab-** — away from: ablation, abduction, abnormal. Don't confuse with **ad-** towards (adduction) · **a- / an-** without.
[^ety-stimulation]: **stimulation** — Latin *stimulus*, "a goad" + *-ation* → goading into activity; deep brain stimulation delivers high-frequency pulses that in practice silence the overactive target.
[^ety-huntington]: **Huntington's disease** — George Huntington (1850–1916), American physician from Long Island, New York, who described the hereditary chorea in 1872, at age 22.
[^pre-neuro]: **neuro- / neur-** — nerve: neuroprotective, neurotrophic, neurotransmitter, neuron. Don't confuse with **nephro-** kidney · **neo-** new.
[^ety-apoptosis]: **apoptosis** — Greek *apo-*, "away, off" + *ptōsis*, "falling" → a falling off, like leaves from a tree: programmed, orderly cell death. **Differentiate:** *apoptosis* (programmed, no inflammation) · *necrosis* (accidental, swelling, inflammation) · *excitotoxicity* (death by over-excitation).
[^pre-anti]: **anti-** — against: anti-apoptotic, antibody, anticoagulant. Don't confuse with **ante-** before (antenatal) · **anti-** in *anticipate* (from *ante-*).
[^suf-osis]: **-osis / -sis** — a process or condition: apoptosis, ptosis, sclerosis. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation · **-oma** tumour · **-ism** a state.
[^ety-trophic]: **neurotrophic** — Greek *neuron*, "nerve" + *trophē*, "nourishment" + *-ic* → nourishing nerves; BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) keeps striatal neurons alive. **Differentiate:** *-trophic* (nourishing, growth) · *-tropic* (turning toward, acting on — gonadotropic).
[^ety-mutation]: **mutation** — Latin *mutare*, "to change" + *-ation* → a change in the DNA sequence. Huntington's is an *expansion* mutation (a repeat that grows).
[^pre-tri]: **tri-** — three: trinucleotide (CAG), triad, trigeminal. Don't confuse with **tetra-** four · **ter-** three times (Latin).
[^pre-poly]: **poly-** — many: polyglutamine (a long run of glutamines encoded by CAG repeats), polysynaptic, polyuria. Don't confuse with **multi-** (Latin synonym) · **mono-** one.
[^ety-excitotoxicity]: **excitotoxicity** — Latin *excitare*, "to rouse" + Greek *toxikon*, "poison (for arrows)" + *-ity* → poisoning by over-excitation: excess glutamate opens NMDA channels, Ca²⁺ floods in and kills the neuron.
[^ety-chorea]: **chorea** — Greek *choreia*, "dance" → irregular, flowing, dance-like jerks; same root as *choreography*. *Sydenham's chorea* follows rheumatic fever; *Huntington's chorea* is hereditary.
[^ety-athetosis]: **athetosis** — Greek *athetos*, "not fixed, without position" (*a-* + *tithenai*, to place) + *-osis* → limbs that cannot hold a fixed position, writhing slowly; named by W. A. Hammond (1871).
[^ety-hemiballismus]: **hemiballismus** — Greek *hēmi-*, "half" + *ballismos*, "jumping about, throwing" (*ballein*, to throw) → violent flinging movements of one side (one limb). Same root as *ballistic*.
