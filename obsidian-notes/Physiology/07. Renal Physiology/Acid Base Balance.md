# 🧪 Acid Base Balance

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Acid–base[^ety-acid][^ety-base] balance is the body's defence of a **blood pH of 7.35–7.45**[^ety-ph] — a range so narrow that **> 7.8 or < 6.9 means death**. Everything in this chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **Who defends pH, and how fast?** — buffers (first line, seconds), lungs (second line, minutes, corrects up to 75 %), kidneys (third line, hours–days, **correct the cause**)
> - ✅ **How does the kidney make "new" HCO₃⁻?** — by excreting H⁺ on **phosphate** (titratable acid) and **ammonia** (ammonium)
> - ✅ **Which disorder is it?** — read pH, pCO₂ and HCO₃⁻, then use the **anion gap** to split metabolic acidosis into **HAGMA** and **NAGMA**

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Sponges, an Exhaust Fan and the Plumber

Think of acid as water flooding a house. **Buffers** are sponges already lying on the floor — they soak up the water instantly but the water is still in the house. The **lungs** are an exhaust fan that blows out CO₂ (the volatile acid) within minutes — fast, but they can only get you most of the way. The **kidneys** are the plumber: slow to arrive, but they drain the fixed acid and **build new sponges (new HCO₃⁻)**, so the problem is actually fixed.

```
CO2 + H2O  ⇌(carbonic anhydrase)⇌  H2CO3  ⇌  H+ (acid)  +  HCO3- (base)
   ▲ LUNGS control this end                         KIDNEYS control this end ▲

1st LINE  BUFFERS  → seconds   → hold pH, do NOT correct the cause  (HCO3- = most important)
2nd LINE  LUNGS    → minutes   → alter pCO2 (alveolar ventilation ∝ 1/pCO2) → corrects up to 75 %
3rd LINE  KIDNEYS  → hours–days→ correct the cause: excrete H+, reabsorb + GENERATE new HCO3-
```

## ⚗️ The Basic Equation

**CO₂ + H₂O** —(**Carbonic Anhydrase**[^ety-carbonic-anhydrase][^pre-an][^suf-ase])→ **H₂CO₃** (weak acid) → **H⁺** (acid) + **HCO₃⁻** (base)

🧠 Every line of this chapter is this one equation pushed to the left or right: add CO₂ and you make more H⁺ (respiratory acidosis); lose HCO₃⁻ and the H⁺ it was holding is set free (metabolic acidosis). Carbonic acid is called *weak* because it only partly dissociates — which is exactly what lets the pair act as a buffer.[^1]

## ⚖️ Maintenance of Acid Base Balance

### pH

- **Normal: 7.35 – 7.45** (**> 7.8 or < 6.9 → Death**).

```
                         ┌─► Demineralization of proteinaceous enzymes (↓ activity)
Derangement of pH ───────┼─► Alkalosis : ↑ CNS excitability
                         └─► Acidosis  : ↓ CNS excitability
```

- **Derangement**[^ety-derangement][^pre-de] of pH → **demineralization**[^ety-demineralization][^suf-ation] of proteinaceous enzymes[^ety-enzyme] (↓ activity).[^2]
- **Alkalosis**[^ety-alkalosis][^suf-osis] → **↑ CNS excitability**[^ety-excitability] (tingling, tetany, seizures).
- **Acidosis**[^ety-acidosis] → **↓ CNS excitability** (drowsiness → coma).

### 1️⃣ First Line Defense — Buffers[^ety-buffer]

> [!info] Maintains pH **without correcting** the cause of imbalance
> A buffer only "hides" H⁺ by binding it; the acid load is still in the body until the lungs or kidneys remove it.

#### Extracellular[^ety-extracellular][^pre-extra] buffer — bicarbonate

```
CO2 + H2O ──(CA)──► H2CO3 ──► H+ + HCO3-
                                    │
                                    ▼
             most important buffer: tightly regulates
             CO2 levels in the LUNG and H+ in the KIDNEY
```

- **HCO₃⁻ = most important buffer** — not because it is chemically the strongest, but because both ends of the equation are **tightly regulated**: **CO₂ by the lung** and **H⁺ (and HCO₃⁻) by the kidney**.[^3]

#### Intracellular[^ety-intracellular][^pre-intra] buffer

a) **Proteins with histidine**[^ety-histidine][^suf-ine] act as buffers.
   - **Eg: Hb in RBC's** (**most plentiful intracellular buffer**).[^ety-haemoglobin]
b) **Phosphate**[^ety-phosphate][^suf-ate]: **urinary buffer**.

> [!note] Note
> **Ammonia**[^ety-ammonia] → **urinary buffer**.

### 2️⃣ Second Line Defense — Lungs

> [!info] Maintains pH by **altering pCO₂ in lungs** (can correct **up to 75 %**)
> **Alveolar ventilation ∝ 1 / pCO₂** — the more you breathe out, the less CO₂ stays in the blood.[^4]

```
                 ALVEOLAR VENTILATION ∝ 1 / pCO2
          ┌───────────────────┴───────────────────┐
   Hyperventilation                        Hypoventilation
          ↓                                        ↓
   ↑ CO2 washout                               ↑ CO2 (retained)
          ↓                                        ↓
       ↓ pCO2                                   ↑ pCO2
```

- **Alveolar**[^ety-alveolar] **ventilation**[^ety-ventilation]: **hyperventilation**[^pre-hyper] → ↑ CO₂ washout → ↓ pCO₂; **hypoventilation**[^pre-hypo] → ↑ CO₂ → ↑ pCO₂.

### 3️⃣ Third Line Defense — Kidneys

- **Kidneys**[^ety-renal] → **correct the cause** → normalize pH by:
  - **Generation of HCO₃⁻ : unique feature for kidney**.[^ety-generation]
  - **H⁺ excretion**[^ety-excretion][^pre-ex].
  - **HCO₃⁻ excretion / reabsorption**[^ety-reabsorption][^pre-re].

#### PCT[^ety-proximal][^ety-tubule][^suf-ule]

**1. Excretion of H⁺ and reabsorption of HCO₃⁻:**

![[Acid Base Balance - PCT HCO3 reabsorption.png]]
*PCT cell between the peritubular capillary and the lumen: H⁺ leaves through the Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger, meets filtered HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → (luminal CA) CO₂ + H₂O → CO₂ recycles into the cell → (CA II) H⁺ + HCO₃⁻; the HCO₃⁻ goes to the blood — "generated HCO₃⁻ is reabsorbed", but filtered = generated, so no net gain (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Where | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Lumen** | **Filtered HCO₃⁻: acts as a buffer for H⁺** → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ →(CA) **CO₂** + H₂O |
| **Brush border** | **Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger**[^ety-exchanger]: Na⁺ in, **H⁺ out** into the lumen |
| **PCT cell** | CO₂ diffuses back in (**recycling**) → CO₂ + H₂O →(**CA II**) H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ |
| **Peritubular**[^ety-peritubular][^pre-peri] **capillary**[^ety-capillary] | **Generated HCO₃⁻ is reabsorbed** |
| **Net result** | **No net HCO₃⁻ gain**, as **HCO₃⁻ filtered = generated** |

🧠 The filtered HCO₃⁻ itself never crosses the cell — it is destroyed in the lumen and an identical HCO₃⁻ is rebuilt inside the cell. That is why this step only *reclaims* bicarbonate; it does not add any.

![Conservation of bicarbonate in the kidney — tubular cell, Na⁺/H⁺ exchange, carbonic anhydrase (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9d/2715_Conservation_of_Bicarbonate_in_Kidney-01.jpg/800px-2715_Conservation_of_Bicarbonate_in_Kidney-01.jpg)
*Conservation of bicarbonate in the kidney: tubular cells are not permeable to HCO₃⁻, so it is "reabsorbed" by being destroyed in the lumen and regenerated inside the cell — the same loop as the page's first PCT diagram (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, Fig. 26.4.3, CC BY via Wikimedia Commons).*

**2. Generation of new HCO₃⁻ by using substitute buffers (unique to kidney).**

**a. With HPO₄²⁻ as buffer:**

![[Acid Base Balance - PCT new HCO3 with phosphate buffer.png]]
*Here the H⁺ pumped into the lumen is caught by filtered HPO₄²⁻ instead of HCO₃⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻ (titratable acid, excreted in urine); the HCO₃⁻ made in the cell is brand-new → "generated HCO₃⁻ gets reabsorbed (net gain)" (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- PCT cell: CO₂ + H₂O →(CA) H₂CO₃ → **H⁺ + HCO₃⁻**.
- H⁺ → lumen via Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger → **H⁺ + HPO₄²⁻ → H₂PO₄⁻** — HPO₄²⁻ **acts as a buffer for H⁺**.
- **H₂PO₄⁻ = titratable**[^ety-titratable] **acid excreted in urine.**
- **Generated HCO₃⁻ gets reabsorbed (net gain).**

**b. With NH₃ as buffer:**

![[Acid Base Balance - PCT new HCO3 with ammonia buffer.png]]
*Glutamine inside the PCT cell → 2 HCO₃⁻ + NH₃; NH₃ crosses into the lumen by non-ionic diffusion, traps the secreted H⁺ as NH₄⁺ (ammonium, excreted in urine); the 2 HCO₃⁻ are new → net gain (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Glutamine**[^ety-glutamine] → **2 HCO₃⁻ + NH₃** (in the PCT cell).
- NH₃ → lumen by **non-ionic diffusion**[^ety-diffusion][^pre-non].
- **H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺** — NH₃ **acts as a buffer for H⁺**; **ammonium is excreted in urine**.
- **New HCO₃⁻ generated (net gain).**[^5]

> [!warning] Note — Limiting pH
> - **pH at which PCT stops excretion of H⁺ = 4.5** (**never seen in PCT** due to strict buffering).
> - **Seen in collecting duct, where urine is acidified.**
>
> 🧠 H⁺ pumps cannot push against an infinitely steep gradient; once urine reaches pH 4.5, secretion stops. Buffers in the PCT lumen mop up H⁺ so fast that the PCT never gets close — only the collecting duct, with few buffers left, drives urine down towards 4.5.

#### Collecting duct

- **Regulated by protein: Hensin**[^ety-hensin].[^6]

**1. H⁺ excretion: in acidosis by intercalated**[^ety-intercalated][^pre-inter] **cell A (Acid excretion)**

![[Acid Base Balance - type A intercalated cell.png]]
*Type A intercalated cell: luminal H⁺–K⁺ ATPase excretes H⁺ (under control of aldosterone); the basolateral Cl⁻–HCO₃⁻ exchanger returns HCO₃⁻ to the peritubular capillary — HCO₃⁻ reabsorption and replenishment (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Membrane | Transporter | Moves |
|---|---|---|
| Luminal | **H⁺ K⁺ ATPase**[^ety-atpase] (**under control of aldosterone**[^ety-aldosterone]) | **H⁺ out → excretion of H⁺**; K⁺ in |
| Basolateral | **Cl⁻ HCO₃⁻ exchanger** | HCO₃⁻ to blood (**HCO₃⁻ reabsorption & replenishment**); Cl⁻ in |

![Alpha (type A) intercalated cell — luminal proton pumps, basolateral chloride–bicarbonate exchanger (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/38/Alpha_Intercalated_Cell_Cartoon.svg/800px-Alpha_Intercalated_Cell_Cartoon.svg.png)
*Molecular cartoon of an α (type A) intercalated cell: carbonic anhydrase makes H⁺ and HCO₃⁻; H⁺ is pumped into the urine, HCO₃⁻ leaves into the blood in exchange for Cl⁻ (Rswarbrick, Wikimedia Commons).*

**2. HCO₃⁻ excretion: in alkalosis by intercalated cell B (Base excretion)**

![[Acid Base Balance - type B intercalated cell.png]]
*Type B intercalated cell — the mirror image: luminal HCO₃⁻–Cl⁻ exchanger excretes HCO₃⁻; basolateral H⁺–K⁺ ATPase returns H⁺ to the blood — H⁺ reabsorption (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Membrane | Transporter | Moves |
|---|---|---|
| Luminal | **HCO₃⁻ Cl⁻ exchanger** | **HCO₃⁻ out → HCO₃⁻ excretion**; Cl⁻ in |
| Basolateral | **H⁺ K⁺ ATPase** | **H⁺ to blood → H⁺ reabsorption**; K⁺ in |

## 🩺 Acid Base Disorders

| | Normal values |
|---|---|
| **pH** | **7.35 – 7.45** |
| **pCO₂** | **35 – 45 mm Hg** |
| **HCO₃⁻** | **22 – 26 mEq/L (Avg = 24)** |

### Metabolic disorders

| Disorder | Causes (H⁺ side) | Causes (HCO₃⁻ side) | Compensation by lungs | Compensation by kidneys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Metabolic acidosis (↓ pH)** | **↑ H⁺:** • **Lactic acidosis**[^ety-lactic] • **Diabetic ketoacidosis**[^ety-diabetes][^pre-dia][^ety-ketoacidosis] • **Salicylic acid poisoning**[^ety-salicylate][^suf-ic] | **↓ HCO₃⁻:** • **Diarrhea**[^ety-diarrhoea][^suf-rrhoea] (intestinal contents are alkaline) | **Hyperventilation** → CO₂ washout → **↓ pCO₂ & ↓ H⁺** | **H⁺ exchanger: H⁺ excretion by intercalated cell A in CD** — **can lead to hyperkalemia**[^ety-hyperkalaemia][^suf-aemia][^7] |
| **Metabolic alkalosis (↑ pH)** | **↓ H⁺:** • **Vomiting**[^ety-vomiting] (loss of acidic stomach contents) • **Hyperaldosteronism**[^ety-hyperaldosteronism][^suf-ism] (↑ H⁺ excretion) • **Diuretics**[^ety-diuretic] → **hypovolemia**[^ety-hypovolaemia] → (+) **RAAS**[^ety-raas] → ↑ aldosteronism → H⁺ excretion → **volume contraction alkalosis**[^ety-contraction][^8] | **↑ HCO₃⁻:** • **Sodium bicarbonate ingestion** | **↓ Respiratory rate and depth (hypoventilation)** → CO₂ build up → **↑ pCO₂** | **Base excretion by type B intercalated cells** |

### Respiratory disorders

| Disorder | Cause | Compensation by kidneys |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory acidosis (↓ pH)** | **↑ pCO₂:** • **Respiratory depression: opioids**[^ety-opioid][^suf-oid]**, barbiturates**[^ety-barbiturate] **intake** • **Respiratory muscle paralysis**[^ety-paralysis][^pre-para][^suf-lysis]**: Guillain–Barré syndrome**[^ety-guillain-barre]**, Polio**[^ety-polio] • **Airway obstruction: COPD**[^ety-copd] | **↑ HCO₃⁻ by:** • **H⁺ excretion** • **Reabsorption and generation of HCO₃⁻** |
| **Respiratory alkalosis (↑ pH)** | **↓ pCO₂: hyperventilation due to:** • **Hysteria**[^ety-hysteria] • **High altitude**[^ety-altitude] | **HCO₃⁻ excretion by type B I cells** → **↓ HCO₃⁻ levels** |

🧠 Compensation always moves the *other* member of the pair in the **same direction** as the primary change: a metabolic fall in HCO₃⁻ is answered by a respiratory fall in pCO₂, a respiratory rise in pCO₂ by a renal rise in HCO₃⁻ — keeping the ratio HCO₃⁻ : pCO₂ (and so pH) near normal.[^9]

> [!note] Note — H⁺ and Ca²⁺ compete for albumin
> **H⁺ and Ca²⁺ competitively bind to albumin**[^ety-albumin] → **↓ H⁺ in respiratory alkalosis** → **↑ Ca²⁺ binding to albumin** → **↓ free form of Ca²⁺ (↓ actions)** → **hypocalcemic**[^ety-hypocalcaemia] **tetany**[^ety-tetany].

```
H+ and Ca2+ compete for albumin
   → ↓ H+ in respiratory alkalosis
   → ↑ Ca2+ binding to albumin
   → ↓ free (ionised) Ca2+  (↓ actions)
   → HYPOCALCEMIC TETANY
```

### Anion Gap[^ety-anion][^pre-ana]

**Laws of electroneutrality**[^ety-electroneutrality][^pre-electro] — the plasma must carry equal numbers of positive and negative charges.

![[Acid Base Balance - normal anion gap gamblegram.png]]
*Normal anion gap: the Na⁺ column (cations) equals the anion column — Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻ (routinely measured anions) + the small top block AG (anions not routinely measured) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Schematic diagram of the plasma anion gap — cations vs measured anions (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/Anion_gap-en.svg/800px-Anion_gap-en.svg.png)
*Schematic of the plasma anion gap: two equal columns of cations and anions; the gap is the part of the anion column that routine tests do not measure (Wikimedia Commons).*

- **Anion gap: contributes to electroneutrality.**
- **Difference between anions & cations** arising d/t[^10] **anions not routinely measured**:
  - Sulfate[^ety-sulfate] · Protein anions · Lactate · Salicylate.
- **Measured by:** **AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻)**
- **Normally = 144 − (108 + 24) = 12 mEq/L** (**Range: 8–16 mEq/L**).[^11]

| | **High anion gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA)** | **Normal anion gap metabolic acidosis (NAGMA)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | **↑ in unmeasured anions** — the AG block grows, HCO₃⁻ shrinks, Cl⁻ unchanged | **↑ in measured anions — ↑ in Cl⁻ → hyperchloremic**[^ety-hyperchloraemic] **metabolic acidosis** — HCO₃⁻ lost, Cl⁻ rises to fill the space, AG unchanged |
| **Examples** | • **Diabetic ketoacidosis** • **Lactic acidosis** • **Salicylate poisoning** | • **Diarrhea** • **Renal tubular acidosis** • **Hyperaldosteronism**[^12] |

![Anion gap in normal state, high-anion-gap and normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/93/Anion_Gap.svg/800px-Anion_Gap.svg.png)
*Graphical representation of the anion gap and how it changes: in HAGMA the unmeasured-anion block grows as HCO₃⁻ falls; in NAGMA chloride replaces the lost HCO₃⁻ and the gap stays normal — the two mini gamblegrams of the page's table (Wikimedia Commons).*

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> pH is set by a **ratio**, not by an amount: pH depends on HCO₃⁻ ÷ (0.03 × pCO₂), so the body can defend it by moving either the numerator (kidneys, metabolic side) or the denominator (lungs, respiratory side). **Buffers** bind H⁺ in seconds but leave the acid in the body; the **lungs** change pCO₂ in minutes and get pH most of the way back (up to 75 %); only the **kidneys** remove fixed acid and **generate new HCO₃⁻** — by excreting H⁺ on phosphate (titratable acid) and on ammonia (ammonium) — so only they correct the cause.
>
> Every disorder is therefore read in two steps: which member of the pair moved first (the primary disorder), and is the other member moving in the same direction to restore the ratio (compensation)? In a metabolic acidosis, the anion gap then tells you whether an unmeasured acid was **added** (HAGMA) or bicarbonate was **lost** and replaced by chloride (NAGMA).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> pH 7.35–7.45, pCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mEq/L, anion gap ≈ 12 (8–16) mEq/L. The PCT reclaims nearly all filtered HCO₃⁻ (no net gain), and the daily fixed-acid load is excreted as titratable acid and ammonium, with type A and type B intercalated cells fine-tuning the collecting duct.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> An acid load (lactic acid, ketoacids, salicylate) or HCO₃⁻ loss (diarrhoea) lowers HCO₃⁻. Buffers absorb the H⁺ at once; within minutes the medulla drives hyperventilation, CO₂ is washed out, pCO₂ falls and pH is pulled back towards normal. Over days the kidney increases H⁺ excretion and ammonia production. Blood gases show low HCO₃⁻ with low pCO₂ and a near-normal pH — the patient may only show deep, rapid breathing.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> When the acid load exceeds what lungs and kidneys can handle — or the compensating organ itself fails (respiratory muscle paralysis in GBS, opioid depression, COPD; renal tubular acidosis) — pH leaves the range. Acidaemia depresses the CNS (confusion → coma) and enzyme function; alkalaemia raises CNS excitability and lowers ionised calcium, producing tetany. Below pH 6.9 or above 7.8 the patient dies.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → acid/base load or ventilatory failure → buffering (seconds) → respiratory compensation (minutes, ≤ 75 %) → renal compensation (hours–days) → compensating organ fails → uncompensated pH change → CNS depression / tetany → death (< 6.9 or > 7.8)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> A normal pH does not mean a normal patient — a compensated disorder hides behind it. Recognising the primary disorder decides treatment (ventilate the CO₂ retainer, give fluid and insulin in DKA, replace volume and chloride in contraction alkalosis), and the anion gap narrows the cause of a metabolic acidosis to a short list.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Metabolic acidosis:** "Low HCO₃⁻, low pCO₂, deep rapid (Kussmaul) breathing" → an added acid (lactate, ketoacids, salicylate) or lost HCO₃⁻ (diarrhoea); the lungs compensate by washing out CO₂.
>
> **HAGMA:** "Anion gap > 16", "diabetic ketoacidosis", "lactic acidosis", "salicylate poisoning" → an unmeasured anion has been added along with H⁺; Cl⁻ is normal.
>
> **NAGMA / hyperchloraemic acidosis:** "Normal anion gap with high chloride", "diarrhoea", "renal tubular acidosis" → HCO₃⁻ was lost and Cl⁻ replaced it, so the gap does not change.
>
> **Metabolic alkalosis:** "Vomiting", "diuretics", "hyperaldosteronism", "volume contraction alkalosis", "bicarbonate ingestion" → H⁺ lost from stomach or kidney (aldosterone drives H⁺–K⁺ ATPase); the lungs hypoventilate to raise pCO₂.
>
> **Respiratory acidosis:** "Opioid / barbiturate overdose", "GBS", "polio", "COPD" → CO₂ retained because ventilation fails; kidneys answer with ↑ HCO₃⁻.
>
> **Respiratory alkalosis:** "Hysteria / panic attack", "high altitude", "perioral tingling and carpopedal spasm" → CO₂ washed out; ↓ H⁺ frees albumin sites for Ca²⁺ → hypocalcaemic tetany.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Gamblegram: two equal columns (cations = anions). A taller AG block with a shorter HCO₃⁻ block = HAGMA; a taller Cl⁻ block with a shorter HCO₃⁻ block and an unchanged AG = NAGMA.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> pH < 6.9 or > 7.8 → death; normal AG 8–16 mEq/L (12); lungs correct at most ~75 %; urine pH cannot fall below 4.5 (limiting pH, reached only in the collecting duct).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "PCT reabsorption of HCO₃⁻ adds bicarbonate to the body"**
>
> **What students think:** Every HCO₃⁻ the PCT returns to blood is a gain.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In the first PCT mechanism, each secreted H⁺ destroys one filtered HCO₃⁻ in the lumen and the cell makes one identical HCO₃⁻ — filtered = generated, so there is **no net gain**; the H⁺ is recycled, not excreted. A net gain happens only when the secreted H⁺ is caught by a **substitute buffer** (HPO₄²⁻ or NH₃) and leaves in the urine.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Reclaim with HCO₃⁻, generate with phosphate and ammonia."
>
> **Trap 2: Calling haemoglobin the most important buffer**
>
> **What students think:** Haemoglobin is the most important buffer of blood.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Hb is the **most plentiful intracellular** buffer (histidine residues). The **most important** buffer is bicarbonate, because its two components are independently controlled — CO₂ by the lungs and HCO₃⁻/H⁺ by the kidneys — so its capacity is constantly renewed.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Most *important* = HCO₃⁻ (regulated); most *plentiful intracellular* = Hb; *urinary* = phosphate and ammonia.
>
> **Trap 3: Expecting the lungs to correct pH completely**
>
> **What students think:** Respiratory compensation brings pH back to exactly normal.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The stimulus for compensation is the abnormal pH itself; as pH approaches normal the drive to hyperventilate fades, so the lungs correct only up to ~75 %. Only the kidney corrects fully, and only over days. Compensation never overshoots.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** A pH that is "over-corrected" means a second, mixed disorder.
>
> **Trap 4: Putting every metabolic acidosis in the high-anion-gap group**
>
> **What students think:** Any metabolic acidosis raises the anion gap.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The gap rises only when the acid brings its own unmeasured anion (lactate, acetoacetate/β-hydroxybutyrate, salicylate). In diarrhoea and RTA the body loses HCO₃⁻ itself; the kidney retains Cl⁻ in its place, so Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) stays 12 — hyperchloraemic, normal-gap acidosis.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Added acid → high gap; lost base → high chloride."
>
> **Trap 5: Forgetting the calcium side-effect of alkalosis**
>
> **What students think:** Total calcium is normal, so tetany in hyperventilation cannot be hypocalcaemic.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** H⁺ and Ca²⁺ compete for the same albumin sites. When H⁺ falls (respiratory alkalosis), albumin binds more Ca²⁺, the **free (ionised)** fraction falls while total calcium is unchanged, and the excitable membranes fire — hypocalcaemic tetany.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Alkalosis = ↓ ionised Ca²⁺ = ↑ excitability; acidosis does the reverse.

> **Related Topic 1: [[Proximal Convoluted Tubule]]**

>
> **Related Topic 2: [[Collecting Duct and Micturition Reflex]]**
> Type A and type B intercalated cells, aldosterone and hensin live here; this is where urine is finally acidified towards the limiting pH of 4.5.

>
> **Related Topic 4: [[Diffusion and Transport of Gases]]**
> Most CO₂ travels as HCO₃⁻ made by carbonic anhydrase in red cells, and haemoglobin buffers the H⁺ — the same equation that opens this chapter.
>
> **Related Topic 5: [[Homeostasis]]**
> pH sits at the top of the correction priority (pH > BP > temperature) because enzymes stop working outside 7.35–7.45.
>
> **Related Topic 6: [[Calcium Homeostasis]]** and **[[Adrenal Gland]]**
> Alkalosis lowers ionised calcium (tetany); aldosterone drives H⁺ excretion, so hyperaldosteronism and volume contraction cause metabolic alkalosis.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ (carbonic anhydrase) H₂CO₃ (weak acid) ⇌ H⁺ (acid) + HCO₃⁻ (base).**
> 2. Normal **pH 7.35–7.45**; **> 7.8 or < 6.9 → death**. Alkalosis ↑ CNS excitability; acidosis ↓ CNS excitability.
> 3. **First line = buffers** — maintain pH **without correcting the cause**; **HCO₃⁻ = most important buffer** (CO₂ regulated by lung, H⁺ by kidney).
> 4. Intracellular buffers: **proteins with histidine — Hb in RBCs = most plentiful intracellular buffer**; **phosphate and ammonia = urinary buffers**.
> 5. **Second line = lungs** — alter pCO₂ (**alveolar ventilation ∝ 1/pCO₂**), **correct up to 75 %**.
> 6. **Third line = kidneys — correct the cause**; **generation of HCO₃⁻ is unique to the kidney**.
> 7. PCT HCO₃⁻ reabsorption: **filtered = generated → no net gain**; new HCO₃⁻ only with **HPO₄²⁻ (titratable acid)** and **NH₃ from glutamine (ammonium)** — **net gain**.
> 8. **Limiting pH = 4.5** — never reached in PCT, **seen in the collecting duct** where urine is acidified.
> 9. Collecting duct regulated by **hensin**; **type A** intercalated cell = **acid excretion** (luminal H⁺–K⁺ ATPase, **aldosterone**); **type B** = **base excretion** (luminal HCO₃⁻–Cl⁻ exchanger).
> 10. Normal values: **pCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg; HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mEq/L (avg 24)**.
> 11. Respiratory acidosis: **opioids, barbiturates, GBS, polio, COPD**; respiratory alkalosis: **hysteria, high altitude**.
> 12. **Respiratory alkalosis → ↑ Ca²⁺ binding to albumin → ↓ free Ca²⁺ → hypocalcaemic tetany.**
> 13. **AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) = 144 − (108 + 24) = 12 mEq/L (8–16)**; **HAGMA**: DKA, lactic acidosis, salicylate; **NAGMA (hyperchloraemic)**: diarrhoea, RTA.

[^1]: Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = 6.1 + log [HCO₃⁻] ÷ (0.03 × pCO₂). With HCO₃⁻ 24 mEq/L and pCO₂ 40 mm Hg the ratio is 20 : 1 and pH = 7.4. Carbonic anhydrase speeds the hydration of CO₂ several thousand-fold; it is plentiful in red cells and renal tubular cells.
[^2]: The page reads "demineralization"; textbooks describe the effect of abnormal pH on enzymes as altered ionisation and **denaturation** (loss of the folded shape) of the enzyme protein, which lowers its activity. The page's wording is kept in the body.
[^3]: The bicarbonate system's pKa (6.1) is far from 7.4, so chemically it is not a strong buffer; its power comes from being an *open* system — the lungs adjust CO₂ and the kidneys adjust HCO₃⁻ continuously (Guyton).
[^4]: Guyton gives the respiratory system a feedback gain of about 1–3: it returns pH 50–75 % of the way to normal within 3–12 minutes; it cannot correct fully because the stimulus (abnormal pH) fades as pH improves.
[^5]: Ammonium is the kidney's main adaptable route: about 30–50 mmol of H⁺ a day leaves as NH₄⁺ normally, rising to as much as 500 mmol/day in chronic acidosis as glutamine metabolism is induced; titratable acid (mainly phosphate) is about 10–30 mmol/day and cannot rise much.

[^ety-barbiturate]: **barbiturate** — from *barbituric acid*, synthesised by Adolf von Baeyer (1864); the name is said to honour a woman named Barbara or St Barbara's day — origin uncertain + *uric* (urea) + *-ate*.
[^ety-paralysis]: **paralysis** — Greek *para-*, "beside, abnormal" + *lyein*, "to loosen" → *paralysis*, "loosening (disabling) at the side" → loss of muscle power. **Differentiate:** *paralysis* (complete loss) · *paresis* (*parienai*, "to let go" — partial weakness) · *palsy* (Old French *paralisie*, the same word worn down).
[^ety-guillain-barre]: **Guillain–Barré syndrome** — Georges Guillain (1876–1961) and Jean Alexandre Barré (1880–1967), French neurologists, who with André Strohl described it in 1916 in two soldiers: an acute ascending demyelinating polyneuropathy that can paralyse the respiratory muscles.
[^ety-polio]: **poliomyelitis** — Greek *polios*, "grey" + *myelos*, "marrow (spinal cord)" + *-itis*, "inflammation" → inflammation of the grey matter (anterior horn cells); bulbar or high-spinal polio paralyses breathing.
[^ety-copd]: **COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease)** — Greek *chronos*, "time" (long-lasting) + Latin *ob-* "against" + *struere* "to build" (built in the way) + *pulmo*, "lung" → long-standing airway blockage; CO₂ retention → chronic respiratory acidosis with renal compensation.
[^ety-hysteria]: **hysteria** — Greek *hystera*, "womb" (the ancient belief that a wandering womb caused it) → older term for functional/psychogenic disorders and panic; hyperventilation in panic washes out CO₂.
[^ety-altitude]: **altitude** — Latin *altus*, "high" + *-itudo*, "state of" → height above sea level. Low inspired PO₂ stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors → hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis (later compensated by renal HCO₃⁻ loss).
[^ety-albumin]: **albumin** — Latin *albumen*, "white of an egg" (*albus*, white) + *-in* → the main plasma protein; its negatively charged sites bind H⁺ and Ca²⁺ competitively, and it is the largest unmeasured anion.
[^ety-hypocalcaemia]: **hypocalcaemia** — Greek *hypo-*, "under" + Latin *calx, calcis*, "lime, limestone" + Greek *haima*, "blood" → low blood calcium; in alkalosis it is the *ionised* fraction that falls.
[^ety-tetany]: **tetany** — Greek *tetanos*, "tension, stretching" (*teinein*, to stretch) → sustained involuntary muscle spasm (carpopedal spasm, Trousseau and Chvostek signs) from low ionised Ca²⁺. **Differentiate:** *tetany* (low Ca²⁺) · *tetanus* (the disease from *Clostridium tetani* toxin, and also a fused muscle contraction in physiology).
[^ety-anion]: **anion / cation** — Greek *ana*, "up" + *ion*, "going" → the ion that goes up to the anode (+); *kata*, "down" → *cation* goes down to the cathode (−). Words coined by Faraday (1834). Anions are negative (Cl⁻, HCO₃⁻), cations positive (Na⁺, K⁺).
[^ety-electroneutrality]: **electroneutrality** — Greek *ēlektron*, "amber" (rubbed amber gathers charge) + Latin *neuter*, "neither" + *-ity* → the state of being neither positive nor negative: total cations = total anions in any body fluid.
[^ety-sulfate]: **sulfate** — Latin *sulfur*, "brimstone" + *-ate* (salt of an *-ic* acid) → salt of sulphuric acid; from metabolism of sulphur-containing amino acids, it accumulates in renal failure (HAGMA).
[^ety-hyperchloraemic]: **hyperchloraemic** — Greek *hyper-*, "over" + *chlōros*, "pale green" (the colour of chlorine gas) + *haima*, "blood" + *-ic* → with too much chloride in the blood — the hallmark of NAGMA.
[^pre-an]: **a- / an-** — without, not: anhydrase (without water), anion is *not* this (it is *ana-*), anaemia, anuria. Don't confuse with **ana-** up, again (anion, anabolic) · **ante-** before · **anti-** against.
[^suf-ase]: **-ase** — an enzyme: carbonic anhydrase, ATPase, kinase, lipase (from *diastase*, the first enzyme named, 1833). Don't confuse with **-ose** a sugar (glucose, fructose) · **-ate** a salt (phosphate).
[^pre-de]: **de-** — down, away, removal, reversal: derangement, demineralization, depolarisation, denaturation. Don't confuse with **di- / dis-** apart, two (diffusion, disaccharide) · **dia-** through.
[^suf-ation]: **-ation / -ization / -ion** — the process or result of an action: demineralization, ventilation, generation, excretion, reabsorption, contraction. Don't confuse with **-osis** an abnormal state (acidosis) · **-ism** a condition or doctrine (hyperaldosteronism).
[^suf-osis]: **-osis** — a process, an abnormal state or increase: acidosis, alkalosis, ketoacidosis. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation (poliomyelitis) · **-iasis** a disease from an agent (cholelithiasis) · **-esis** an action (diuresis).
[^pre-extra]: **extra-** — outside, beyond: extracellular, extravascular. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exocrine) · **ex-** out of (excretion) · **intra-** within · **inter-** between.
[^pre-intra]: **intra-** — within, inside: intracellular, intravenous, intra-tubular. Don't confuse with **inter-** between (intercalated) · **intro-** inward · **extra-** outside.
[^suf-ine]: **-ine** — chemical suffix for amino acids, amines and alkaloids: histidine, glutamine, creatinine, morphine. Don't confuse with **-in** a protein or hormone (albumin, renin, insulin) · **-ide** a binary compound (chloride).
[^suf-ate]: **-ate** — salt or ester of an *-ic* acid: phosphate (phosphoric), sulfate, lactate, salicylate, bicarbonate. Don't confuse with **-ite** salt of an *-ous* acid (sulphite, nitrite — one oxygen fewer) · **-ide** binary salt (chloride).
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, above, excessive: hyperventilation, hyperkalaemia, hyperaldosteronism, hyperchloraemic. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under, deficient · **supra- / super-** above (Latin equivalents).
[^pre-hypo]: **hypo-** — under, below, deficient: hypoventilation, hypovolaemia, hypocalcaemia. Don't confuse with **hyper-** over · **hypno-** sleep · **sub- / infra-** below (Latin equivalents).
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (ef-, e-)** — out of, away: excretion, exchanger, excitability. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exocrine) · **extra-** beyond (extracellular) · **ec- / ecto-** out, outer.
[^pre-re]: **re-** — back, again: reabsorption, recycling, regeneration, replenishment. Don't confuse with **retro-** backwards, behind (retroperitoneal) · **pre-** before.
[^suf-ule]: **-ule / -ula / -ulus / -olus** — small (diminutive): tubule, alveolus, capillary (*capillus*), molecule. Don't confuse with **-ole** in chemistry, a ring compound (imidazole) · **-ul** in *ampulla*, which is itself the diminutive.
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: peritubular, pericardium, perioral. Don't confuse with **para-** beside (paralysis, parathyroid) · **pre-** before · **epi-** upon.
[^pre-non]: **non-** — not (Latin *non*): non-ionic, non-volatile, non-titratable. Don't confuse with **nona-** nine · **un- / in- / a-** not (English, Latin and Greek equivalents).
[^pre-inter]: **inter-** — between: intercalated, interstitial, intercostal. Don't confuse with **intra-** within (intracellular) · **infra-** below.
[^pre-dia]: **dia-** — through, across, apart: diabetes, diarrhoea, diuretic, dialysis. Don't confuse with **di-** two (dimer, disaccharide) · **dys-** bad, difficult (dyspnoea).
[^suf-ic]: **-ic** — "of, relating to"; in chemistry the higher-oxidation acid: carbonic, salicylic, lactic, phosphoric, hyperchloraemic. Don't confuse with **-ous** the lower-oxidation acid (sulphurous) · **-ical** (synonym: physiological).
[^suf-rrhoea]: **-rrhoea / -rrhea** — flow, discharge: diarrhoea, amenorrhoea, rhinorrhoea. Don't confuse with **-rrhage** bursting forth of blood (haemorrhage) · **-rrhaphy** suturing · **-rrhexis** rupture.
[^suf-aemia]: **-aemia / -emia** — a state of the blood: hyperkalaemia, hypovolaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperchloraemic, acidaemia. Don't confuse with **-uria** a state of the urine (proteinuria) · **anaemia** (*an-* + *haima* — lack of blood, a whole word).
[^suf-ism]: **-ism** — a state, condition or doctrine: hyperaldosteronism, hypothyroidism, metabolism. Don't confuse with **-osis** a process/abnormal state (acidosis) · **-ist** a person (physiologist).
[^suf-oid]: **-oid** — like, resembling: opioid, steroid, thyroid, arachnoid. Don't confuse with **-ode** a path or way (electrode, cathode) · **-ose** sugar.
[^pre-para]: **para-** — beside, alongside, abnormal: paralysis, parathyroid, paracrine. Don't confuse with **peri-** around (peritubular) · **pre-** before.
[^suf-lysis]: **-lysis / -lytic** — loosening, breaking down, dissolving: paralysis, glycolysis, haemolysis, electrolyte. Don't confuse with **-lithiasis** stones · **-ptosis** falling, drooping.
[^pre-ana]: **ana-** — up, upward, again: anion (goes up to the anode), anabolic, anaphylaxis. Don't confuse with **an- / a-** without (anhydrase) · **ante-** before · **anti-** against.
[^pre-electro]: **electro-** — electricity, charge (Greek *ēlektron*, amber): electroneutrality, electrolyte, electrocardiogram. Don't confuse with **electrode** (*-ode*, a path) — it is a related whole word, not a prefix.
