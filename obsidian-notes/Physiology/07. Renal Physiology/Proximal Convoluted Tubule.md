# 🧽 Proximal Convoluted Tubule

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The proximal[^ety-proximal] convoluted[^ety-convoluted][^pre-con] tubule[^ety-tubule][^suf-ule] (PCT) is the **workhorse of reabsorption**[^ety-reabsorption][^pre-re]: it takes back **two-thirds (67 %) of the filtered NaCl, water, K⁺ and urea**, and **100 % of glucose and amino acids**, so the fluid leaving it is still **isotonic**. Everything in this chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **What makes it so good at absorbing?** — microvilli, mitochondria, infolded basal membrane, leaky tight junctions
> - ✅ **How are Na⁺, glucose and water carried?** — Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase on the blood side drives NHE, SGLT2, Na⁺–amino acid and Na⁺–PO₄³⁻ carriers; water follows through aquaporin-1
> - ✅ **What happens when a carrier saturates?** — glucose transport maximum (375 mg/min; threshold 200 mg/dL) → glycosuria; PAH secretion plateaus

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Sponge Wrung by a Pump

Think of the PCT as a **giant sponge** (microvilli = the sponge's surface) whose cells are kept "thirsty for sodium" by a **pump on the back wall** (Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase). Because the pump keeps Na⁺ low inside the cell, sodium rushes in from the lumen — and everything that shares a door with sodium (glucose, amino acids, phosphate, H⁺ going the other way) is dragged along. Water simply follows the salt, so the fluid stays isotonic. A door can only turn so fast: when too much glucose arrives, the doors are all busy (**saturation**) and the excess spills into the urine.

```
BASOLATERAL PUMP (Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase, needs ATP from mitochondria)
        │ keeps cell Na⁺ LOW
        ▼
LUMINAL DOORS (Na⁺ in, downhill) ── drag ──► glucose (SGLT2) · amino acids · PO₄³⁻ · H⁺ out (NHE)
        ▼
WATER FOLLOWS (AQP-1, obligatory, no hormone) → fluid leaves ISOTONIC (TF/P Na⁺ = 1)
        ▼
CARRIERS SATURATE → glucose Tm 375 mg/min (threshold 200 mg/dL) → glycosuria · PAH secretion plateau
```

## ♻️ Reabsorption

| Solute / Substance | Proportion of filtered substance reabsorbed |
|---|---|
| **NaCl** | **67 % (2/3rd)** |
| **H₂O** | **67 % (2/3rd)** |
| **K⁺** | **67 % (2/3rd)** |
| **Urea**[^ety-urea] | **67 % (2/3rd)**[^5] |
| **Ca²⁺** | **70 %** |
| **Phosphate**[^ety-phosphate] **& bicarbonate**[^ety-bicarbonate][^pre-bi] | **80 %** |
| **Glucose**[^ety-glucose][^suf-ose]**, amino acids** | **100 %** |

> [!info] Equal amounts of Na⁺ and H₂O reabsorbed → **PCT fluid is isotonic**[^ety-isotonic][^pre-iso]
> 🧠 Because water leaves exactly in step with sodium, the osmolarity of the tubular fluid at the end of the PCT is still ~300 mOsm/L — the same as plasma. The volume has fallen by two-thirds, the concentration has not.

> [!warning] **Note: PCT is the only segment absorbing glucose.**

### SODIUM REABSORPTION

![[Proximal Convoluted Tubule - sodium reabsorption in PCT cell.png]]
*Sodium reabsorption across a PCT cell — Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase on the blood side; on the lumen side the Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger (NHE), SGLT2, Na⁺–amino acid and Na⁺–PO₄³⁻ cotransporters; Cl⁻ rises in the lumen and is reabsorbed later (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Side | Transporter | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood side** (peritubular[^ety-peritubular][^pre-peri] capillary) | **Na⁺ K⁺ ATPase**[^ety-atpase][^suf-ase] | Pumps 3 Na⁺ out to blood, 2 K⁺ in → keeps cell Na⁺ low |
| **Urine side** (lumen[^ety-lumen]) | **Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger (NHE)**[^ety-exchanger] | Na⁺ in, H⁺ out → **major channel protein**[^6] |
| Urine side | **SGLT 2** (**Na⁺ glucose cotransporter**[^ety-cotransporter][^pre-co] **2**) | Na⁺ + glucose in together |
| Urine side | **Na⁺ amino acid cotransporter** | Na⁺ + amino acids in |
| Urine side | **Na⁺ PO₄³⁻ cotransporter** | Na⁺ + phosphate in |
| Lumen | **↑ Cl⁻** | **d/t preferential absorption of Na⁺ in early PCT** (Cl⁻ **reabsorbed in later parts of PCT**)[^7] |

> [!note] **Note: SGLT-1 is found in GIT.**
> 🧠 SGLT1 (high affinity, low capacity) absorbs glucose and galactose in the small intestine (and also sits in the late S3 segment); SGLT2 (low affinity, high capacity) does ~90 % of renal glucose reabsorption in S1–S2.[^8]

*Glucose reabsorption through SGLT2 on the luminal membrane — 100 % reabsorption by secondary active transport that can saturate; SGLT2 blockers (canagliflozin, empagliflozin, sotagliflozin) ↓ reabsorption and ↑ glucose excretion → used in diabetes mellitus, but ↑ bacterial growth → UTI, especially in older people (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Blockers: Canagliflozin, Empagliflozin, Sotagliflozin**[^ety-gliflozin] (⊖ SGLT 2)[^10]
- **↓ reabsorption & ↑ excretion of glucose.**

                    ▼                              ▼
     Used to manage diabetes mellitus       ↑ bacterial growth
                                                   │

### Saturation of SGLT-2

- **At 200 mg/dL of plasma glucose → 375 mg/dl of glucose is transported (Transport maximum).**[^11]

```
Plasma glucose 200 mg/dL
        ↓
Saturation of SGLT-2
        ↓
Excretion of excess glucose
```

- **Splay**[^ety-splay]**: deviation in reabsorption curve d/t diverse / heterogeneous**[^ety-heterogeneous][^pre-hetero] **nephrons → different TmG.**

![[Proximal Convoluted Tubule - glucose titration curve.png]]
*Glucose titration curve from the notes — filtered load rises in a straight line; reabsorption rises with it, then bends (splay) and plateaus at TmG = 375 mg/min (saturation); excretion (renal glycosuria) starts at a plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Line on the graph | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Filtered level at glomerulus** | Straight line — GFR × plasma glucose |
| **Reabsorption (plateau) due to saturation** | Rises, then flattens at **TmG = 375** |
| **Splay** | The rounded bend before the plateau |
| **Excretion = Renal glycosuria**[^ety-glycosuria][^pre-glyco][^suf-uria] | Starts at **200 mg/dl** (renal threshold) |

### WATER REABSORPTION

![[Proximal Convoluted Tubule - water reabsorption.png]]
*Water reabsorption in the PCT — Na⁺ enters through the Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger and water follows through aquaporin-1 (AQP-1): obligatory water reabsorption by osmosis, not influenced by hormones; facultative (ADH-dependent) reabsorption happens later in the collecting duct (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Luminal protein | Role |
|---|---|
| **Na⁺–H⁺ exchanger** | Brings Na⁺ into the cell |
| **Aquaporin 1 (AqP 1)**[^ety-aquaporin][^pre-aqua] | Water channel — water follows the Na⁺ |

**Obligatory**[^ety-obligatory][^pre-ob] **water reabsorption: osmosis**[^ety-osmosis][^suf-osis]**.**
- **Na⁺ follows H₂O.**[^12]
- **Not influenced by hormones.**

> [!note] Note: **Facultative**[^ety-facultative] **water reabsorption** occurs in the **collecting duct** (**facilitated diffusion**[^ety-diffusion]).
> - **Hormone dependent: ADH**[^ety-adh][^pre-anti].

### TUBULAR FLUID PLASMA RATIO (TF/P)

- **↑ reabsorption** → **↑ plasma conc. (P)** and **↓ tubular conc. (TF)**.
- **TF/P = 1 or close to 1**: **reabsorption is same as that of water in PCT.**

🧠 TF/P compares the concentration of a substance in the tubular fluid with its concentration in plasma at the same point. If a solute is taken back faster than water, it becomes dilute in the tubule (TF/P < 1); slower than water, it becomes concentrated (TF/P > 1); exactly with water, it stays at 1.

![[Proximal Convoluted Tubule - TF-P ratio along proximal tubule.png]]
*TF/P ratios along the length of the proximal tubule — Cl⁻ and creatinine rise above 1, urea, Na⁺ and osmolality stay near 1, HCO₃⁻, amino acids and glucose fall far below 1 (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| TF/P | Substances (on the graph)[^13] | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **> 1** | **Cl⁻, creatinine**[^ety-creatinine] | **Reabsorption < water** |
| **= 1** | **Urea, Na⁺, Osm** | Reabsorbed in step with water |
| **< 1** | **HCO₃⁻, amino acids, glucose** | **Reabsorption > water** |

## 📤 Secretion

- **PCT secretes para amino hippuric acid (PAH)**[^ety-pah][^pre-para] **via carrier protein → saturation seen.**
- **Excretion of PAH (E) = Filtered load (F) + secretion (S).**

![[Proximal Convoluted Tubule - PAH secretion curve.png]]
*PAH titration curve from the notes — filtered load is a straight line; secretion rises and then plateaus (saturation); excretion is steep early (filtration + secretion) and less steep later (only filtration, secretion saturated) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Part of the excretion curve | Slope | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Early part of excretion** | **More steep (Filtration + secretion)** | **E = F + S** |
| **2. Late part of excretion** | **Less steep (Only filtration)** | **E = F** (No secretion d/t saturation)[^14] |

- **Secretion: plateau d/t saturation.**
- **Filtered load:** rises in a straight line with plasma PAH.

🧠 At low plasma PAH, almost every molecule that escapes filtration is secreted, so PAH clearance ≈ renal plasma flow. Once the carrier saturates, the extra PAH can leave only by filtration, the excretion line runs parallel to the filtered-load line, and PAH clearance falls toward GFR.

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> The PCT is a **sodium-driven, isotonic, high-capacity reabsorber**. One basolateral pump (Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase, powered by abundant mitochondria) keeps intracellular Na⁺ low; every luminal carrier then uses the inward Na⁺ gradient to pull something else in (glucose, amino acids, phosphate) or push H⁺ out (NHE). Water follows passively through aquaporin-1 and leaky tight junctions, so two-thirds of the filtrate goes back with **no change in osmolarity** and **no hormone needed**.
>
> Because the work is done by **carriers**, it can **saturate**: glucose reabsorption has a transport maximum (375 mg/min; threshold 200 mg/dL) and PAH secretion plateaus. Saturation explains glycosuria in diabetes, the action of SGLT2 inhibitors and why PAH clearance measures RPF only at low plasma levels.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Plasma glucose 70–110 mg/dL, filtered glucose well below Tm, so 100 % is reabsorbed and urine is glucose-free. NaCl, water, K⁺ are reabsorbed 67 %, HCO₃⁻ and phosphate 80 %, Ca²⁺ 70 %, and the fluid entering the loop is isotonic.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> As plasma glucose rises above ~200 mg/dL (uncontrolled diabetes), the least efficient nephrons saturate first (splay) and glucose appears in the urine before the average Tm of 375 mg/min is reached. Osmotic diuresis begins. On an SGLT2 inhibitor the same glycosuria is deliberate and lowers blood sugar.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Heavy glycosuria drags water and Na⁺ with it → polyuria, thirst, dehydration; glucose-rich urine feeds bacteria → genital mycotic infections and UTIs (especially in the elderly). In shock, the oxygen-hungry S3 segment undergoes acute tubular necrosis, and a generalised PCT carrier failure (Fanconi syndrome) wastes glucose, amino acids, phosphate and bicarbonate.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → hyperglycaemia / SGLT2 block / ischaemia → splay and threshold reached (200 mg/dL) → Tm exceeded (375 mg/min) → glycosuria → osmotic diuresis → dehydration, UTI; ischaemia → S3 necrosis → AKI
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Glycosuria with normal blood sugar points to a tubular defect (renal glycosuria, Fanconi syndrome, pregnancy — lower threshold), not diabetes. SGLT2 inhibitors are now first-line add-ons in diabetes, heart failure and CKD, but patients must be warned about UTIs, genital infections and euglycaemic ketoacidosis.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Renal glycosuria:** "Glucose in urine, normal blood sugar" → a lowered threshold or Tm (SGLT2 gene defect, pregnancy) — the PCT, not the pancreas, is at fault.
>
> **Diabetes mellitus glycosuria:** "Plasma glucose > 180–200 mg/dL", "polyuria, polydipsia" → filtered load exceeds Tm; glucose drags water (osmotic diuresis).
>
> **SGLT2 inhibitor:** "-gliflozin", "UTI / genital mycotic infection in an elderly diabetic", "weight loss, ↓ BP" → glycosuria by design; natriuresis lowers BP.
>
> **Acute tubular necrosis:** "Shock", "muddy-brown casts", "S3 segment / straight part of PCT" → the most ischaemia-sensitive segment dies first.
>
> **Fanconi syndrome:** "Glycosuria + aminoaciduria + phosphaturia + bicarbonaturia" → global PCT transport failure (the 100 %, 80 % rows of the table all fail at once).
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> **Brush border** (PAS-positive microvilli) on cuboidal cells with eosinophilic, mitochondria-rich cytoplasm = PCT on histology; a titration curve with a rounded **splay** before the plateau = glucose Tm.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Renal threshold ~200 mg/dL (≈ 180 in many texts); Tm glucose 375 mg/min (men) / ~300 (women); TF/P of glucose falls to near 0 within the first quarter of the PCT.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Confusing renal threshold with transport maximum**
>
> **What students think:** Glucose appears in the urine only when Tm (375 mg/min) is reached.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Glucose starts appearing at a plasma level of ~200 mg/dL (the **threshold**), well before the average Tm is reached, because nephrons are heterogeneous — some saturate early. The rounded part of the reabsorption curve between threshold and Tm is the **splay**.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Threshold = a **plasma concentration** (mg/dL); Tm = a **rate** (mg/min); splay = the gap between them.
>
> **Trap 2: "SGLT1 is the main renal glucose carrier"**
>
> **What students think:** SGLT1 and SGLT2 are interchangeable.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** SGLT2 (low affinity, high capacity, early PCT) does most renal reabsorption and is the drug target; SGLT1 is the intestinal (GIT) carrier and mops up the remainder in late S3.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**2** for the kidney (two kidneys), **1** for the one gut."
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking PCT reabsorption changes osmolarity**
>
> **What students think:** Removing two-thirds of the salt makes the fluid dilute.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Water leaves in exactly the same proportion (through AQP-1 and leaky junctions), so osmolarity stays ~300 mOsm/L — PCT fluid is **isotonic**. Dilution starts only in the thick ascending limb.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** PCT = **volume** changes, not **concentration**; TF/P of Na⁺ and Osm = 1.
>
> **Trap 4: Calling PCT water reabsorption ADH-dependent**
>
> **What students think:** ADH controls all water reabsorption.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** PCT reabsorption is **obligatory** — it follows Na⁺ by osmosis through AQP-1, regardless of hormones. Only the **facultative** reabsorption in the collecting duct (AQP-2) is ADH-dependent.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** AQP-1 = obligatory (PCT, descending limb); AQP-2 = facultative (collecting duct, ADH).
>
> **Trap 5: Reading TF/P > 1 as "secreted"**
>
> **What students think:** A TF/P above 1 means the substance is added to the tubule.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** TF/P rises above 1 whenever a solute is reabsorbed **less** than water (Cl⁻ early in the PCT, creatinine), because water removal concentrates what is left behind. Secretion is one cause, but not the only one.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Compare with water: < 1 = reabsorbed faster than water; = 1 = with water; > 1 = slower than water (or secreted).

>

[^1]: The page writes the bracket as "Filtration − (Reabsorption + secretion)". The standard mass-balance equation is **Excretion = Filtration − Reabsorption + Secretion** (secretion *adds* to what is excreted), as written in [[Glomerular Filtration Rate]]. The page's wording is kept above.
[^2]: The basolateral infoldings interdigitate with neighbouring cells and are packed with mitochondria (seen as basal striations on light microscopy); they hold most of the Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase that powers all PCT transport.
[^3]: "Leaky" tight junctions have low electrical resistance and let water and some ions (Cl⁻, K⁺, Ca²⁺) move between cells — the paracellular route. This is why PCT reabsorption is isosmotic and why the PCT cannot build steep gradients (unlike the "tight" collecting duct).
[^4]: The S3 segment (pars recta) in the outer medulla has a high metabolic rate and relies on oxidative metabolism, but medullary blood flow and Po₂ are low — so it is the site of maximal damage in ischaemic acute tubular necrosis (and also in many toxic injuries).
[^5]: The page gives urea 67 %; Guyton gives ~50 % of filtered urea reabsorbed in the PCT (TF/P of urea rises slightly above 1, as on the TF/P graph). The page's value is kept in the table.
[^6]: NHE3 (sodium–hydrogen exchanger isoform 3) is the main luminal Na⁺ entry route in the PCT; it is a secondary active **antiporter** rather than a channel, and its H⁺ secretion reclaims filtered HCO₃⁻.
[^7]: Early PCT reabsorbs Na⁺ with HCO₃⁻, glucose and amino acids, leaving Cl⁻ behind, so luminal Cl⁻ rises (TF/P ≈ 1.2–1.4). In the late PCT this Cl⁻ gradient drives Cl⁻ reabsorption, partly between the cells (paracellular), and creates a lumen-positive potential that pushes Na⁺ out too.
[^8]: SGLT2: 1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose, low affinity, high capacity, S1–S2, ~90 % of renal glucose reabsorption. SGLT1: 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose, high affinity, low capacity, S3 and small intestine (glucose and galactose). Glucose leaves the PCT cell into blood by facilitated diffusion through GLUT2.
[^9]: "2° active transport" (secondary active transport): glucose moves uphill into the cell using the energy of Na⁺ moving downhill; that Na⁺ gradient is made by the ATP-consuming Na⁺–K⁺ pump (primary active transport).
[^10]: Dapagliflozin and ertugliflozin are other common SGLT2 inhibitors. **Sotagliflozin** is a dual SGLT1/SGLT2 inhibitor (it also blocks intestinal SGLT1). Other effects: mild natriuresis and BP fall, weight loss, cardio- and renoprotection; adverse effects — genital mycotic infections, UTI, volume depletion, euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis.
[^11]: ⚠ The page writes "375 mg/dl"; transport maximum is a **rate**, so the unit is **mg/min** (Guyton: average TmG ≈ 375 mg/min in men, ≈ 300 in women). The threshold is a plasma concentration: Guyton ~200 mg/dL arterial (~180 mg/dL venous in many clinical texts). Filtered load at 100 mg/dL = 125 mL/min × 1 mg/mL = 125 mg/min.
[^12]: The page writes "Na⁺ follows H₂O"; physiologically it is the other way round — **water follows Na⁺** (Na⁺ reabsorption creates the osmotic gradient and water moves after it through AQP-1). The page's wording is kept in the body.
[^13]: Classic values at the end of the PCT (Costanzo): TF/P inulin ≈ 3 (two-thirds of water removed), Cl⁻ ≈ 1.2–1.4, Na⁺ and osmolarity = 1, HCO₃⁻ ≈ 0.2–0.3, glucose and amino acids → near 0 by the first quarter of the tubule. Inulin's TF/P is used to mark the fraction of water reabsorbed.
[^14]: Once secretion is saturated, PAH clearance falls progressively toward GFR (because secretion becomes a smaller fraction of the excreted load). This is why RPF is measured with **low** plasma PAH concentrations.
