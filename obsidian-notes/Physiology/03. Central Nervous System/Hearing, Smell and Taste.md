# 👂 Hearing, Smell and Taste

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Three special senses that share one plan — a **receptor cell**, a **relay** and a **cortex** — but use different stimuli: **hearing** is mechanical (sound bends the hair cells of the **cochlea**[^ety-cochlea]), **smell** (**olfaction**[^ety-olfaction][^suf-ory]) and **taste** (**gustation**[^ety-gustation][^suf-ation]) are chemical. The chapter answers:
> - ✅ **How is the stimulus turned into a signal?** — K⁺ entry into hair cells, odorant binding on bipolar olfactory neurons, five taste receptors.
> - ✅ **Which pathway does it take?** — E COLI MA for hearing, bulb → limbic cortex/thalamus → orbitofrontal for smell, VII/IX/X → NTS → VPM for taste.
> - ✅ **What goes wrong clinically?** — ototoxicity, OAE testing, hyperacusis, anosmia after cribriform fracture, entorhinal cortex in Alzheimer's.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Piano, a Sniffer Dog and Five Locks

The **cochlea is a piano keyboard rolled up**: each place on the basilar membrane is tuned to one pitch (base high, apex low) and the hair cell at that place rings the bell when it bends toward its tallest cilium. **Smell is a sniffer dog** with a direct line to memory and emotion — it reaches the limbic cortex without waiting for the thalamus. **Taste is five locks** (sweet, salt, sour, bitter, umami) on the taste receptor cell, each with its own key-receptor, reported up three cranial nerves to one medullary nucleus.

```
HEARING : sound → basilar membrane (base: high · apex: low) → hair cell bends TOWARD kinocilium → K⁺ IN from endolymph (+80 mV) → depolarise
          → Eighth nerve → Cochlear nuclei → superior Olivary → Lateral lemniscus → Inferior colliculus → Medial geniculate → Auditory cortex 41, 42
          (mnemonic E COLI MA)
SMELL   : odour → bipolar olfactory neuron → cribriform plate → bulb (mitral, tufted: glutamate) → limbic cortex + thalamus → orbitofrontal cortex
TASTE   : sweet T1R · salt ENaC · sour HCN · bitter T2 · umami mGluR4 → VII (ant 2/3) · IX (post 1/3) · X → NTS → VPM → taste centre
```

**Hair cell movement (in response to sound):**

```
                 Hair cell movement (in response to sound)
             ┌──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┐
     Towards kinocilium                            Away from kinocilium
             ↓                                             ↓
  K⁺ influx (from endolymph)                        Hyperpolarization
             ↓                                             ↓
       Depolarization                         Inhibits nerve conduction
             ↓
   Conducts nerve impulse
```

- **Towards kinocilium → K⁺ influx (from endolymph) → depolarization**[^ety-depolarization][^pre-de] **→ conducts nerve impulse.**
- **Away from kinocilium → hyperpolarization**[^pre-hyper] **→ inhibits nerve conduction.**
- 🧠 Unusually, the hair cell is **depolarised by K⁺ entering**, not leaving — because the endolymph bathing its top is K⁺-rich and +80 mV, so both concentration and voltage push K⁺ in.[^3]

- **AKA frequency analyzer.**
- **Principle: travelling wave theory.**[^ety-bekesy]

|---|---|
| **Broad** | **Narrow** |

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Each of these senses works by a **receptor cell that changes its membrane potential when its specific stimulus arrives**, and a **dedicated relay chain** that ends in its own cortex. Hearing is unique in that the **K⁺-rich, +80 mV endolymph** makes K⁺ the depolarising ion: bending the hair bundle toward the kinocilium lets K⁺ in, and the **place** on the basilar membrane that vibrates codes the pitch (base high, apex low). The auditory pathway (E COLI MA) is bilateral from the superior olive upward, which is why the olive and colliculus can compare the two ears to find the direction of a sound.
>
> Smell and taste are chemical senses: smell uses **bipolar neurons that regenerate** and reaches the **limbic cortex (memory, emotion)** before the thalamus; taste uses **five receptor types**, three cranial nerves (VII, IX, X), the **NTS** and the **VPM**.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Sound travels as a wave along the basilar membrane, peaks at its characteristic place and bends the hair cells; OHCs amplify (producing otoacoustic emissions) and IHCs send most afferent signals up the VIII nerve; the stapedial reflex damps loud sound. Odorants are detected by bipolar neurons replaced from basal cells; tastes by type II/III cells.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Early ototoxicity (aminoglycosides) damages outer hair cells first — OAEs disappear while the audiogram may still be near normal, starting at high frequencies (base). Partial facial nerve weakness reduces the stapedial reflex and loud sounds become uncomfortable. Mild smell loss is often unnoticed; early Alzheimer's begins silently in the entorhinal cortex.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Inner and outer hair cells are lost → permanent sensorineural deafness. Complete facial palsy → hyperacusis on that side. Cribriform plate fracture shears the olfactory neurons → anosmia (often with CSF rhinorrhoea). Advanced Alzheimer's spreads from the entorhinal cortex to the hippocampus → memory loss, often preceded by poor smell identification.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → trigger (aminoglycoside, noise, head injury, Bell's palsy, neurodegeneration) → silent damage (OHC loss, OAE absent, entorhinal tangles) → partial deficit (high-frequency loss, hyperacusis, hyposmia) → full deficit (deafness, anosmia, dementia)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> OAE testing detects OHC damage before deafness is obvious and screens newborns; monitoring hearing during aminoglycoside therapy prevents permanent loss; asking about smell after head injury and in memory complaints catches cribriform fractures and early Alzheimer's.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Ototoxicity:** "Gentamicin / streptomycin / amikacin", "high-frequency hearing loss", "outer hair cells" → aminoglycosides kill OHCs first, starting at the base of the cochlea.
>
> **Hyperacusis:** "Bell's palsy", "sounds painfully loud on one side" → facial nerve to stapedius is paralysed; the stapedial (attenuation) reflex is lost.
>
> **Otoacoustic emissions:** "Newborn hearing screening", "Kemp echoes", "sensitive hearing test" → tests OHC function.
>
> **Anosmia after trauma:** "Head injury", "watery nasal discharge", "cribriform plate fracture" → olfactory neurons torn as they pass the cribriform plate.
>
> **Alzheimer's disease:** "Earliest change in the entorhinal cortex", "loss of smell with memory decline" → entorhinal cortex links smell to memory.
>
> **Taste receptors:** "T1R sweet", "ENaC salt", "T2R bitter", "mGluR4 umami" → each taste has its own receptor; umami is the taste of glutamate (ripe tomatoes).
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Three-chambered cochlear cross-section with Reissner's membrane above and basilar membrane below = scala media; a "W"/"V" row of stereocilia graded to the kinocilium = hair bundle; mitral-shaped neurons in the bulb = mitral cells.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Endolymphatic potential +80 mV; Brodmann 41, 42 (auditory); OHC > IHC in number, IHC > OHC in innervation; base = high frequency, apex = low frequency; tongue ant 2/3 (VII, chorda tympani), post 1/3 (IX), palate & pharynx (X).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "K⁺ leaving the hair cell depolarises it"**
>
> **What students think:** As in neurons, K⁺ efflux causes repolarisation, so K⁺ cannot depolarise.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The top of the hair cell is bathed in endolymph, which is K⁺-rich and +80 mV. When the bundle bends toward the kinocilium the mechanotransduction channels open and K⁺ flows **in**, depolarising the cell. K⁺ then leaves at the base into the K⁺-poor perilymph.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Endolymph = K⁺ in from the top; perilymph = Na⁺ like ECF."
>
> **Trap 2: Getting the basilar membrane backwards**
>
> **What students think:** Low frequencies are detected at the base because it is "broad".
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The base (near the oval window) is narrow and stiff and resonates with **high** frequencies; the apex (near the helicotrema) is wide and floppy and resonates with **low** frequencies. The page writes broad/narrow the other way round but its frequency rows are right — learn the frequency rule.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "High at the hatch (oval window), low at the loop (helicotrema)."
>
> **Trap 3: Mixing up OHC and IHC roles**
>
> **What students think:** Outer hair cells, being more numerous, send most of the auditory signal.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** OHCs are more numerous but **less innervated** — they act as the cochlear amplifier and are the source of OAEs; IHCs are fewer but **more innervated** and carry ~90–95 % of afferent fibres. OHCs are damaged first by drugs and noise.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Outer = more cells, more damage, OAE; Inner = more nerves, the real messenger."
>
> **Trap 4: Thinking smell must relay in the thalamus first**
>
> **What students think:** Like every sense, olfaction goes receptor → thalamus → cortex.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The olfactory bulb projects directly to the olfactory (piriform) and limbic cortex — entorhinal cortex and amygdala — without a thalamic relay; the thalamic (dorsomedian) route to the orbitofrontal cortex is a second, later branch for conscious discrimination.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Smell skips the switchboard."
>
> **Trap 5: Wrong nerve for the stapedial reflex or for taste**
>
> **What students think:** The trigeminal nerve is the efferent for the stapedius; the glossopharyngeal nerve carries taste from the anterior tongue.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The stapedius is supplied by the **facial** nerve (tensor tympani by the trigeminal), so facial palsy causes hyperacusis. Taste from the anterior 2/3 travels in the **chorda tympani (VII)**; the glossopharyngeal carries the posterior 1/3; the vagus the pharynx (and epiglottis).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "VII does the front of the tongue and the stapedius; IX the back; X the throat."

> [!tip]+ **🔗 Related Concepts & Cross-Topic Connections**
>

>

>
> **Related Topic 2: [[Vision]]**

>
> **Related Topic 3: [[Somatosensory Pathways]]**

>

> The olfactory bulb feeds the limbic cortex — entorhinal cortex (memory, Alzheimer's) and amygdala (emotion) — the reason smells trigger memories and feelings.
>
> **Related Topic 5: [[Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters]]**
> Glutamate (mitral and tufted cells) excites and GABA (granule and periglomerular cells) inhibits in the olfactory bulb; umami is sensed by a metabotropic glutamate receptor.
>
> **Related Topic 6: [[Membrane Potentials]]**
> The +80 mV endocochlear potential and the K⁺-rich endolymph explain why K⁺ influx depolarises the hair cell.
>

> Hearing screening (OAE), monitoring for aminoglycoside ototoxicity, testing the stapedial reflex in facial palsy, smell testing after head injury and in suspected Alzheimer's, and taste testing to localise lesions of VII, IX and X are the bedside uses of this chapter.
>

> 11. **Olfactory epithelium:** sustentacular (supportive), **bipolar olfactory neurons**, **basal (stem) cells — regeneration**, **Bowman's glands — mucus**; **cribriform plate fracture damages olfactory neurons**.
> 12. **Olfactory bulb:** **mitral & tufted (glutamate, excitatory)**; **granule & periglomerular (GABA, inhibitory)**. Limbic cortex → **entorhinal (memory)** + **amygdala (emotion)**; **orbitofrontal = conscious smell**; **entorhinal cortex = earliest degeneration in Alzheimer's**.
> 13. **Taste receptors:** sweet **T1R (GPCR)**, salt **ENaC**, sour **HCN**, bitter **T2R**, umami **mGluR4**. Cells: **I glial, II taste receptor, III presynaptic, IV basal (stem)**.
> 14. **Taste pathway:** ant 2/3 **chorda tympani (VII)**, post 1/3 **IX**, palate & pharynx **X** → **NTS (integration centre)** → **VPM** → taste centre (anterior nucleus/insula, **frontal operculum**).

[^1]: Endolymph: K⁺ ~150 mmol/L, Na⁺ ~1 mmol/L, +80 mV relative to perilymph (the endocochlear potential), generated by the stria vascularis. Perilymph resembles ECF/CSF: Na⁺ ~140, K⁺ ~5 mmol/L.
[^2]: About 3,500 inner hair cells and 12,000–20,000 outer hair cells per cochlea; ~90–95 % of the afferent fibres of the cochlear nerve come from IHCs. OHCs receive mainly efferent fibres (olivocochlear bundle) and act as the "cochlear amplifier" by changing length (electromotility, prestin).
[^3]: The hair cell's interior is about −60 mV and the endolymph +80 mV, so ~140 mV drives K⁺ inward through the mechano-electrical transduction channels at the tips of the stereocilia (opened by tip links). In the mature mammalian cochlea the kinocilium disappears after development; "toward the kinocilium" means toward the tallest stereocilia. Vestibular hair cells keep their kinocilium.
[^4]: Georg von Békésy's measurements: the basilar membrane widens from ~0.04 mm at the base to ~0.5 mm at the apex and becomes far less stiff, so high frequencies peak near the base and low frequencies near the apex (place theory / tonotopy). Noise and ototoxic drugs therefore affect high frequencies (base) first.
[^5]: Aminoglycosides are taken up into hair cells and generate free radicals; neomycin, amikacin and kanamycin are mainly cochleotoxic, streptomycin and gentamicin mainly vestibulotoxic. Cisplatin and high-dose loop diuretics are other ototoxic drugs.
[^6]: Otoacoustic emissions were discovered by David Kemp in 1978. Standard texts describe them as faint **sounds** produced by OHC electromotility and recorded by a microphone in the ear canal (spontaneous or evoked by clicks); their absence indicates OHC (cochlear) dysfunction. They are used for newborn hearing screening. The page's "K⁺ current measured in OHCs" is kept in the body.
[^7]: The medial superior olive compares the arrival time of sound at the two ears (low frequencies) and the lateral superior olive compares intensity (high frequencies); the inferior colliculus integrates these cues. From the superior olive upward each side receives input from both ears, so a unilateral lesion above the cochlear nuclei does not cause deafness in one ear.
[^8]: Area 41 = primary auditory cortex (transverse gyrus of Heschl); area 42 = secondary auditory cortex; Wernicke's area (posterior area 22) lies behind them in the planum temporale, which is usually larger on the left (language-dominant) side.
[^9]: The acoustic (attenuation) reflex contracts both the stapedius (facial nerve) and the tensor tympani (trigeminal nerve) after a latency of ~40–80 ms. Textbooks describe it as protecting the **cochlea** from damaging loud sounds, masking low-frequency sound and reducing sensitivity to one's own voice; because of its latency it cannot protect against sudden explosions. The page's "prevents rupture of tympanic membrane" is kept in the body.
[^10]: Olfactory receptor neurons (~10–20 million) are bipolar neurons replaced from basal cells every 1–2 months — the only neurons regularly replaced in adults. Their axons (fila olfactoria) pass through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid; a fracture tears them → anosmia, often with CSF rhinorrhoea. Odorant receptors are G-protein-coupled (G_olf → cAMP); Linda Buck and Richard Axel won the 2004 Nobel Prize for discovering them.
[^11]: Standard texts: the olfactory tract projects **directly** (no thalamic relay) to the primary olfactory (piriform) cortex, amygdala and entorhinal cortex; a second route via the **dorsomedial nucleus of the thalamus** reaches the **orbitofrontal cortex** for conscious discrimination of odours. The page's "dorsomedian nucleus" is the dorsomedial nucleus.
[^12]: Neurofibrillary tangles appear first in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex (Braak stages I–II) before spreading to the hippocampus and neocortex; impaired smell identification is an early clinical sign.
[^13]: Current texts identify the sour (H⁺) sensor of type III cells as the proton channel **OTOP1**; older texts named HCN and ASIC channels as candidates. The page's HCN is kept in the body.
[^14]: Sweet = T1R2 + T1R3 and umami = T1R1 + T1R3 (G-protein-coupled, gustducin); umami is also detected by taste-specific mGluR4 (and mGluR1). Bitter = the T2R family (~25 receptors), so a huge range of (often toxic) molecules taste bitter. Salt = amiloride-sensitive ENaC.
[^15]: Standard descriptions: type I cells are glia-like supporting cells; type II cells carry the sweet, bitter and umami receptors and release ATP; type III cells form true synapses, sense sour (and use serotonin); basal (type IV) cells are stem cells that renew taste-bud cells every ~10 days.
[^16]: In standard texts the palate is supplied by the greater petrosal branch of the facial nerve and the vagus carries taste from the epiglottis and lower pharynx; all three nerves end in the rostral (gustatory) part of the NTS.
[^17]: The page reads "Anterior nucleus"; standard texts name the primary gustatory cortex as the **anterior insula** and **frontal operculum**. The page's wording is kept in the body.

[^pre-pre]: **pre-** — before: presynaptic, preganglionic, prevent(ive). Don't confuse with **post-** after (postsynaptic) · **pro-** forward · **peri-** around.
[^pre-glosso]: **glosso- / gloss-** — tongue (Greek *glōssa*): glossopharyngeal, glossitis, hypoglossal. Don't confuse with **lingu-** tongue (Latin: lingual) · **gluco- / glyco-** sugar · **glotto-** the glottis (also from *glōtta*, tongue — epiglottis).
