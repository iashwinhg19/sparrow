# 💪 Skeletal Muscles

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Skeletal muscle[^ety-muscle][^ety-skeletal] is the voluntary, striated muscle whose **function is contraction**[^ety-contraction][^pre-con] **and relaxation**[^ety-relaxation][^pre-re]. Its **functional unit is the sarcomere**[^ety-sarcomere][^pre-sarco][^suf-mere] — the stretch between two Z lines where thin **actin** and thick **myosin** filaments slide over each other. The chapter answers:
> - ✅ **What is it built from?** — contractile (actin, myosin), supporting (desmin, titin, dystrophin), regulatory (troponin C, I, T; tropomyosin) and relaxation (SERCA) proteins
> - ✅ **How does the nerve switch it on?** — the neuromuscular junction: ACh on nicotinic receptors → end-plate potential → muscle action potential
> - ✅ **How does the electrical event become force?** — excitation–contraction coupling (DHPR–RyR, Ca²⁺ on troponin) and the **cross-bridge cycle** powered by ATP

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Rowing Crew Under a Safety Cover

Think of the myosin heads as **oars**, the actin filament as the **rope they pull**, and tropomyosin as a **safety cover** laid along the rope. Calcium is the **coxswain's call**: it binds troponin, which drags the cover aside, and the oars grab the rope (cross-bridge), pull (power stroke) and — only when a fresh ATP arrives — let go to grab again. When the call stops, SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into storage and the cover slides back: relaxation.

```
Motor neuron AP → Ca²⁺ in → ACh released → nicotinic AChR → Na⁺ in → END-PLATE POTENTIAL (graded)
   → muscle AP → T-tubule → DHPR (key) turns RyR (lock) → Ca²⁺ OUT of SR
   → Ca²⁺ + troponin C → tropomyosin moves → myosin-binding sites exposed
   → CROSS-BRIDGE → POWER STROKE (ADP + Pi released) → new ATP DETACHES head → cycle repeats
   → SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into SR → RELAXATION
SARCOMERE: Z ── I (actin) ── A (myosin, constant) ── H (no overlap) ── M ── … ── Z
CONTRACTION: A band constant · H zone and I band shorten · overlap ↑
```

### Mechanism of contraction

![[Skeletal Muscles - sliding filament mechanism of contraction.png]]
*Muscle relaxed (top) vs muscle contracted (bottom) — the sarcomere shortens with contraction: the A band stays constant, the H zone and I band both shorten, and actin & myosin overlap ↑ (cropped from the handwritten page; a watermark over the right-hand "half of I band" label was blanked).*

| On contraction | Change |
|---|---|
| **A band** | **Constant** |
| **H zone** | **Shortens** |
| **I band** | **Shortens** |
| **Sarcomere (Z to Z)** | **Shortens** |
| **Actin & myosin overlap** | **↑** |

> [!tip] 🧠 Why the A band never changes
> The A band *is* the myosin filament, and filaments do not shrink — they **slide**. Only the zones defined by the gap between filaments (I band, H zone) get smaller as actin is pulled towards the M line. "**A**lways the same = **A** band."[^4]

**Skeletal muscle:**

## 🔄 Muscle Contraction

### Sequence of events

![[Skeletal Muscles - excitation contraction coupling DHPR RyR.png]]
*Sequence of events — 1. action potential runs along the sarcolemma into the T-tubule; 2. conformational change (lock & key: DHPR = key on the T-tubule, RyR = lock on the sarcoplasmic reticulum); 3. Ca²⁺ efflux from the SR; 4. Ca²⁺ + troponin; 5. myosin head binds to actin; 6. muscle contraction. The SERCA pump takes Ca²⁺ back in (recycled) → muscle relaxation (cropped from the handwritten page).*

1. **Action potential.**
2. **Conformational**[^ety-conformational] **change (Lock & Key model).**
3. **Ca²⁺ efflux** (from the SR).
4. **Ca²⁺ + Troponin.**
5. **Myosin head binds to actin.**
6. **Muscle contraction.**

**Sarcoplasmic reticulum:** **Ca²⁺ storage.**
- **SERCA pump:** Ca²⁺ influx (back into the SR) → **muscle relaxation**; **Ca²⁺ recycled.**

**Ca²⁺ receptors:**
- **DHPR: Dihydropyridine**[^ety-dihydropyridine][^pre-di][^pre-hydro] **Receptor** (on the **T-tubule**[^ety-tubule] — the "key").
- **RyR: Ryanodine**[^ety-ryanodine] **Receptor** (on the SR — the "lock").
- **They have a mechanical interaction.**[^14]

```
AP along sarcolemma → down the T-tubule
   ↓
DHPR (voltage sensor, KEY) changes shape ──mechanical link──► RyR (LOCK) opens
   ↓
Ca²⁺ EFFLUX from SR → Ca²⁺ + troponin C → myosin head binds actin → CONTRACTION
   ↓ (stimulus stops)
SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into SR (recycled) → RELAXATION
```

### Applied aspect

**1. DHPR blockers:**
- **Calcium channel blockers** → Eg: **Nifedipine**[^ety-nifedipine].[^15]

**2. RyR blockers:**
- Eg: **Dantrolene**[^ety-dantrolene] **Sodium.**
- Used in **malignant**[^ety-malignant] **hyperthermia**[^ety-hyperthermia][^pre-hyper].
  - **MOA of malignant hyperthermia:** **overactive RyR (↑↑ Ca²⁺ release)** → **sustained muscle contraction** → **↑↑ body heat**.[^16]

**3. Brody's**[^ety-brody] **myopathy**[^ety-myopathy][^suf-pathy]**:**
- **SERCA pump:** coded by the **ATP2A1 gene**.
  - **ATP2A1 mutation**[^ety-mutation] → **affects muscle relaxation** → **muscle cramps following exercise**.[^17]

> [!warning] 🧠 One pattern behind the three diseases
> Every applied example is a fault in **Ca²⁺ handling**: block the sensor (DHPR) or the release channel (RyR) and contraction weakens; a **leaky RyR** floods the cell with Ca²⁺ (malignant hyperthermia); a **weak SERCA** cannot clear Ca²⁺, so the muscle cannot relax (Brody — cramps after exercise).

### Molecular mechanism — the cross-bridge cycle

![[Skeletal Muscles - cross bridge cycle.png]]
*Molecular mechanism — 1. binding of ATP to the myosin head (head has ATPase activity, 45°); 2. ATP hydrolysis (ADP + Pi, head cocked to 90°); Ca²⁺ → 3. cross-bridge formation (actin–myosin interaction); 4. power stroke — sliding of the myosin head → releases ADP + Pi; 5. detachment of the myosin head from actin triggered by a new molecule of ATP (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Step | Event | Myosin head |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Binding of ATP to myosin head** (head **has ATPase activity**) | 45° |
| **2** | **ATP hydrolysis**[^ety-hydrolysis][^suf-lysis] → ADP + Pi stay bound | Cocked to **90°** |
| — | **Ca²⁺** exposes the binding site on actin | — |
| **3** | **Cross bridge formation — actin–myosin interaction** | 90°, attached |
| **4** | **Power stroke:** sliding of myosin head → **releases ADP + Pi** | Swings back, pulling actin |
| **5** | **Detachment of myosin head from actin triggered by a new molecule of ATP** | Free again → step 1 |

```
1 ATP binds head (45°) ──► 2 ATP hydrolysis (ADP+Pi, 90°) ──Ca²⁺──► 3 cross-bridge (actin–myosin)
        ▲                                                                   │
        │                                                                   ▼
5 NEW ATP detaches head ◄────────────────────────────── 4 POWER STROKE (releases ADP + Pi)
```

- **Cross bridge cycling + Power stroke → enhanced muscle contraction.**[^18]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Skeletal muscle turns a **nerve impulse into force through calcium**. The neuromuscular junction converts the motor neuron's action potential into ACh, ACh into an end-plate potential and that into a muscle action potential; the T-tubule DHPR then **mechanically** opens the RyR, Ca²⁺ floods out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, binds troponin C and pulls tropomyosin off actin, and myosin heads cycle — **ATP is needed both to cock the head and to release it**. Relaxation is just as active: SERCA must pump Ca²⁺ back.
>
> So every disease and drug in the chapter is a fault at one link of that chain: the receptor (myasthenia gravis), the presynaptic Ca²⁺ channel (Lambert–Eaton), Na⁺/K⁺/Ca²⁺ channels (tetrodotoxin, dendrotoxin, conotoxin), the RyR (malignant hyperthermia, dantrolene), SERCA (Brody myopathy) or the scaffold that keeps the membrane anchored to actin (dystrophin — Duchenne).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Each motor neuron AP releases enough ACh to produce an end-plate potential well above threshold (a large safety margin), every fibre of the motor unit contracts, and SERCA clears Ca²⁺ within milliseconds so the muscle relaxes.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> When the safety margin shrinks — fewer nicotinic receptors (myasthenia gravis) or less ACh release (Lambert–Eaton) — single contractions are normal but repeated ones fail: fatigable weakness in MG, weakness that improves with repetition in LEMS. In Duchenne dystrophy, membranes tear with use while regeneration keeps pace for the first years; serum CK is already very high.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Myasthenic crisis or DMD reaches the respiratory muscles → respiratory paralysis → death. In malignant hyperthermia a triggering anaesthetic opens defective RyRs, Ca²⁺ floods the cytosol → sustained contraction (rigidity), hypermetabolism, ↑↑ body heat, hyperkalaemia and rhabdomyolysis.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → defect at one link (antibody, toxin, gene mutation, anaesthetic trigger) → reduced safety margin / Ca²⁺ mishandling → compensation (more ACh, regeneration) → failure under load → weakness, paralysis, rigidity or hyperthermia
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Locating the faulty link decides treatment: cholinesterase inhibitors and immunotherapy for MG, tumour search and 3,4-diaminopyridine for LEMS, dantrolene and stopping the trigger for malignant hyperthermia, steroids and respiratory support for DMD.

>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Myasthenia gravis:** "Autoantibodies against nicotinic ACh receptors", "postsynaptic", "ptosis and diplopia worse in the evening" → fatigable weakness.
>
> **Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome:** "Autoantibodies against voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels", "presynaptic", "small-cell lung cancer", "strength improves after brief exercise".
>
> **Duchenne muscular dystrophy:** "Dystrophin gene mutation", "boy with calf pseudohypertrophy, Gowers sign", "respiratory muscle paralysis → death".
>
> **Malignant hyperthermia:** "Overactive RyR", "after halothane / succinylcholine", "rigidity, ↑↑ body heat" → treat with dantrolene sodium.
>
> **Brody myopathy:** "ATP2A1 / SERCA mutation", "muscle cramps / stiffness after exercise", "slow relaxation".
>
> **Toxins:** "Pufferfish" → tetrodotoxin (Na⁺ channel); "black mamba" → dendrotoxin (K⁺ channel); "cone snail" → conotoxin (Ca²⁺ channel).
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Under the microscope the A band (dark, myosin) stays the same length while the I band and H zone shrink during contraction; a desmin-positive tumour = a muscle-derived sarcoma.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> RMP −70 mV; presynaptic terminal depolarises to about −40 mV before Ca²⁺ channels open; thick filament 1.6 µm; myosin = 2 heavy + 4 light chains; respiratory muscle involvement in DMD or MG marks the life-threatening stage.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "The A band shortens during contraction"**
>
> **What students think:** All bands shorten when the sarcomere shortens.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Filaments slide; they do not shorten. The A band equals the length of the myosin filament (1.6 µm), so it is constant. The I band (actin-only zone) and the H zone (myosin-only zone) shrink because overlap increases.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "A band = Always the same; H and I hide."
>
> **Trap 2: ATP is needed only for the power stroke**
>
> **What students think:** ATP powers the stroke; its absence would make muscle limp.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** On the page, the power stroke releases ADP + Pi; it is the **binding of a new ATP that detaches** the head from actin. Without ATP the heads stay locked on actin — rigor (rigor mortis after death). ATP is also needed by SERCA for relaxation.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "ATP lets go" — no ATP, no release.
>
> **Trap 3: Myasthenia gravis vs Lambert–Eaton sites**
>
> **What students think:** Both are postsynaptic receptor diseases.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** MG antibodies attack the **postsynaptic** nicotinic ACh receptor; LEMS antibodies attack the **presynaptic** voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel, so less ACh is released. Repetition worsens MG but improves LEMS (Ca²⁺ builds up in the terminal).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**L**EMS = **L**aunching side (pre); MG = **M**uscle side (post)."
>
> **Trap 4: Calcium channel blockers weaken skeletal muscle**
>
> **What students think:** Since DHPR is the dihydropyridine receptor, nifedipine should paralyse skeletal muscle.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In skeletal muscle DHPR works as a **voltage sensor mechanically coupled to RyR**; Ca²⁺ entry through it is not needed for contraction. Clinically, dihydropyridines act on L-type channels of vascular smooth muscle (and the heart, where Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release does need entry).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Skeletal = mechanical coupling (lock & key); cardiac = Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release.
>
> **Trap 5: Which troponin does what**
>
> **What students think:** Troponin I binds Ca²⁺ ("I for ion").
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Troponin **C** binds **C**alcium; troponin **I** is **I**nhibitory (holds the complex in the blocking position); troponin **T** binds **T**ropomyosin.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** C = Calcium, I = Inhibitory, T = Tropomyosin.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Muscle function = **contraction & relaxation**; coverings: **epimysium** (whole muscle) · **perimysium** (fascicle) · **endomysium** (fibre); fibre membrane = **sarcolemma**.
> 2. **Sarcomere = functional unit = area between 2 Z lines.**
> 3. Actin = **thin filament (G-actin)**; myosin = **thick filament, 2 heavy + 4 light chains, 1.6 µm fixed length, head has ATPase activity**.
> 4. **A band = myosin**, **I band = actin**, **H band = no overlap**, **Z line = boundary**, M line = middle.
> 5. **Titin** Z → M line, spring, elastic support, **giant/largest protein** · **nebulin = molecular ruler** · **α-actinin → actin to Z** · **myomesin → myosin to M**.
> 6. On contraction: **A band constant; H zone and I band shorten; overlap ↑**.
> 7. Protein types: contractile (actin, myosin) · supporting (**desmin, titin, dystrophin**) · regulatory (**troponin C, I, T**) · relaxation (**SERCA**).
> 8. **Desmin** = intermediate filament, **tumour marker for sarcomas**, Z line → sarcolemma; **dystrophin** = links sarcolemma to actin → mutation = **Duchenne muscular dystrophy** (respiratory paralysis → death).
> 9. **Troponin C binds Ca²⁺, I inhibitory, T binds tropomyosin**; tropomyosin covers myosin-binding sites on actin.
> 10. NMJ: AP → Na⁺ in/K⁺ out → −40 mV → **VG Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx → ACh exocytosis → nicotinic receptor → Na⁺ influx → end-plate potential (graded) → threshold → muscle AP**.
> 11. **Tetrodotoxin (pufferfish) · dendrotoxin (black mamba) · conotoxin (snails)**; **MG = anti-nicotinic AChR, postsynaptic; LEMS = anti-VG Ca²⁺ channel, presynaptic**; MEPP = spontaneous (quantal) ACh release.
> 12. E–C coupling: **DHPR (T-tubule, key) mechanically opens RyR (SR, lock)** → Ca²⁺ efflux → troponin → contraction; **SERCA → relaxation**.
> 13. **DHPR blocker = nifedipine; RyR blocker = dantrolene → malignant hyperthermia (overactive RyR); Brody myopathy = ATP2A1 (SERCA) mutation → cramps after exercise**; cross-bridge cycle: ATP binds → hydrolysis (90°) → cross-bridge → **power stroke releases ADP + Pi** → **new ATP detaches**.

[^1]: Skeletal muscle is striated, voluntary and multinucleated; cardiac muscle is striated, involuntary and joined by intercalated discs; smooth muscle is non-striated and involuntary — see [[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles]].
[^2]: Each muscle fibre is a single multinucleated cell; its plasma membrane (sarcolemma) is covered externally by a basal lamina, and it dips into the fibre as the T-tubules. Inside, the fibre is packed with myofibrils, each a chain of sarcomeres.
[^3]: Myosin II is a hexamer (~480 kDa): two heavy chains, whose tails coil together and whose globular heads carry the ATPase and actin-binding sites, plus two pairs of light chains (essential and regulatory) on the neck. A thick filament holds ~200–300 myosin molecules; a thin filament is a double helix of F-actin (polymerised G-actin) with tropomyosin and troponin, about 1 µm long.
[^4]: The sliding-filament theory was proposed in 1954 by two independent pairs — A. F. Huxley & R. Niedergerke and H. E. Huxley & J. Hanson — from exactly this observation: the A band keeps its length while the I band and H zone narrow.
[^5]: SERCA is not a channel but a **P-type Ca²⁺-ATPase pump**: it uses one ATP to move two Ca²⁺ ions from the cytosol back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum against a steep gradient. That is why relaxation, like contraction, needs ATP. The page's "Ca²⁺ channel protein" is kept in the body.
[^6]: Desmin is the type III intermediate filament of muscle; it links neighbouring Z discs to each other and to the sarcolemma (at costameres) and to the nucleus. Immunostaining for desmin identifies tumours of muscle origin — rhabdomyosarcoma and leiomyosarcoma.
[^7]: The dystrophin gene (Xp21) is the largest human gene; DMD is X-linked recessive, usually from frameshift deletions that abolish the protein. Boys present at 3–5 years with proximal weakness, Gowers sign, calf pseudohypertrophy and very high CK; death typically comes in the second or third decade from respiratory failure or dilated cardiomyopathy. In-frame deletions leave a shorter, partly working dystrophin — the milder Becker dystrophy.
[^8]: Troponin I binds actin and holds tropomyosin over the myosin-binding sites; when Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, the complex shifts and tropomyosin rolls deeper into the actin groove. Cardiac isoforms of troponin I and T are the standard blood markers of myocardial injury.
[^9]: The term *excitation–contraction coupling* was introduced by Alexander Sandow in 1952. In skeletal muscle the link is the T-tubule–SR "triad" (one T-tubule between two terminal cisternae).
[^10]: Motor-unit size matches the precision needed: a few muscle fibres per neuron in the extraocular and laryngeal muscles, a thousand or more in large limb muscles such as the gastrocnemius. Fibres of one unit are scattered among those of other units, as the page's colour-coded drawing shows; force is graded by recruiting more units.
[^11]: "Drops to −40 mV" means the membrane becomes **less negative** (depolarises) from −70 towards −40 mV — the voltage at which the presynaptic voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (P/Q type) open.
[^12]: Tetrodotoxin (pufferfish / fugu) blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels → no action potentials → paralysis and respiratory failure. Dendrotoxins (mamba venom) block voltage-gated K⁺ channels (Kv1) → prolonged presynaptic depolarisation → excess ACh release and hyperexcitability. ω-Conotoxins (*Conus* cone snails) block N-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels (a synthetic ω-conotoxin, ziconotide, is used as an analgesic); other conotoxins (α-) block nicotinic receptors. Other classic NMJ blockers: botulinum toxin (stops ACh release), α-bungarotoxin and curare (block the nicotinic receptor).
[^13]: Fatt and Katz (1952) recorded these ~0.5 mV potentials at the resting end-plate; each is produced by one vesicle ("quantum") of ACh released spontaneously — the basis of the quantal theory of transmitter release. The page's note line ends with a colon and nothing after it.
[^14]: The DHPR of skeletal muscle (Cav1.1, an L-type Ca²⁺ channel) acts mainly as a **voltage sensor**: depolarisation changes its shape and, by direct protein–protein contact, opens RyR1 on the terminal cisterna — Ca²⁺ entry is not needed. In cardiac muscle Ca²⁺ entering through DHPR triggers RyR2 (Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release).
[^15]: Nifedipine and other dihydropyridines block L-type Ca²⁺ channels of vascular smooth muscle (antihypertensive, anti-anginal). Because skeletal E–C coupling is mechanical, therapeutic doses do not weaken skeletal muscle.
[^16]: Malignant hyperthermia is an autosomal dominant pharmacogenetic disorder (mostly *RYR1*, occasionally *CACNA1S* — the DHPR gene) triggered by volatile anaesthetics (halothane, sevoflurane) and succinylcholine. Early signs: rising end-tidal CO₂, tachycardia, masseter or generalised rigidity; later hyperthermia, hyperkalaemia, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis. Dantrolene (2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated) blocks Ca²⁺ release through RyR1.
[^17]: Brody disease (Irwin Brody, 1969) is usually autosomal recessive: loss of SERCA1 — the fast-twitch-fibre isoform coded by *ATP2A1* — slows Ca²⁺ re-uptake, so muscles stiffen and cramp during and after exercise and relax slowly; unlike myotonia, the EMG is electrically silent.
[^18]: Each cycle moves the thin filament about 10 nm and uses one ATP. Without ATP, heads stay bound in the rigor state — the basis of rigor mortis. Release of Pi is the step that triggers the power stroke; ADP leaves at its end.

[^ety-skeletal]: **skeletal** — Greek *skeletos*, "dried up" (*skeletos sōma*, a dried body, a mummy) + *-al* → belonging to the skeleton; skeletal muscle moves the bones.

[^ety-tubule]: **T-tubule** — *T* for **transverse** (Latin *trans-*, "across" + *vertere*, "to turn") + Latin *tubulus*, "small tube" → tubes of sarcolemma running across the fibre to carry the action potential inside.
[^ety-ryanodine]: **ryanodine** — an alkaloid from the South American plant *Ryania speciosa* that binds this Ca²⁺-release channel with high affinity — hence "ryanodine receptor".
[^ety-nifedipine]: **nifedipine** — a coined drug name; its *-dipine* stem (from dihydro**pyridine**) marks the dihydropyridine Ca²⁺-channel blockers (amlodipine, nimodipine).
[^ety-dantrolene]: **dantrolene** — a coined drug name for a hydantoin derivative (the *-dant-* echoes hydantoin); it acts on RyR1 to stop Ca²⁺ release — the specific antidote for malignant hyperthermia.
[^ety-malignant]: **malignant** — Latin *malignus*, "evil-natured, wicked" (*malus*, bad + *-gnus*, born) → tending to get worse and kill; *malignant hyperthermia* is a lethal, rapidly escalating hyperthermia.
[^ety-hyperthermia]: **hyperthermia** — Greek *hyper-*, "over" + *thermē*, "heat" + *-ia* → excess body heat because heat production exceeds loss, with a normal hypothalamic set-point. **Differentiate:** *hyperthermia* (set-point normal — malignant hyperthermia, heat stroke) vs *fever / pyrexia* (set-point raised).
[^ety-brody]: **Brody's myopathy** — Irwin A. Brody, American neurologist, who described in 1969 a patient whose muscles relaxed slowly after exercise because of defective SR Ca²⁺ uptake.
[^ety-myopathy]: **myopathy** — Greek *mys*, "muscle" + *pathos*, "suffering, disease" + *-y* → a primary disease of the muscle fibres (vs *neuropathy*, disease of nerves).
[^ety-mutation]: **mutation** — Latin *mutare*, "to change" + *-ation* → a change in the DNA sequence. **Differentiate:** *mutation* (change in the gene) vs *polymorphism* (a common, usually harmless variant).
[^ety-hydrolysis]: **hydrolysis** — Greek *hydōr*, "water" + *lysis*, "loosening, splitting" → splitting a bond by adding water; ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi.
[^pre-con]: **con- / com- / co-** — together, with: contraction, conformational, coupling. Don't confuse with **contra-** against · **counter-** opposite.
[^pre-re]: **re-** — back, again: relaxation, regulatory, recycled. Don't confuse with **retro-** backwards, behind · **pre-** before.
[^pre-sarco]: **sarco-** — flesh, muscle: sarcomere, sarcolemma, sarcoplasmic, sarcoma. Don't confuse with **sacro-** the sacrum (sacroiliac) · **carcino-** cancer of epithelium.
[^suf-mere]: **-mere / -mer** — a part, a unit: sarcomere, polymer, centromere. Don't confuse with **-meter** a measure · **-mery** division into parts (metamerism).
[^suf-cardia]: **-cardia / cardio-** — heart: cardiac, tachycardia, myocardium. Don't confuse with the stomach's **cardia** (the part next to the heart) · **-cardium** a layer of the heart.
[^pre-epi]: **epi-** — upon, over: epimysium, epineurium, epithelium. Don't confuse with **endo-** within · **peri-** around · **hypo-** under.
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: perimysium, perineurium, pericardium. Don't confuse with **para-** beside · **pre-** before · **epi-** upon.
[^pre-endo]: **endo-** — within: endomysium, endoplasmic, endocytosis. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside · **ecto-** outer · **epi-** upon.
[^suf-lemma]: **-lemma** — husk, sheath, membrane: sarcolemma, neurilemma (Schwann-cell sheath), axolemma. Don't confuse with **lemma** in logic (a proposition) · **-lamina** a thin plate (basal lamina).
[^pre-myo]: **myo- / my-** — muscle: myosin, myomesin, myopathy, myasthenia. Don't confuse with **myelo-** marrow, spinal cord (myelin) · **myco-** fungus · **myo-** in *myopia* (Greek *myein*, to close the eyes).
[^suf-ase]: **-ase** — an enzyme: ATPase, acetylcholinesterase, synthase. Don't confuse with **-ose** a sugar (glucose) · **-ate** a salt or ester (glutamate).
[^pre-an]: **an- / a-** — not, without: anisotropic (not equal), anaemia, atrophy. Don't confuse with **ana-** up, again (anabolism) · **ad- (a-)** towards · **ante-** before.
[^pre-iso]: **iso-** — equal: isotropic, isometric, isotonic. Don't confuse with **homo-** same · **equi-** equal (Latin twin).
[^suf-plasm]: **-plasm / -plasma** — formed substance: sarcoplasm, cytoplasm, protoplasm. Don't confuse with **-plasia** formation of tissue (hyperplasia) · **-plasty** surgical repair.
[^suf-oma]: **-oma** — a tumour or swelling: sarcoma, carcinoma, lipoma. Don't confuse with **-osis** a condition · **stoma** an opening (*-stomy*) · **coma** (deep unconsciousness — not a suffix).
[^pre-dys]: **dys-** — bad, faulty, difficult: dystrophin, dystrophy, dyspnoea. Don't confuse with **dis-** apart, reversal (dissociation) · **de-** removal.
[^suf-trophy]: **-trophy / -trophic** — nourishment, growth: dystrophy, atrophy, hypertrophy. Don't confuse with **-tropic / -tropin** turning towards, stimulating (gonadotropin) · **-plasia** formation of cells.
[^pre-glyco]: **glyco- / gluco-** — sugar, sweet: glycoprotein, glycogen, glucose. Don't confuse with **glia** glue · **glyc-** in *glycine* (the sweet-tasting amino acid — same root).
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (e-, ef-)** — out of: excitation, efflux, efferent. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek) · **extra-** beyond.
[^pre-exo]: **exo-** — outside, outward (Greek): exocytosis, exocrine, exogenous. Don't confuse with **ex-** out of (Latin) · **ecto-** outer · **endo-** within.
[^pre-syn]: **syn- / sym-** — together: synaptic, syndrome, syntrophin. Don't confuse with **sin-** left · **semi-** half.
[^pre-tetra]: **tetra-** — four (Greek): tetrodotoxin, tetralogy, tetraplegia. Don't confuse with **tetanus** (Greek *tetanos*, rigid — not a number) · **quadri-** four (Latin twin).
[^pre-a]: **a- / an-** — not, without: myasthenia (without strength), atrophy, anoxia. Don't confuse with **ab-** away · **ad-** towards · **ana-** up.
[^pre-post]: **post-** — after, behind: postsynaptic, postganglionic, postpartum. Don't confuse with **pre-** before · **posterior** the back side.
[^pre-pre]: **pre-** — before, in front: presynaptic, preganglionic, prevertebral. Don't confuse with **pro-** forward · **peri-** around · **para-** beside.
[^pre-di]: **di-** — two, twice (Greek): dihydropyridine, dipeptide, diplopia. Don't confuse with **dia-** through (dialysis) · **dis-** apart · **dys-** bad.
[^pre-hydro]: **hydro-** — water, hydrogen: dihydropyridine, hydrolysis, hydrocephalus. Don't confuse with **hygro-** moisture · **hydra-** in *hydatid* (a cyst, *hydatis* water drop).
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, excessive: hyperthermia, hyperkalaemia, hypertrophy. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under · **supra- / super-** above (Latin twins).
[^suf-pathy]: **-pathy** — disease, suffering: myopathy, neuropathy, cardiomyopathy. Don't confuse with **-path** a practitioner (osteopath) · **-pathic** in *idiopathic* (disease of unknown cause) · **sympathy** (feeling with).
[^suf-lysis]: **-lysis** — loosening, splitting, dissolution: hydrolysis, paralysis, glycolysis. Don't confuse with **-stasis** standing still · **-ptosis** falling, drooping.
