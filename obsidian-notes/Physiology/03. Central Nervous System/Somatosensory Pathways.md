# 🧭 Somatosensory Pathways

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Somatosensory**[^ety-somatosensory][^pre-somato] pathways carry the **general sensations** of the body (touch, pressure, vibration, joint position, pain, temperature, itch) from the skin through the **spinal cord** and **thalamus**[^ety-thalamus] to the **sensory cortex**[^ety-cortex]. Everything in the chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **Which pathway carries which sensation** — dorsal column medial lemniscal (DCML) vs anterolateral (spinothalamic)?
> - ✅ **Where does each pathway cross** — medulla vs spinal cord — and so which side is lost in a lesion (Brown-Séquard syndrome)?
> - ✅ **How does the brain read the signal** — thalamic relay, cortical map (homunculus) and the laws of sensory physiology?

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Two Highways, One Relay Station, One Map

Picture sensation travelling on **two roads**. The **DCML** is the new express highway: thick myelinated fibres, fast, carrying the "precision" sensations (where my joints are, fine touch, vibration, what object is in my hand); it climbs on the **same side** and only **crosses in the medulla**. The **anterolateral pathway** is the old country road: thin slow fibres carrying the "protective" sensations (pain, temperature, crude touch, pressure, itch); it **crosses at once in the spinal cord**. Both stop at the **thalamus** (relay, 3rd order neuron) and end on a **map** in the postcentral gyrus (4th order) — the homunculus.

```
RECEPTOR → 1st order (dorsal root ganglion) → 2nd order (cord / medulla) → 3rd order (THALAMUS, VPL) → 4th order (CORTEX, areas 3,1,2)

DCML  = NEW · myelinated · FAST · proprioception, fine touch, vibration, stereognosis · crosses in MEDULLA
AL    = OLD · unmyelinated · SLOW · pain, temperature, crude touch, pressure, itch     · crosses in SPINAL CORD

HEMISECTION (Brown-Séquard): DCML sensations + motor lost on the SAME side · pain + temperature lost on the OPPOSITE side
```

## 🪜 Levels of Control for General Sensations

The page opens with the four levels a general sensation passes through:

```
Skin
  ↓
Spinal cord
  ↓
Thalamus
  ↓
Sensory cortex
```

- Each level hands the signal to a new **neuron**[^ety-neuron]: **receptor → 1st order neuron** (enters the **spinal cord**[^ety-spinal][^suf-al]) → **2nd order neuron** (crosses, ascends through the brainstem) → **3rd order neuron** (thalamus) → **4th order neuron** (cerebral cortex).[^1]
- 🧠 In plain words: four neurons in a relay race — the baton (the impulse) changes hands at the cord or medulla, at the thalamus and at the cortex.

## 🛤️ Spinothalamic Pathways

**From spinal cord to thalamus**[^ety-spinothalamic][^pre-spino].

### Pathways

1. **Posterior column pathway** (Dorsal column spinothalamic pathway).[^2]
2. **Anterolateral**[^ety-anterolateral][^pre-antero] **spinothalamic pathway**.
3. **Spinocerebellar**[^ety-spinocerebellar][^suf-ar] **tract**.[^3]

![Spinal cord cross-section with the ascending and descending tracts labelled (Wikimedia Commons)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b2/Spinal_cord_tracts_-_English.svg/800px-Spinal_cord_tracts_-_English.svg.png)
*Somatosensory pathway in the spinal cord — posterior column (fasciculus gracilis medially, fasciculus cuneatus laterally), posterior and anterior spinocerebellar tracts, lateral and anterior spinothalamic tracts; the descending (motor) tracts are on the same diagram (Polarlys / Selket / Mikael Häggström, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA).*

### Dorsal column medial lemniscal pathway vs anterolateral pathway

| Feature | **Dorsal column medial lemniscal**[^ety-lemniscus] **pathway** | **Anterolateral pathway** |
|---|---|---|
| **Myelination**[^ety-myelination][^suf-ation] | **Myelinated (majority)** | **Unmyelinated**[^pre-un] |
| **According to evolution**[^ety-evolution] | **New** | **Old** |
| **Conduction**[^ety-conduction][^pre-con] **speed** | **Fast** | **Slow**[^4] |
| **Sensations** | • **Conscious proprioception**[^ety-proprioception][^pre-proprio] (joint position sense) <br>• **Fine touch** <br>• **Vibration**[^ety-vibration] <br>• **Stereognosis**[^ety-stereognosis][^pre-stereo][^suf-gnosis] (ability to identify objects by touch on eyes closed) | • **Lateral pathway: Pain** <br>• **Anterior pathway:** – crude touch – pressure <br>• **Temperature** <br>• **Itch** |
| **Level of decussation**[^ety-decussation] | **Medulla**[^ety-medulla] | **Spinal cord** |

> [!tip] Why the crossing level matters
> The DCML climbs on the **same side** as the stimulus and crosses **high up (medulla)**; the anterolateral pathway crosses **immediately (spinal cord)**. So below the medulla the two pathways for one half of the body run on **opposite sides** of the cord — the whole basis of Brown-Séquard syndrome.[^5]

### Pathway — course of the two tracts

| Step | DCML (sensation from right side of body) | Anterolateral (sensation from right side of body) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st order neuron | **Dorsal root ganglion**[^ety-ganglion] → ascends on the **same side** in the **fasciculus gracilis / fasciculus cuneatus** | **Dorsal root ganglion** → synapses in the dorsal horn |
| Crossing | 2nd order neuron from **nucleus gracilis / nucleus cuneatus** crosses in the **medulla** → **medial lemniscus** | 2nd order neuron **crosses over in the spinal cord** → **anterolateral ST tract** |
| Brainstem | Medial lemniscus through **medulla** | Anterolateral ST tract through **medulla** |
| 3rd order neuron | **Thalamus** | **Thalamus** |
| Ends in | **Sensory cortex** | **Sensory cortex** |

**DCML — which fasciculus for which limb:**
- **Lower limb:** **Gracile fasciculus**[^ety-gracilis] → **Nucleus gracilis**.
- **Upper limb:** **Cuneate fasciculus**[^ety-cuneatus] → **Nucleus cuneatus**.

### Somatotopic arrangement

**C: Cervical · T: Thoracic · L: Lumbar · S: Sacral** — the order of body segments in each tract, from **medial to lateral**:

| | **Dorsal column medial lemniscal pathway** | **Anterolateral pathway** |
|---|---|---|
| Somatotopic[^ety-somatotopic][^suf-ic] arrangement (medial → lateral) | **(medial) S L T C (lateral)** | **(medial) C T L S (lateral)** |

- 🧠 DCML: the sacral fibres enter first and are pushed **medially** by each new segment that joins laterally, so the cervical (arm) fibres end up **lateral** (cuneatus).
- 🧠 Anterolateral: fibres cross and join from the medial side, so the newest (cervical) fibres are **medial** and sacral fibres **lateral** — the reverse order.[^6]

![[Somatosensory Pathways - somatotopic arrangement SLTC and CTLS.png]]
*Somatotopic arrangement — dorsal columns SLTC (medial → lateral), anterolateral tract CTLS (medial → lateral); white matter, grey matter, dorsal, lateral and ventral horns labelled (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### 🩺 Applied aspect — Brown-Séquard syndrome[^ety-brown-sequard]

**Brown-Séquard syndrome: Hemisection**[^ety-hemisection][^pre-hemi] **of spinal cord** — injury to one half of the spinal cord.

![[Somatosensory Pathways - Brown-Sequard hemisection.png]]
*Brown-Séquard syndrome — injury to one half of the spinal cord (cropped from the handwritten page).*

#### Clinical manifestations

**Ipsilateral**[^ety-ipsilateral][^pre-ipsi]:
- **Loss of dorsal column sensations:**
  - Proprioception.
  - Vibration.
  - Fine touch.
- **Motor paralysis**[^ety-paralysis][^suf-lysis].

**Contralateral**[^pre-contra]:
- **Loss of anterolateral column sensations:**
  - **Pain.**
  - **Temperature.**

> [!info] Reading the lesion
> Same side = the tracts that have **not yet crossed** at that level (dorsal columns, lateral corticospinal tract). Opposite side = the tract that **already crossed** below (lateral spinothalamic). Pain and temperature loss starts one or two segments below the lesion.[^7]

*Brown-Séquard syndrome — ipsilateral motor loss and loss of proprioception, fine touch, vibratory sense and two-point discrimination; contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation (cropped from the handwritten page).*

*Cross-section of a lateral hemisection explaining why proprioception and motor power are lost on the side of the lesion and pain/temperature on the opposite side (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA).*

## 🔀 Thalamus

- **Hallmark: Sensory relay station.**
- **Order of neurons:** **3rd order**.
- **Nuclei:** **Ventropostero-lateral (VPL)**[^ety-vpl][^pre-ventro], for **general senses: touch, pain, temperature**.[^8]
- **Clinical significance:**

> [!danger] **Dejerine-Roussy syndrome**[^ety-dejerine-roussy] (Thalamic pain syndrome)
> - **Pathology:** **Stroke (infarction**[^ety-infarction][^pre-in]**) → nerve fibres in VPL nuclei are irritable → ↑ neuronal signalling.**
> - **Symptom:** **Excruciating**[^ety-excruciating][^pre-ex] **pain.**[^9]

## ⚖️ Laws in Sensory Physiology

### Bell–Magendie law[^ety-bell-magendie]
- **Spinal dorsal root: Sensory.**
- **Spinal ventral root: Motor.**

![[Somatosensory Pathways - Bell-Magendie dorsal and ventral roots.png]]
*Spinal cord — dorsal root (sensory) with its dorsal root ganglion and ventral root (motor): the Bell–Magendie law (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Law of specific nerve energies
- AKA **Muller's Doctrine**[^ety-muller].
- **Sensory receptors**[^ety-receptor][^suf-tor] **detect specific sensations.**
- **Eg:**
  - Pain receptors: **Pain**.
  - Touch receptors: **Touch**.
  - Cold temperature receptors: **Cold**.[^11]

![[Somatosensory Pathways - sensory receptors in skin.png]]
*Sensory receptors in the skin — pain receptor, cold temperature receptor, touch receptors, nerve fibres, hair, epidermis and dermis (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Law of projection[^ety-projection]
- **On receptor stimulation, the response of the cerebral cortex is projected to the lowest receptor level.**
- 🧠 Wherever along the pathway the fibre is stimulated, the brain feels the sensation **at the receptor's home** — hit the ulnar nerve at the elbow and the tingle is felt in the little finger.[^12]

![[Somatosensory Pathways - law of projection pathway.png]]
*Projection pathway — the cortex projects the sensation back to the level of the receptor (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Cortical plasticity[^ety-plasticity][^suf-ity]
- **Reorganization of cortical representation by adjacent areas following injury.**
- **Clinical significance:** **Phantom**[^ety-phantom] **limb pain.**
- **Eg:**

```
Hand amputation
      ↓
Cortical takeover by arm (the arm area spreads into the silent hand area)
      ↓
Arm pain
      ↓
Perceived as hand pain
```

- 🧠 After **amputation**[^ety-amputation] the hand area of the cortex is not left idle; the neighbouring arm area takes it over, so a signal from the arm stump is read partly as "hand".[^13]

### Weber–Fechner law[^ety-weber-fechner]
- **Magnitude of sensation felt is directly proportional to log**[^ety-logarithm] **intensity of initial stimulus.**
- **Eg:**
  - **1 kg + 10 g → ↑ in weight not perceived.**
  - **1 kg + 100 g → ↑ in weight perceived.**
- 🧠 The brain notices a **fraction** of what is already there, not a fixed amount: 10 g is 1 % of 1 kg (missed), 100 g is 10 % (noticed).[^14]

![[Somatosensory Pathways - Weber-Fechner law weights.png]]
*Weber–Fechner law — 1 kg + 10 g: increase in weight not perceived; 1 kg + 100 g: increase in weight perceived (cropped from the handwritten page).*

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> General sensation is carried by **two parallel chains of four neurons** that differ in **what they carry, how fast and where they cross**. The DCML (myelinated, new, fast) carries the discriminative sensations — conscious proprioception, fine touch, vibration, stereognosis — and stays **ipsilateral until the medulla**; the anterolateral pathway (unmyelinated, old, slow) carries pain, temperature, crude touch, pressure and itch and **crosses at once in the spinal cord**. Both relay in the **thalamus (VPL, 3rd order)** and end on the **postcentral gyrus (areas 3, 1, 2, 4th order)**, where the body is mapped by **use, not size**.
>
> Once you know the crossing level, every lesion can be predicted: a hemisection leaves the uncrossed systems (dorsal columns, corticospinal) failing on the **same side** and the crossed one (spinothalamic) failing on the **opposite side**; a thalamic infarct gives contralateral sensory loss and later thalamic pain.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> A stimulus at a receptor follows the law of specific nerve energies (each receptor reports its own modality) and the law of projection (the sensation is felt at the receptor). DCML and anterolateral signals cross at their own levels, relay in the VPL and reach areas 3, 1, 2, where the homunculus localises them; Weber–Fechner sets how big a change must be to be noticed.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A partial lesion of one tract gives a dissociated sensory loss — only proprioception/vibration (dorsal columns) or only pain/temperature (spinothalamic). Cortical plasticity begins to remap a deafferented area; the patient may compensate for lost proprioception with vision (steady with eyes open).
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Hemisection produces the full Brown-Séquard picture: ipsilateral loss of proprioception, vibration, fine touch and motor paralysis, contralateral loss of pain and temperature. A VPL infarct gives contralateral hemi-anaesthesia followed by excruciating thalamic pain (Dejerine-Roussy); after amputation, cortical takeover produces phantom limb pain.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → injury (trauma, stab wound, tumour, stroke, amputation) → loss in one tract (dissociated loss) → hemisection / thalamic infarct → full syndrome → cortical reorganisation (phantom or thalamic pain)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The side and the modality of loss localise the lesion: same-side proprioceptive loss with opposite-side pain loss means a cord hemisection; all modalities lost on one side of the body means a thalamic or cortical lesion. Pain that persists after the stimulus is gone (phantom, thalamic) is central pain and is treated with neuromodulating drugs, not more analgesia at the periphery.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Brown-Séquard syndrome:** "Hemisection", "stab wound to the back", "ipsilateral weakness and loss of vibration, contralateral loss of pain and temperature" → the uncrossed DCML and corticospinal tract fail on the lesion side; the spinothalamic tract that already crossed fails on the opposite side.
>
> **Dejerine-Roussy (thalamic pain) syndrome:** "Stroke", "VPL", "excruciating / burning pain on the hemi-anaesthetic side" → infarction makes the surviving VPL neurons irritable and over-signal.
>
> **Phantom limb pain:** "Amputated hand still hurts", "arm touched → hand felt" → cortical plasticity: the arm area takes over the hand area.
>
> **Dorsal column loss:** "Cannot tell joint position", "loss of vibration", "astereognosis", "unsteady with eyes closed" → DCML (gracile for lower limb, cuneate for upper limb).
>
> **Referred pain:** "Heart pain felt in the left arm", "diaphragm pain at the shoulder tip" → no visceral representation in the homunculus; the brain projects the pain to the somatic structure of the same embryonic segment.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> A homunculus with huge lips and thumb = representation by use; SLTC vs CTLS drawn on a cord section = somatotopy of dorsal column vs anterolateral tract; a cord cross-section with one half shaded = hemisection.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Order of neurons: receptor → 1st (DRG) → 2nd (cord/medulla) → 3rd (thalamus) → 4th (cortex). Crossing: DCML at the medulla, anterolateral at the spinal cord. Cortex: areas 3, 1, 2 (postcentral) vs motor 4 (precentral). Weber–Fechner: a 10 % change (100 g on 1 kg) is felt, 1 % (10 g) is not.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Putting pain on the side of a hemisection**
>
> **What students think:** All sensation below a hemisection is lost on the injured side.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The 2nd order neurons of the anterolateral pathway cross in the spinal cord within a segment or two of entry, so the spinothalamic tract in the injured half carries pain and temperature from the **opposite** half of the body. Only the dorsal column fibres (uncrossed until the medulla) and the lateral corticospinal tract (already crossed in the medulla) serve the same side.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Pain crosses low, position crosses high" — draw the two crossings before answering.
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up the two somatotopic orders**
>
> **What students think:** The arrangement is the same in both tracts.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In the dorsal columns sacral fibres are medial and cervical lateral (SLTC); in the anterolateral tract it is reversed (CTLS medial → lateral). This matters for extramedullary compression (outer fibres first) vs intramedullary lesions (inner fibres first — "sacral sparing" in the spinothalamic tract).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Write the word "SLTC" for the back of the cord and read it backwards for the front.
>
> **Trap 3: Confusing VPL with VPM and 3, 1, 2 with 4**
>
> **What students think:** The thalamic nucleus for body sensation is VPM; the sensory cortex is area 4.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** VPL relays the body (DCML and anterolateral); VPM relays the face and taste. Areas 3, 1, 2 are the postcentral (sensory) gyrus; area 4 is the precentral (motor) gyrus.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "L for Limbs (VPL), M for Mouth (VPM)"; sensory 3-1-2 is **post**central, motor 4 is **pre**central.
>
> **Trap 4: Thinking the homunculus is drawn to body size**
>
> **What students think:** The trunk and back have the largest cortical area because they are the largest parts.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Representation follows receptor density and use: thumb and lips are maximal; hip, back and trunk are minimal; viscera have no representation at all (hence referred pain).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Represented by use, not by size."
>
> **Trap 5: Reading Weber–Fechner as a linear law**
>
> **What students think:** Sensation rises in proportion to the stimulus, so adding 10 g should always be felt.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Sensation is proportional to the **log** of the intensity: the just-noticeable change is a fixed fraction of the starting stimulus. 10 g on 1 kg (1 %) is below that fraction; 100 g (10 %) is above it.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Think in percentages, not grams.

> [!tip]+ **🔗 Related Concepts & Cross-Topic Connections**
>
> Understanding how this topic connects to other areas of medicine deepens your knowledge and helps you see the bigger picture. These connections also help you answer integrated questions on exams.
>

>
> **Related Topic 2: [[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres]]**

>

> **Clinical Integration:**
> Bedside sensory examination tests each tract separately — tuning fork and joint position for the dorsal columns, pin and temperature for the spinothalamic tract — and the side and level of each loss localise a cord, brainstem, thalamic or cortical lesion. Brown-Séquard syndrome, thalamic pain, phantom limb pain and referred pain are the four applied syndromes of this chapter.
>
> **Exam Integration:**
> Questions ask: which sensation is lost on which side in hemisection, the level of decussation of each tract, SLTC vs CTLS, Brodmann 3, 1, 2, thalamic relay nucleus (VPL), the eponyms (Bell–Magendie, Müller, Weber–Fechner, Dejerine-Roussy) and the definition of each sensory law.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Levels of control: **skin → spinal cord → thalamus → sensory cortex**; four neurons (receptor/DRG 1st, cord or medulla 2nd, thalamus 3rd, cortex 4th).
> 2. Three pathways: **posterior column, anterolateral spinothalamic, spinocerebellar**.
> 3. DCML = **myelinated, new, fast** — **conscious proprioception, fine touch, vibration, stereognosis**; decussates in the **medulla**.
> 4. Anterolateral = **unmyelinated, old, slow** — **lateral: pain; anterior: crude touch, pressure; temperature; itch**; decussates in the **spinal cord**.
> 5. **Gracile fasciculus → nucleus gracilis = lower limb; cuneate fasciculus → nucleus cuneatus = upper limb.**
> 6. Somatotopy (medial → lateral): **DCML SLTC · anterolateral CTLS**.
> 7. **Brown-Séquard = hemisection**: **ipsilateral** loss of proprioception, vibration, fine touch + **motor paralysis**; **contralateral** loss of **pain and temperature**.
> 8. Thalamus = **sensory relay station**, **3rd order neuron**, **VPL** for general senses.
> 9. **Dejerine-Roussy syndrome** (thalamic pain): stroke → irritable VPL fibres → ↑ signalling → **excruciating pain**.
> 10. Sensory cortex = **postcentral gyrus, Brodmann 3, 1, 2** (motor = precentral); function = **localization**.
> 11. Homunculus: **medial leg, lateral face; maximum thumb, lip; minimum hip, back, trunk; no viscera** → referred pain (same embryonic origin).
> 12. Laws: **Bell–Magendie** (dorsal sensory, ventral motor) · **Müller's doctrine** (specific nerve energies) · **projection** (felt at the receptor) · **cortical plasticity** (**phantom limb pain**) · **Weber–Fechner** (sensation ∝ **log intensity**).

[^1]: The DCML's 2nd order neuron starts in the nucleus gracilis/cuneatus of the medulla; the anterolateral pathway's 2nd order neuron starts in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. Both 3rd order neurons are in the thalamus (VPL), both 4th order neurons in the somatosensory cortex.
[^2]: The page writes "Dorsal column spinothalamic pathway"; the standard name is the **dorsal column–medial lemniscal (DCML) pathway** — it reaches the thalamus through the medial lemniscus, not the spinothalamic tract. The page's own table uses "Dorsal column medial lemniscal pathway".
[^3]: The spinocerebellar tracts (posterior and anterior) carry **unconscious** proprioception from muscle spindles and tendon organs to the **cerebellum**, not to the thalamus or cortex; they are listed with the ascending pathways because they share the lateral white column.
[^4]: Typical values: DCML fibres are large myelinated Aβ fibres (~30–70 m/s). The anterolateral system uses thinly myelinated Aδ fibres (fast, sharp pain, ~5–30 m/s) and unmyelinated C fibres (slow, burning pain, ~0.5–2 m/s) — so "unmyelinated" on the page is true of the majority, not every fibre.
[^5]: DCML fibres cross as internal arcuate fibres in the lower medulla (sensory decussation) and form the medial lemniscus. Anterolateral fibres cross in the anterior white commissure, usually within one or two segments of entry.
[^6]: Standard texts: dorsal columns — sacral medial, cervical lateral (gracilis medial, cuneatus lateral); lateral spinothalamic tract — cervical medial (deep), sacral lateral (superficial). An intramedullary tumour therefore hits cervical spinothalamic fibres first and spares sacral ones ("sacral sparing").
[^7]: At the level of the lesion there is also ipsilateral lower motor neuron weakness and ipsilateral loss of all sensation in that dermatome (dorsal root and horn damaged). Below the lesion the motor paralysis is of upper motor neuron (spastic) type because the lateral corticospinal tract is cut. A pure hemisection is rare; partial forms are common, and stab wounds are the classic cause.
[^8]: VPL (ventral posterolateral nucleus) relays the body; **VPM** (ventral posteromedial) relays the face (trigeminal lemniscus) and taste. The page's "ventropostero-lateral" is the same nucleus as VPL.
[^9]: Thalamic pain syndrome usually follows an infarct of the thalamogeniculate branches of the posterior cerebral artery. Typically there is first a contralateral hemi-anaesthesia; weeks later a severe burning pain with allodynia (pain from light touch) appears on the same anaesthetic side and responds poorly to ordinary analgesics.
[^10]: Mechanism (convergence–projection theory): visceral pain fibres and somatic fibres from the same spinal segment converge on the same dorsal horn neurons; the brain, used to signals from the skin, projects the pain to that skin area. Examples: heart → left arm and chest (T1–T4), diaphragm → shoulder tip (C3–C5), appendix → periumbilical (T10).
[^11]: Müller's point is that the sensation depends on **which nerve** is activated, not on how it is activated — pressing on the eyeball stimulates the optic pathway and is "seen" as light (phosphenes).
[^12]: The ulnar-nerve example and phantom sensations are the classic illustrations of the law of projection in Guyton; the projection is set by the cortical area the fibre ends in.
[^13]: Ramachandran showed that after arm amputation touching the face can be felt on the missing hand, because the face area lies next to the hand area on the homunculus — the same cortical take-over described on the page.
[^14]: Weber (1834) found that the just-noticeable difference is a constant fraction of the starting stimulus (for lifted weights roughly 2–3 %); Fechner (1860) turned this into the logarithmic law S = k log I.
