# 🧵 Characteristics of Nerve Fibres

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Nervous tissue is built from two cell families: **neurons**[^ety-neuron][^pre-neuro] — the **functional units** that carry electrical signals — and **glial cells**[^ety-glia] — the far more **numerous supporting cells** that insulate, feed, clean and guard them. Everything in this chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **Who supports the neuron?** — the glia (astrocytes run the blood–brain barrier, oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells make myelin, microglia are the immune cells)
> - ✅ **How is a neuron built and how fast does it conduct?** — soma, dendrites, axon, terminal; speed depends on **myelin, diameter and temperature**
> - ✅ **What is the signal?** — the **action potential**: −70 → −55 → +35 → −70 → −90 mV, with refractory periods, m and h gates and the all-or-none law

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Power Cable and Its Maintenance Crew

Picture the neuron as a long **electric cable** and the glia as the **crew that keeps it working**. The cable's insulation (myelin) decides how fast the current travels; the crew cleans up spilled chemicals, guards the gate to the bloodstream and repairs damage. The signal itself is a **fuse**: once lit past a threshold it burns all the way with the same size flame (all-or-none), and the burnt stretch cannot be relit for a moment (refractory period).

```
GLIA (1 neuron : 10 glia)                    NEURON (functional unit)
  Astrocyte   → BBB · migration · synapses     Dendrites → RECEIVE
               · clearing · NH3 · fMRI           Soma      → nucleus + Nissl (protein factory)
  Oligodendro → myelin, MANY axons, CNS          Axon hillock / initial segment → AP STARTS (motor)
  Schwann     → myelin, ONE axon, PNS            Axon + myelin + nodes → CONDUCTS (fast if myelinated)
  Microglia   → immune (macrophages)             Terminal  → hands the signal on

NCV ∝ myelin · ∝ diameter · ∝ temperature
ACTION POTENTIAL:  −70 (RMP) ─local─► −55 (firing level) ─Na⁺ in─► +35 (peak) ─K⁺ out─► −70 ─► −90 (hyperpol.)
                   ARP (no response) ──► RRP (only a stronger stimulus) ──► normal
```

### Multipolar neuron — structure

![Multipolar neuron — dendrites, cell body, axon hillock, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, axon terminals (Blausen)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/10/Blausen_0657_MultipolarNeuron.png/800px-Blausen_0657_MultipolarNeuron.png)

**1. Cell body (Soma**[^ety-soma]**)** — contains 2 structures:
- **Nucleus**[^ety-nucleus]**:** controls functions of the neuron.
- **Nissl bodies**[^ety-nissl] **(Rough endoplasmic reticulum**[^ety-reticulum]**):** synthesize proteins.
  - **Affected during nerve cell injury.**[^11]

**2. Dendrites**[^ety-dendrite][^suf-ite]**:**
- **Finger-like projections.**
- **Receiving end of stimulus in neuron.**

**3. Axon:**
- **Axon hillock**[^ety-hillock]**:** part of the cell body giving rise to the axon.
- **Initial segment:** first proper beginning of the axon.
- **Conduction**[^ety-conduction][^pre-con] **of electrical signals:** based on **myelin**
  - **Fast:** myelinated.
  - **Slow:** unmyelinated.
- **Node of Ranvier**[^ety-ranvier]**:**
  - Part of axon **devoid of myelin**.
  - **Maximum Na⁺ channels present (2000–12000/sq.m)**: for action potential.[^12]

**4. Synaptic terminal (Axonal terminal):**
- **Ending of a neuron.**

### Significance
- **Most important electrical activity:** **Action potential.**
- **Site of initiation of action potential:**
  - **Motor neuron:** **Axon hillock + Initial segment.**
  - **Sensory neuron:** **1st Node of Ranvier.**[^13]

### Factors affecting Nerve Conduction Velocity (NCV)

**1. Myelin:**
- **Provides insulation**[^ety-insulation]**:** **Thickness of neuron ∝ Nerve conduction velocity.**
- **Composition:**
  - **Lipids (80 %):** **Sphingomyelin**[^ety-sphingomyelin].
  - **Proteins (20 %):** **Myelin Basic Protein (MBP)**, **zero protein**.[^14]
- **Clinical significance:**
  - **Multiple sclerosis**[^ety-sclerosis][^suf-osis] **(Demyelinating**[^ety-demyelination] **disorder):** **Autoantibodies**[^pre-auto] **against MBP** → **break down myelin** → **↓ NCV**.[^15]

**Myelinated axon:**
- **↑ NCV.**
- **↑ Axonal resistance**[^ety-resistance][^pre-re]**:** ions move **only through the node of Ranvier** (**↓ loss**).
- **↓ Membrane capacitance**[^ety-capacitance]**:** ability to store charges ↓ → ions used for action potential (**Thickness ∝ 1 / Capacitance**).[^16]

```
MYELIN ──► ↑ membrane resistance (no leak between nodes) ─┐
       └─► ↓ membrane capacitance (little charge stored)  ─┴─► current jumps node → node ──► ↑ NCV
DEMYELINATION (MS) ──► leak + charge stored in the bare membrane ──► ↓ NCV / conduction block
```

*(a) Myelinated axon → ↑ NCV vs (b) demyelinated axon → ↓ NCV; big neuron → ↑ NCV vs small neuron → ↓ NCV (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**2. Size of neuron:**
- **Diameter ∝ nerve conduction velocity.**

**3. Temperature**[^ety-temperature]**:**
- **Temperature ∝ nerve conduction velocity.**[^17]

## ⚡ Action Potential of Neuron

### Phases

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - nerve action potential phases.png]]
*Nerve action potential — amplitude (mV, y-axis) against time (x-axis): stimulus → latent period → response; local potential from −70 to the firing level (−55); depolarisation to the peak (+35, the part above 0 = overshoot); repolarisation; hyperpolarisation to −90 (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Latent period**[^ety-latent]**:**
- It is the **time gap between stimulus and response**.

| | **Phases** | **Change in potential** | **Ion changes** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | **Local potential** | −70 mV (**RMP**) → −55 mV (**Firing level**) | **Slow Na⁺ influx**[^ety-flux][^pre-in] (less) |
| **II** | **Depolarization**[^ety-depolarisation] | −55 mV (**Threshold**[^ety-threshold]) → +35 mV | **Fast Na⁺ influx (more):** opening of **voltage gated Na⁺ channels** (↑ intracellular[^pre-intra] positive charge) |
| | **Overshoot of action potential**[^ety-potential] | 0 mV → +35 mV | (same — fast Na⁺ influx) |
| **III** | **Repolarization** | +35 mV → −70 mV | **K⁺ efflux**[^pre-ex] |
| **IV** | **Hyperpolarization** | −70 mV → −90 mV | • **Slow closure of K⁺ channels** · • **Influx of Cl⁻ ions**[^18] |

> [!info] 🧠 Why the curve has this shape
> A small stimulus lets a little Na⁺ trickle in (**local potential**). If that nudges the membrane to the **firing level (−55 mV)**, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels snap open — more Na⁺ in → more depolarisation → more channels open (positive feedback) — and the potential shoots up past zero. The Na⁺ channels then inactivate and the slower K⁺ channels open, so K⁺ leaves and the potential falls back; the K⁺ channels close late, so it **undershoots** to −90 before settling at −70.

> [!note] Note
> **E_Na = +60 mV**, but during the action potential the potential reached is **≈ +35 mV**.[^19]

### Clinical significance of hyperpolarization
- **Principle:** **Hyperpolarization** (**↑ intracellular −ve charge**) → **inhibits neuronal function**.
- **Physiology:** **GABA**[^ety-gaba] → **inhibitory**[^ety-inhibitory] **neurotransmitters** (due to neuronal hyperpolarization).
- **Applied anatomy:** **GABA agonists**[^ety-agonist] (**for sedation**[^ety-sedation]):
  - **Barbiturates**[^ety-barbiturate].
  - **Benzodiazepines**[^ety-benzodiazepine].[^20]

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - change in ion permeability.png]]
*Change in ion permeability (mmho/cm²) over time (ms) — the fast, tall Na⁺ permeability curve produces depolarization; the slower, longer K⁺ permeability curve produces repolarization (cropped from the handwritten page).*[^21]

### Refractory period[^ety-refractory]

**1. Absolute Refractory Period (ARP):**
- From **threshold point to 1/3rd repolarisation**.
- On a second stimulus → **no response** in the neuron.
- **Inactivated**[^ety-inactivation] **voltage gated Na⁺ channels.**

**2. Relative Refractory Period (RRP):**
- From **1/3rd repolarization to the end of the action potential**.
- On a second stimulus of **maximum strength** → **response seen**.[^22]

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - refractory periods.png]]
*Refractory periods — ARP from the response (R) to one-third repolarisation (a second stimulus S gives no response); RRP from there to the end of the action potential (only a stronger S responds) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!note] Note — Voltage gated sodium channels
> - **m gate:** **Activation gate.**
> - **h gate:** **Inactivation gate.**[^23]

| State | m gate (activation) | h gate (inactivation) | Na⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open state** | Open | Open | Na⁺ flows in |
| **Closed state** | Closed | Open | No flow (but ready to open) |
| **Inactivation state** | Open | Closed | No flow (cannot reopen until reset) |

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - sodium channel m and h gates.png]]
*The three states of the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel — open (m open, h open, Na⁺ enters), closed (m closed, h open), inactivation (m open, h closed) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!tip] 🧠 Link the gates to the refractory periods
> **ARP = h gates shut** (inactivated) — no stimulus, however strong, can open them. **RRP = some h gates have reset** but K⁺ channels are still open — only a stronger-than-normal stimulus can recruit enough Na⁺ channels.

### Types of action potential

**Biphasic**[^ety-biphasic] **action potential:**
- **Electrode**[^ety-electrode] **placed on the surface of the neuron** → action potential recorded.

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - biphasic action potential.png]]
*Biphasic action potential — a +ve phase, an isoelectric stretch, then a −ve phase as the impulse passes the two surface electrodes in turn (cropped from the handwritten page).*[^24]

**Compound**[^ety-compound][^pre-com] **action potential:**
- **Multiple peaks.**
- In a **mixed nerve:** due to **many nerve fibres & varying conduction velocity**.[^25]

![[Characteristics of Nerve Fibres - compound action potential.png]]
*Compound action potential — several overlapping peaks, one for each group of fibres in a mixed nerve conducting at its own velocity (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Graded potential (GP) vs Action potential (AP)

| | **Graded**[^ety-graded] **potential** | **Action potential** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | Local potential | **Summation**[^ety-summation] of local potential → reach threshold → AP |
| **Summation** | Possible | Not possible |
| **Threshold** | Not needed | **Needed** |
| **Amplitude**[^ety-amplitude] **of potential** | Changes | Constant |
| **All or none law** | Does not obey | **Obeys** |

> [!info] 🧠 One idea behind the whole table
> A graded potential is a **dimmer switch** — its size follows the stimulus, adds up with others and fades with distance. An action potential is an **on/off switch** — once threshold is reached it fires at full, constant size (all-or-none), and the refractory period stops two from piling on top of each other.[^26]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> A nerve fibre is a **leaky, charge-storing cable** that has to deliver an **all-or-none** signal fast and far. Everything in the chapter is a solution to that problem: **myelin** raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance so the current is not lost and jumps from node to node (where Na⁺ channels are packed), **diameter** lowers internal resistance, **temperature** speeds the channel gates, and the **glia** keep the cable insulated (oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells), its surroundings clean (astrocytes clearing K⁺, glutamate and NH₃) and its blood supply matched to activity (neurovascular coupling).
>
> The signal itself is a **positive-feedback burst with a built-in off-switch**: Na⁺ entry opens more Na⁺ channels until the h gates inactivate them; that inactivation is the absolute refractory period, which makes each action potential separate, constant in size and one-directional.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Myelinated fibres conduct by saltatory conduction at high NCV; the initial segment (motor) or first node (sensory) fires a +35 mV spike from a −70 mV rest; astrocytes buffer K⁺ and glutamate, convert NH₃ into glutamine and keep the BBB tight.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Patchy demyelination (early multiple sclerosis) slows conduction (↓ NCV, delayed evoked potentials) but the impulse still gets through; symptoms come and go and worsen with heat (warmth shortens the action potential and blocks marginal fibres). A rise in blood ammonia is buffered by astrocytic glutamine synthesis.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Widespread loss of myelin produces conduction block — weakness, sensory loss, optic neuritis. When ammonia exceeds the astrocytes' capacity (liver failure), glutamine accumulates inside them, they swell and hepatic encephalopathy follows. Drugs that push neurons into hyperpolarisation (barbiturates, benzodiazepines) sedate, and in overdose depress respiration.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → injury to myelin or glia (autoimmunity against MBP, ammonia, hypoxia) → slowed conduction / ↑ glutamine → compensation by remaining fibres and astrocytes → conduction block / astrocyte swelling → full clinical syndrome
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Nerve conduction studies separate demyelination (↓ NCV, preserved amplitude) from axonal loss (↓ amplitude, near-normal NCV). Treating the cause early (immunomodulation in MS, lowering ammonia in liver failure) keeps the disease in the compensated stage.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Astrocyte:** "Star-shaped", "GFAP", "foot processes", "tumour marker", "glutamate–glutamine cycle", "neurovascular coupling / functional MRI" → the most versatile glial cell; GFAP-positive tumour = astrocytoma.
>
> **Microglia:** "Modified immune cells", "monocyte–macrophage system", "the only glia of mesodermal (immune) lineage" → brain macrophages.
>
> **Oligodendrocyte vs Schwann cell:** "Myelinates multiple axons, CNS" vs "single axon, PNS" → MS hits oligodendrocyte myelin; Guillain–Barré hits Schwann-cell myelin.
>
> **Multiple sclerosis:** "Demyelinating disorder", "autoantibodies against MBP", "↓ NCV" → CNS demyelination.
>
> **Site of AP initiation:** "Axon hillock + initial segment" (motor) vs "first node of Ranvier" (sensory).
>
> **Hyperpolarisation drugs:** "GABA agonists for sedation" → barbiturates and benzodiazepines.
>

>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> −70 mV RMP, −55 mV firing level, +35 mV peak, −90 mV hyperpolarisation, E_Na +60 mV; Na⁺ channels 2000–12000 per µm² at the nodes; myelin 80 % lipid / 20 % protein; neurons : glia = 1 : 10.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "The action potential peaks at E_Na"**
>
> **What students think:** The membrane depolarises all the way to the Na⁺ equilibrium potential (+60 mV).
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Before E_Na is reached, h gates inactivate the Na⁺ channels and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; Na⁺ entry stops and K⁺ exit starts, so the peak stops at about +35 mV. The overshoot is only the 0 → +35 part.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "E_Na +60 is the ceiling, +35 is the peak."
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells**
>
> **What students think:** Both myelinate one axon each, or Schwann cells work in the CNS.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Oligodendrocytes are CNS cells that send processes to myelinate segments of multiple axons; Schwann cells are PNS cells that myelinate a single segment of a single axon. This explains why MS (CNS) spares peripheral nerves and why peripheral nerves can regenerate along Schwann-cell tubes.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**O**ligo = **O**ne cell, many axons, CNS; **S**chwann = **S**ingle axon, PNS."
>
> **Trap 3: Saying the motor neuron fires at the first node**
>
> **What students think:** All neurons start the AP at the first node of Ranvier.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In motor neurons the axon hillock + initial segment has the lowest threshold and fires first; in sensory neurons the receptor's generator potential is at the periphery, so the first node of Ranvier is the first excitable site.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Motor → hillock; Sensory → first node.
>
> **Trap 4: ARP vs RRP**
>
> **What students think:** No stimulus can excite the neuron during the whole refractory period.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Only the ARP (threshold to one-third repolarisation) is absolute — the Na⁺ channels are inactivated. In the RRP a stimulus of maximum (supra-normal) strength gives a response, because some channels have reset.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** ARP = h gates shut (impossible); RRP = h gates resetting (possible with a stronger stimulus).
>
> **Trap 5: Graded potential vs action potential**
>
> **What students think:** Both obey the all-or-none law.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Graded potentials change amplitude with stimulus size, can be summated and need no threshold; only the action potential obeys all-or-none with constant amplitude, and it cannot be summated.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Graded = graded size; Action = all or none."

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Neuron** = functional unit; **glia** = supporting cells; **neurons : glia = 1 : 10**.
> 2. Glia by size: **macroglia** (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, Schwann, ependymal, satellite) vs **microglia** (modified immune cells from the **monocyte–macrophage system**).
> 3. Astrocytes: star-shaped; **protoplasmic → grey matter**, **fibrous → white matter**; **GFAP** = intermediate filament → **tumour marker**.
> 4. Astrocyte functions: **BBB (most critical — endothelial tight junctions + astrocyte foot processes)**, neuronal **migration** (radial glia), **synapse formation (thrombospondin)**, **synaptic clearing** to the perivascular space, **NH₃ detoxification (glutamate → glutamine)**, **neurovascular coupling** (principle of **fMRI**).
> 5. **Oligodendrocyte → multiple axons, CNS; Schwann cell → single axon, PNS.**
> 6. Neuron types: unipolar (invertebrates), bipolar (**retina**), pseudounipolar (**DRG**), multipolar (**spinal α motor neuron — most numerous**).
> 7. Nissl bodies = RER → protein synthesis → **affected in nerve injury**; node of Ranvier = **max Na⁺ channels (2000–12000/µm²)**.
> 8. AP starts at **axon hillock + initial segment (motor)** and **1st node of Ranvier (sensory)**.
> 9. NCV ∝ **myelin thickness**, **diameter**, **temperature**; myelin = **80 % lipid (sphingomyelin) / 20 % protein (MBP, zero protein)**; myelin ↑ resistance, ↓ capacitance.
> 10. **MS:** autoantibodies vs **MBP** → demyelination → **↓ NCV**.
> 11. AP: **−70 → −55 (local, slow Na⁺) → +35 (fast Na⁺, VG Na⁺ channels) → −70 (K⁺ efflux) → −90 (slow K⁺ closure, Cl⁻ influx)**; E_Na = +60 but peak ≈ **+35**.
> 12. **ARP** = threshold → ⅓ repolarisation, inactivated Na⁺ channels, no response; **RRP** = ⅓ → end, response to **maximum-strength** stimulus. **m gate = activation, h gate = inactivation.**
> 13. **Compound AP** = multiple peaks in a **mixed nerve**; **AP obeys all-or-none, needs threshold, constant amplitude, no summation** — graded potential is the opposite.

[^1]: The page's ratio (1 : 10) is the classic textbook figure. Modern cell counts of the human brain (isotropic fractionator, 2009) put neurons and glia closer to 1 : 1 overall, with the ratio varying by region; examiners still expect "glia are more numerous".
[^2]: Microglia are the brain's resident macrophages. Older texts derive them from blood monocytes (mononuclear phagocyte system, as on the page); current evidence shows most arise from yolk-sac macrophage precursors that colonise the brain in fetal life, which is why microglia are the only glia not of neuroectodermal origin.
[^3]: GFAP is a type III intermediate filament (≈ 10 nm, between actin and microtubules in size). Immunostaining for GFAP identifies astrocytes and tumours derived from them (astrocytoma, glioblastoma, ependymoma), and serum GFAP is studied as a marker of brain injury.
[^4]: The actual barrier is the endothelium — continuous tight junctions with no fenestrations and few vesicles; astrocyte end-feet (with pericytes and the basement membrane) induce and maintain these endothelial properties. Circumventricular organs (area postrema, posterior pituitary, median eminence, etc.) have no BBB.
[^5]: Strictly, radial glia are embryonic glial progenitors whose long processes serve as guide rails for migrating cortical neurons (and Bergmann glia do the same in the cerebellum); most later transform into astrocytes — hence the page's "(Astrocytes)".
[^6]: Astrocytes take up K⁺ released by active neurons and redistribute it towards blood vessels ("spatial buffering", via Kir4.1 channels), and remove synaptic glutamate with transporters (EAAT1/EAAT2), ending its action and preventing excitotoxicity.
[^7]: Glutamine synthetase is found almost only in astrocytes, so they are the brain's sole route for disposing of ammonia (glutamate + NH₃ + ATP → glutamine). The glutamine returns to neurons, where glutaminase regenerates glutamate. In liver failure, high blood ammonia makes astrocytes accumulate glutamine and swell (Alzheimer type II astrocytes) — the basis of hepatic encephalopathy and cerebral oedema. The page writes "glutamine synthase"; the enzyme's formal name is glutamine *synthetase* because it uses ATP.
[^8]: The page names *neuroligin* receptors. Standard texts describe glutamate acting on astrocytic **metabotropic glutamate receptors** → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → phospholipase A₂ → arachidonic acid → vasodilator metabolites (prostaglandin E₂, epoxyeicosatrienoic acids) at the end-feet. Neuroligins are synaptic adhesion proteins that bind neurexins. Functional MRI detects the resulting rise in oxygenated blood (BOLD signal). The page's word is kept in the body.
[^9]: One oligodendrocyte can myelinate segments of up to ~40–50 axons; one Schwann cell myelinates a single internode of a single axon. Unmyelinated peripheral axons are still enclosed — several to one Schwann cell (Remak bundles).
[^10]: Other textbook examples: bipolar neurons also in the olfactory epithelium and the cochlear and vestibular ganglia; pseudounipolar neurons in all cranial sensory ganglia (e.g., trigeminal); true unipolar neurons are typical of invertebrates and rare in adult vertebrates.
[^11]: Nissl substance is RER with free ribosomes that stains with basic dyes (cresyl violet). It fills the soma and dendrites but is absent from the axon hillock and axon — the histological way to find the hillock. After axon injury it breaks up (chromatolysis) — see [[Classification of Nerve Fibres]].
[^12]: The page's unit reads "/sq.m"; the figure is per **square micrometre**. Ganong gives Na⁺ channel densities of ~50–75/µm² in the cell body, 350–500/µm² at the initial segment, < 25/µm² under the myelin, **2000–12000/µm² at the nodes of Ranvier** and 20–75/µm² at the terminals.
[^13]: The initial segment fires first in motor neurons because its threshold is lowest (high Na⁺ channel density, no myelin). In a sensory neuron the stimulus produces a generator (receptor) potential at the ending, and the first node of Ranvier is the first point that can generate a propagated action potential.
[^14]: The page's 80 : 20 lipid-to-protein ratio is the textbook value (Ganong ~70–80 % lipid). Besides sphingomyelin, myelin is rich in cholesterol and galactocerebrosides. "Zero protein" = **myelin protein zero (P₀)**, the main structural protein of peripheral (Schwann-cell) myelin — mutations cause Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1B; in CNS myelin the main protein is proteolipid protein (PLP).
[^15]: Multiple sclerosis is an immune-mediated (largely T-cell–driven, with B-cell and antibody components) demyelination of the CNS; myelin basic protein and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein are among the target antigens. The page's "autoantibodies against MBP" summarises this.
[^16]: Myelin increases the *transverse* (membrane) resistance and decreases membrane capacitance about 50-fold, so little current leaks out or is spent charging the internode; the local current depolarises the next node and the impulse jumps — saltatory conduction. Conduction velocity of myelinated mammalian fibres ≈ 6 m/s per µm of outer diameter; of unmyelinated fibres it rises only with the square root of diameter.
[^17]: Within the physiological range, cooling slows conduction (roughly 2 m/s per °C in myelinated nerves) and cold can block it; heat speeds it, but in demyelinated fibres a rise in temperature can cause conduction block (Uhthoff phenomenon in MS).
[^18]: Standard texts attribute after-hyperpolarisation mainly to voltage-gated K⁺ channels that close slowly, so K⁺ keeps leaving after the membrane has returned to rest. Cl⁻ influx is the mechanism of GABA-A inhibitory postsynaptic potentials — the hyperpolarisation discussed in the next box. The page lists both.
[^19]: E_Na from the Nernst equation is about +60 mV (Guyton +61 mV). The spike stops at ~+35 mV because Na⁺ channels inactivate (h gates) and K⁺ channels open before E_Na is reached.
[^20]: Both act on the GABA-A receptor, a Cl⁻ channel: benzodiazepines increase the **frequency** of channel opening, barbiturates increase the **duration** of opening (and at high doses open it directly — hence their greater danger in overdose). Mnemonic: "**Fre**nch **Bar**tenders" — **Fre**quency = benzodiazepines, **Dur**ation = **Bar**biturates.
[^21]: The graph is the Hodgkin–Huxley conductance curve (1 mmho = 1 mS). Guyton notes Na⁺ conductance rises ~5000-fold during the upstroke; K⁺ conductance rises more slowly, peaks later and lasts longer, which is why repolarisation follows depolarisation and over-runs into hyperpolarisation.
[^22]: Ganong defines the absolute refractory period as lasting from the time the firing level is reached until repolarisation is about one-third complete, and the relative refractory period from that point to the start of after-depolarisation. The ARP limits the maximum firing frequency and makes conduction one-way.
[^23]: The m and h gates are Hodgkin and Huxley's terms (1952). At rest m is closed and h open; depolarisation opens m quickly (activation) and closes h a little later (inactivation, the "ball-and-chain" of the channel's inner loop); repolarisation must reopen h and shut m before the channel can fire again.
[^24]: With both electrodes on the surface of an intact nerve, the impulse makes the first electrode negative (recorded as one deflection), passes between them (isoelectric), then makes the second negative (opposite deflection) — biphasic. If one electrode sits on a crushed (inactive) part, only one deflection is seen — monophasic.
[^25]: Erlanger and Gasser recorded the compound action potential of a mixed nerve and named its successive peaks — A (α, β, γ, δ), B and C — by conduction velocity: the basis of the fibre classification in [[Classification of Nerve Fibres]]. Unlike a single fibre, the compound AP's amplitude grows with stimulus strength as more fibres are recruited.
[^26]: Graded potentials include receptor (generator) potentials, synaptic potentials (EPSP, IPSP) and the end-plate potential; they spread electrotonically and fade over a few millimetres, and may be depolarising or hyperpolarising.

[^ety-ammonia]: **ammonia** — from *sal ammoniacus*, "salt of Ammon", collected near the temple of the god Amun in Libya → NH₃, the toxic product of amino-acid breakdown.
[^ety-glutamate]: **glutamate / glutamine** — Latin *gluten*, "glue" (glutamic acid was first isolated from wheat gluten in 1866) + *-amate* (salt of the acid) / *-amine* (the amide form, with an extra NH₂). **Differentiate:** *glutamate* — excitatory neurotransmitter; *glutamine* — its harmless storage and transport form carrying the extra nitrogen.
[^ety-mri]: **MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)** — *magnetic* (Greek *Magnētis lithos*, stone of Magnesia) + *resonance* (Latin *re-*, "again" + *sonare*, "to sound" → re-sounding, the nuclei re-emitting energy) + *imaging*. *Functional* MRI images activity-related changes in blood oxygenation.
[^ety-hyperemia]: **hyperaemia** — Greek *hyper-*, "over" + *haima*, "blood" + *-ia*, "condition" → excess blood in a part. **Differentiate:** *active hyperaemia* (more arterial inflow — exercise, activity, inflammation) vs *passive hyperaemia / congestion* (less venous outflow) vs *ischaemia* (too little blood).
[^ety-neuroligin]: **neuroligin** — Greek *neuron* + Latin *ligare*, "to bind" + *-in* (a protein) → a nerve-binding protein; neuroligins are postsynaptic adhesion molecules that bind presynaptic neurexins (the same *lig-* as ligand, ligament).
[^ety-vasodilator]: **vasodilator** — Latin *vas*, "vessel" + *dilatare*, "to spread out, widen" + *-or*, the agent → something that widens vessels. Opposite *vasoconstrictor* (*constringere*, to draw tight).
[^ety-arachidonic]: **arachidonic acid** — Greek *arachis*, "groundnut, peanut" → arachidic acid was first found in peanut oil; arachidonic acid is its 20-carbon unsaturated relative, parent of prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes (the eicosanoids, Greek *eikosi*, twenty).
[^ety-prostaglandin]: **prostaglandin** — named by Ulf von Euler (1935), who first found the activity in seminal fluid and thought it came from the **prostate gland**; in fact almost every tissue makes them.
[^ety-myelin]: **myelin** — Greek *myelos*, "marrow" + *-in* → the fatty "marrow" of nerves; coined by Rudolf Virchow (1854). Same root in *myelitis* (spinal cord) and *myeloid* (bone marrow) — context decides; do not confuse with *myo-* (muscle).
[^ety-axon]: **axon** — Greek *axōn*, "axis, axle" → the main axis of the nerve cell along which the impulse travels away from the soma (term popularised by Kölliker, 1896).
[^ety-polar]: **unipolar / bipolar / pseudounipolar / multipolar** — Latin *polus*, "pole, end of an axis" (Greek *polos*, pivot) + the number prefix → the number of processes ("poles") leaving the cell body.
[^ety-invertebrate]: **invertebrate** — Latin *in-*, "not, without" + *vertebra*, "joint, a turning" (*vertere*, to turn) → an animal without a backbone.
[^ety-retina]: **retina** — Medieval Latin, from *rete*, "net" → the net-like inner coat of the eye (its vessels look like a net). Its bipolar cells link photoreceptors to ganglion cells.
[^ety-ganglion]: **ganglion** — Greek *ganglion*, "a swelling, a knot under the skin" (Galen applied it to knots on nerves) → a collection of nerve-cell bodies outside the CNS. Plural *ganglia*. **Differentiate:** *ganglion* (outside the CNS) vs *nucleus* (a cluster of cell bodies inside the CNS).
[^ety-soma]: **soma** — Greek *sōma*, "body" → the cell body. Same root in *somatic* (of the body), *chromosome* (coloured body), *somatotropin*.
[^ety-nucleus]: **nucleus** — Latin *nucleus*, "kernel, inner part of a nut" (diminutive of *nux*, nut) → the kernel of the cell.
[^ety-nissl]: **Nissl bodies** — Franz Nissl (1860–1919), German neuropathologist, who devised the basic-dye stain (1880s–1890s) that shows them as blue granules in the soma.
[^ety-reticulum]: **reticulum** — Latin, "a little net" (*rete*, net + *-culum*, small) → the endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membranes; *rough* ER carries ribosomes (protein synthesis), *smooth* ER does not. Same root as *sarcoplasmic reticulum* in muscle.
[^ety-dendrite]: **dendrite** — Greek *dendron*, "tree" + *-ite*, "belonging to" → the tree-like branches of the neuron that receive signals.
[^ety-hillock]: **axon hillock** — English *hill* + *-ock*, a diminutive → the little hill of the cell body from which the axon rises.
[^ety-conduction]: **conduction** — Latin *con-*, "together, with" + *ducere*, "to lead" + *-tion* → leading (an impulse) along. **Differentiate:** *conduction* (along one cell's membrane) · *transmission* (across a synapse to another cell) · *saltatory conduction* (Latin *saltare*, to jump — node to node).
[^ety-ranvier]: **node of Ranvier** — Louis-Antoine Ranvier (1835–1922), French histologist, who described the regular gaps in the myelin sheath in the 1870s. *Node* — Latin *nodus*, "knot".
[^ety-insulation]: **insulation** — Latin *insula*, "island" + *-ation* → making something an island, cut off from its surroundings; myelin "islands" the axon electrically.
[^ety-sphingomyelin]: **sphingomyelin** — Greek *sphinx* + *myelin* → J. L. W. Thudichum (1884) named sphingosine after the Sphinx "in commemoration of the many enigmas" it posed; sphingomyelin is its phospholipid found in myelin (deficient breakdown → Niemann–Pick disease).
[^ety-sclerosis]: **sclerosis** — Greek *sklēros*, "hard" + *-osis*, "condition" → hardening; in *multiple sclerosis* the scattered demyelinated plaques heal with hard glial scars (Charcot's *sclérose en plaques*, 1868). **Differentiate:** *multiple sclerosis* (CNS demyelination) · *amyotrophic lateral sclerosis* (motor-neuron degeneration) · *atherosclerosis* (*athērē*, gruel — hardened arteries).
[^ety-demyelination]: **demyelination** — Latin *de-*, "removal, away" + *myelin* + *-ation*, the process → the process of stripping myelin from an intact axon. **Differentiate:** *demyelination* (myelin lost after it formed — MS, Guillain–Barré) vs *dysmyelination* (myelin never formed properly — leukodystrophies) vs *axonal degeneration* (the axon itself dies).
[^ety-resistance]: **resistance** — Latin *re-*, "back, against" + *sistere*, "to stand" → standing against (the flow of current); *R* in Ohm's law (V = IR).
[^ety-capacitance]: **capacitance** — Latin *capax*, "able to hold" (*capere*, to take) + *-ance* → the ability to hold (store) charge on the membrane; the thicker the insulation, the smaller the stored charge per millivolt.
[^ety-temperature]: **temperature** — Latin *temperare*, "to mix in due proportion, to moderate" → the body's degree of heat; it speeds or slows every channel gate.
[^ety-latent]: **latent** — Latin *latere*, "to lie hidden" + *-ent* → the hidden interval while the impulse travels from the stimulating to the recording electrode. **Differentiate:** *latent period* (stimulus → response) vs *refractory period* (response → readiness for the next).
[^ety-flux]: **influx / efflux** — Latin *in-*, "into" + *fluxus*, "a flowing" (*fluere*, to flow) → flowing in; *ex-* (*ef-*), "out of" → flowing out. Na⁺ influx depolarises; K⁺ efflux repolarises.
[^ety-depolarisation]: **depolarisation / repolarisation / hyperpolarisation** — Latin *de-*, "away, down" + *polaris* (*polus*, pole) + *-ation* → losing the membrane's polarity (inside becomes less negative, even positive); *re-*, "again" → restoring the resting polarity; *hyper-*, "beyond" → becoming more negative than rest. **Grade them:** −70 → +35 (de-) → −70 (re-) → −90 (hyper-).
[^ety-threshold]: **threshold** — Old English *therscold*, the sill of a doorway → the level that must be crossed to enter; here the firing level (−55 mV) at which voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open en masse.
[^ety-potential]: **potential** — Latin *potentia*, "power, capability" (*posse*, to be able) → electrical potential = the capacity to do work per unit charge, measured in volts. *Action potential* = the potential change that "acts" (propagates).
[^ety-gaba]: **GABA** — **g**amma-**a**mino**b**utyric **a**cid: *gamma* (the amino group sits on the third, γ, carbon) + *amino* + *butyric* (Latin *butyrum*, "butter" — butyric acid gives rancid butter its smell). The main inhibitory transmitter of the brain.
[^ety-inhibitory]: **inhibitory** — Latin *in-*, "in" + *habere*, "to hold" → *inhibere*, "to hold in, restrain" + *-ory* → restraining (neuronal firing). **Differentiate:** *inhibition* (reduced activity) vs *excitation* (increased) vs *depression* (general fall in activity).
[^ety-agonist]: **agonist** — Greek *agōnistēs*, "contestant, champion" (*agōn*, contest) → a drug that activates a receptor like the natural ligand. **Differentiate:** *antagonist* (*anti-* + *agōnistēs* — blocks it) · *partial agonist* · *inverse agonist* (produces the opposite effect).
[^ety-sedation]: **sedation** — Latin *sedare*, "to settle, calm" + *-ation* → the process of calming. **Differentiate:** *sedation* (calm, drowsy) · *hypnosis* (sleep) · *anaesthesia* (loss of sensation and consciousness).
[^ety-barbiturate]: **barbiturate** — from *barbituric acid*, synthesised by Adolf von Baeyer (1864) from urea and malonic acid; why he chose "barbit-" is disputed (St Barbara's Day or a woman named Barbara are the usual stories) — origin uncertain.
[^ety-benzodiazepine]: **benzodiazepine** — *benzo-* (a fused benzene ring, from *benzoin* resin) + *di-*, "two" + *aza-*, "nitrogen" + *-epine*, a seven-membered ring → a benzene ring fused to a seven-membered ring with two nitrogens.
[^ety-refractory]: **refractory** — Latin *refractarius*, "stubborn, obstinate" (*refringere*, "to break up") → the period when the membrane obstinately refuses to respond. **Differentiate:** *absolute* (no response to any stimulus) vs *relative* (response only to a stronger stimulus).
[^ety-inactivation]: **inactivation** — Latin *in-*, "not" + *activus*, "active" + *-ation* → the process of being made non-active; for the Na⁺ channel, the h gate plugging the pore although the m gate is open. **Differentiate:** *closed* (resting, can open) vs *inactivated* (cannot open until reset by repolarisation).
[^ety-biphasic]: **biphasic** — Latin *bi-*, "two" + Greek *phasis*, "appearance, phase" + *-ic* → having two phases (a positive and a negative deflection). **Differentiate:** *monophasic* (one deflection — one electrode on injured tissue) vs *biphasic* (both electrodes on intact surface).
[^ety-electrode]: **electrode** — Greek *ēlektron*, "amber" (rubbed amber gives static electricity) + *hodos*, "way, path" → the path by which electricity enters or leaves; coined by Michael Faraday (1834).
[^ety-compound]: **compound** — Latin *com-*, "together" + *ponere*, "to put" → put together; the compound action potential is the sum of many single-fibre action potentials.
[^ety-graded]: **graded** — Latin *gradus*, "a step" → coming in steps of different size; a graded potential's amplitude matches the stimulus.
[^ety-summation]: **summation** — Latin *summa*, "the total" + *-ation* → adding up. **Differentiate:** *spatial summation* (inputs at the same time from different places) vs *temporal summation* (inputs in quick succession at one place).
[^ety-amplitude]: **amplitude** — Latin *amplitudo*, "width, size" (*amplus*, large) → the height of a wave from baseline to peak.
[^pre-neuro]: **neuro- / neur-** — nerve, nervous system: neuron, neuroglia, neurotransmitter, neurovascular, neuroligin. Don't confuse with **nephro-** kidney · **-neuria / -algia** (neuralgia = nerve pain).
[^suf-cyte]: **-cyte** — cell: astrocyte, oligodendrocyte, monocyte. Don't confuse with **-cytosis** a process or an excess of cells (phagocytosis, leucocytosis) · **-blast** an immature, forming cell (neuroblast).
[^pre-oligo]: **oligo-** — few, scanty: oligodendrocyte, oliguria, oligohydramnios. Don't confuse with **poly-** many · **a- / an-** none (anuria).
[^pre-macro]: **macro-** — large: macroglia, macrophage, macrocytic. Don't confuse with **micro-** small · **mega- / megalo-** great, enlarged (megaloblast).
[^pre-micro]: **micro-** — small: microglia, microenvironment, micrometre (µm). Don't confuse with **macro-** large · **mini-** (Latin, small) · **myo-** muscle.
[^pre-epi]: **epi- (ep-)** — upon, over: ependyma, epineurium, epithelium. Don't confuse with **endo-** within · **peri-** around · **hypo-** under.
[^pre-mono]: **mono-** — single, one (Greek): monocyte, monophasic. Don't confuse with **uni-** one (Latin twin: unipolar) · **poly-** many · **multi-** many (Latin).
[^pre-proto]: **proto-** — first, primitive: protoplasmic, protoplasm, prototype. Don't confuse with **pro-** before, forward (prophase) · **pre-** before.
[^suf-plasm]: **-plasm / -plasma** — formed substance: protoplasm, cytoplasm, sarcoplasm. Don't confuse with **-plasia** growth, formation of tissue (hyperplasia) · **-plasty** surgical reshaping (rhinoplasty).
[^pre-anti]: **anti-** — against: antibody, antigen, antagonist. Don't confuse with **ante-** before (antenatal) · **anti-cipate** (from *ante-*).
[^pre-endo]: **endo-** — within: endothelium, endoneurium, endoplasmic reticulum. Don't confuse with **epi-** upon · **exo-** outside · **ecto-** outer.
[^suf-ation]: **-ation / -ition / -ion** — the process or result of an action: migration, conduction, depolarisation, summation. Don't confuse with **-osis** a condition or state (sclerosis) · **-ity** a quality (excitability).
[^pre-syn]: **syn- / sym-** — together, with: synapse, synthase, sympathetic. Don't confuse with **sin-** (Latin *sinister*, left) · **semi-** half.
[^suf-in]: **-in** — a protein or neutral substance: myelin, neuroligin, thrombospondin, actin, myosin. Don't confuse with **-ine** an amine, amino acid or alkaloid (glutamine, adrenaline) · **-ase** an enzyme.
[^pre-trans]: **trans-** — across, through, beyond: neurotransmitter (sent across the synapse), transection, transport. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **dia-** through (diapedesis).
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: perivascular, perineurium, perimysium. Don't confuse with **para-** beside · **pre-** before · **epi-** upon.
[^pre-de]: **de-** — down, away, removal, reversal: detoxification, depolarisation, demyelinating. Don't confuse with **dis-** apart (dissociation) · **dys-** bad, abnormal (dysmyelination).
[^suf-ine]: **-ine** — an amine, amino acid or alkaloid: glutamine, acetylcholine, nifedipine. Don't confuse with **-in** a protein (myelin) · **-ide** a binary chemical compound (chloride).
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, above, excessive: hyperaemia, hyperpolarisation, hyperthermia. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under, deficient · **supra-** above (Latin: suprathreshold).
[^suf-emia]: **-aemia / -emia** — a condition of the blood: hyperaemia, anaemia, hyperammonaemia. Don't confuse with **-uria** a condition of the urine · **-osis** a general state.
[^pre-uni]: **uni-** — one (Latin): unipolar, unilateral. Don't confuse with **mono-** one (Greek twin) · **bi-** two · **multi-** many.
[^pre-bi]: **bi-** — two (Latin): bipolar, biphasic, bilateral. Don't confuse with **di-** two (Greek: dipeptide, benzodiazepine) · **bis-** twice · **dis-** apart.
[^pre-pseudo]: **pseudo-** — false, resembling: pseudounipolar (looks unipolar but its single stalk is two fused processes), pseudohypertrophy. Don't confuse with **para-** beside · **quasi-** almost.
[^pre-multi]: **multi-** — many (Latin): multipolar, multiple sclerosis, multinucleate. Don't confuse with **poly-** many (Greek twin: polyneuropathy) · **oligo-** few.
[^suf-ite]: **-ite** — belonging to, a part of: dendrite, neurite, somite. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation (neuritis) · **-ide** a chemical compound.
[^pre-con]: **con- / com- / co-** — together, with, thoroughly: conduction, constancy, congenital. Don't confuse with **contra-** against · **counter-** opposite.
[^suf-osis]: **-osis** — a condition, state or process (often abnormal): sclerosis, necrosis, thrombosis. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation · **-iasis** a disease from an agent (giardiasis).
[^pre-auto]: **auto-** — self: autoantibodies, autoimmune, autonomic. Don't confuse with **allo-** other (allograft) · **hetero-** different.
[^pre-re]: **re-** — back, again: repolarisation, resistance, receptor. Don't confuse with **retro-** backwards, behind · **pre-** before.
[^pre-in]: **in- (1)** — into: influx, injection. **in- (2)** — not: inactivation, invertebrate. Same spelling, two Latin prefixes — context decides. Don't confuse with **intra-** within · **inter-** between.
[^pre-intra]: **intra-** — within, inside: intracellular, intravenous. Don't confuse with **inter-** between (intercellular) · **extra-** outside (extracellular).
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (ef-, e-)** — out of: efflux, excitation, efferent. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exocytosis) · **extra-** beyond.
[^pre-com]: **com- (con-, co-)** — together: compound, compression, complex. Don't confuse with **contra-** against · **counter-** opposite.
