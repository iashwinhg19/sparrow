# 🧠 CSF, CBF and BBB

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The brain lives in a **closed bony box** filled with three things — **brain tissue, blood and cerebrospinal fluid** — and protected by a **barrier**. This chapter covers:
> - ✅ **CSF** — the cushioning water bath: where it is made, how it circulates and is absorbed, how it differs from plasma.
> - ✅ **CBF** — the brain's blood supply: how much, why the brain is so sensitive to hypoxia, and what changes the flow.
> - ✅ **BBB** — the wall that decides what enters the brain, its gatekeeper (P-glycoprotein), the gaps in it (circumventricular organs), and the **Monro–Kellie doctrine** that ties all three volumes together.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Water-Bed in a Locked Safe

Picture the brain as a delicate jelly floating on a **water-bed (CSF)** inside a **locked safe (skull)**. The water is made continuously by taps inside the bed (**choroid plexus**) and drained out through one-way valves (**arachnoid villi**) into the veins. Fresh fuel is pumped in through pipes (**CBF**), but every pipe is lined by **security guards (BBB)** who let only small, fat-soluble or ID-carrying (transporter) visitors in — except at a few **unguarded windows (circumventricular organs)**. Because the safe cannot expand, anything that swells inside must squeeze something else out (**Monro–Kellie**).

```
CSF   choroid plexus (ependymal cells) → lateral ventricles → foramen of Monro → III ventricle → aqueduct of Sylvius
      → IV ventricle → foramina of Luschka & Magendie → subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi → venous sinuses
      150 ml · 550 ml/day · turnover 3.7/day · 70–180 mm H₂O · regulated at ABSORPTION
CBF   750 ml/min = 14 % CO · 20 % of resting O₂ · autoregulation 65–140 mmHg · ↑ pCO₂ → ↑ CBF · ↓ 1 °C → ↓ 7 %
BBB   endothelial tight junctions + astrocyte foot processes · in: lipid-soluble, gases, glucose (GLUT-1), water (AQP4)
      out: protein hormones, H⁺, dopamine · gatekeeper P-glycoprotein · no BBB at CVOs (SOAP)
SKULL CSF + blood + brain = CONSTANT (Monro–Kellie)
```

## 💧 Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF)[^ety-cerebrospinal]

### General properties
- **Function:** **Lubrication**[^ety-lubrication] **& cushion.**[^1]
- **Site of production:** **Ependymal**[^ety-ependymal][^pre-epi] **cells of choroid**[^ety-choroid] **plexus**[^ety-plexus].

| **Ependymal cell** |
|---|
| **Glial**[^ety-glia] **cell** |
| **Lines the choroid plexus** (and the ventricles) |
| **Secretes**[^ety-secretion] **CSF** |

![[CSF, CBF and BBB - blood-CSF barrier at the choroid plexus.png]]
*Blood–CSF barrier (section of choroid plexus) — a capillary with fenestrated endothelial cells in connective tissue, covered by ependymal cells that secrete CSF into the ventricular cavity; a virus in the blood is held back at the ependymal layer (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Where the barrier sits:* the choroid plexus **capillaries are leaky (fenestrated**[^ety-fenestrated]**)**, so the real wall between blood and CSF is the layer of **ependymal cells joined by tight junctions** — the **blood–CSF barrier**.[^2]

### Parameters of CSF

| Parameters | Value |
|---|---|
| **1. Volume** | **150 ml** |
| **2. Rate of production** | **550 ml/day** |
| **3. Turn over** | **3.7 times/day** |
| **4. CSF pressure** — Normal | **70–180 mm H₂O** |
| — Average | **112 mm H₂O** (**Filtration**[^ety-filtration] **= absorption**[^ety-absorption][^pre-ab]) |

- 🧠 *Check the arithmetic:* 550 ml made per day ÷ 150 ml in the system ≈ **3.7** — the whole CSF is replaced almost four times a day.[^3]

**Regulation**[^ety-regulation][^suf-ation]**:** **at the level of absorption** (**↓ absorption → ↑ CSF volume → ↑ CSF pressure**).[^4]

## 🩸 Cerebral Blood Flow (CBF)

### General properties
- **Blood supply:**
  - **From the circle of Willis**[^ety-willis].
  - **Grey matter > white matter.**
- **Function:** **Nutrition.**
- **Normal CBF:** **750 ml/min (or) 14 % of cardiac output (CO).**[^8]
- **O₂ consumption:**
  - **20 % of total body resting O₂ consumption** (**susceptible to hypoxia**[^ety-hypoxia][^pre-hypo]).
  - **Areas most susceptible to hypoxia:**
    1. **Hippocampus**[^ety-hippocampus].
    2. **Thalamus**[^ety-thalamus].
    3. **Inferior colliculus**[^ety-colliculus].
    4. **Basal ganglia.**[^9]

> [!note] Note
> **Liver receives the highest % of cardiac output.**[^10]

- 🧠 *Why so sensitive:* the brain is 2 % of body weight but burns 20 % of the resting O₂, has almost no stores of O₂ or glucose, and depends on aerobic metabolism — consciousness is lost within about 10 s of stopping flow.

### Factors affecting CBF

| No. | Factor | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Autoregulation**[^ety-autoregulation][^pre-auto] | **Perfusion**[^ety-perfusion][^pre-per] **pressure range of 65–140 mmHg → CBF constant** |
| 2 | **Hypercarbia**[^ety-hypercarbia][^pre-hyper] | **↑ pCO₂ → ↑ CBF** |
| 3 | **Hypothermia**[^ety-hypothermia] | **↓ by 1 °C → ↓ CBF by 7 %** |

- **Significance of hypothermia:** **therapeutic**[^ety-therapeutic] **hypothermia used in neurosurgeries to ↓ blood loss.**[^11]
- 🧠 *Why CO₂ dilates:* CO₂ crosses into the vessel wall and brain fluid, forms H⁺, and H⁺ relaxes cerebral arterioles — more flow washes the CO₂ out. That is why **hyperventilation (↓ pCO₂) shrinks the brain's blood volume** and is used briefly to lower raised intracranial pressure.[^12]

## 🧱 Blood Brain Barrier (BBB)

### Structure of BBB
1. **Endothelial**[^ety-endothelium][^pre-endo] **cell: tight junctions.**
2. **Astrocytes**[^ety-astrocyte][^suf-cyte]:
   - **Star-shaped glial cell.**
   - **Have foot processes** (end-feet wrapped round the capillaries).[^13]

![[CSF, CBF and BBB - blood-brain barrier.png]]
*Blood–brain barrier — a brain capillary (red cells inside) wrapped by the foot processes of star-shaped astrocytes (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Movement across BBB

| **Substances able to cross BBB** | **Substances unable to cross BBB** |
|---|---|
| 1. **Lipid soluble substances: steroid**[^ety-steroid] **hormones** | 1. **Water soluble substances: protein hormones** |
| 2. **Gases: O₂, CO₂, nitrogen** | 2. **Ions: H⁺** |
| 3. **Nutrients: glucose** (via transporters **GLUT-1**[^ety-glut]) | 3. **Neurotransmitters: dopamine** |
| 4. **Water: via aquaporin**[^ety-aquaporin][^pre-aqua] **4 channels** | — |

- 🧠 *The rule:* small and **fat-soluble** = free entry; **water-soluble** or **charged** = no entry **unless a transporter** carries it (glucose by GLUT-1, L-DOPA by an amino-acid carrier).[^14]

### Applied aspect
- **Caisson's**[^ety-caisson] **disease:** **↑ levels of N₂ in brain.**[^15]
- **Neuromyelitis optica**[^ety-neuromyelitis][^suf-itis] **(Devic's**[^ety-devic] **disease):** **autoantibodies produced against aquaporin 4 → demyelination**[^ety-demyelination][^pre-de].[^16]
- **L-Dopa can cross BBB → used in treatment of Parkinson's**[^ety-parkinson] (dopamine itself cannot).

### Gatekeepers of BBB

**P-glycoprotein**[^ety-glycoprotein][^pre-glyco] — **channel which filters drugs crossing the BBB** (pumps them from the endothelial cell back into the blood).

![[CSF, CBF and BBB - P-glycoprotein gatekeeper.png]]
*Gatekeeper of the BBB — P-glycoprotein on the endothelial cell (blood side below, brain side above) catches drug molecules that have entered the cell and pumps them back into the blood (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Significance:**
- **P-glycoprotein blockers → concentrate drug in brain.**
  - **Used in Rx of brain neoplasms**[^ety-neoplasm][^pre-neo][^suf-plasm].[^17]

### Circumventricular organs[^ety-circumventricular][^pre-circum]
- **BBB is absent.**
- **Mnemonic: SOAP**
  - **S**ub **fornicial organ (SFO)**[^ety-subfornical].
  - **O**rgan **vasculosum of lamina terminalis (OVLT)**[^ety-ovlt].
  - **A**rea **postrema (AP)**[^ety-area-postrema].
  - **P**osterior **pituitary**[^ety-pituitary].

![[CSF, CBF and BBB - circumventricular organs.png]]
*Circumventricular organs on a mid-sagittal MRI — SFO (subfornical organ), OVLT (organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis), ME (median eminence), NH (neurohypophysis), PG (pineal gland) and AP (area postrema) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Significance:**
- **Allows passage of protein hormones** (so the brain can "taste" the blood).
- **Eg: Angiotensin**[^ety-angiotensin] **II acts on:**
  - **SFO → ↑ thirst.**
  - **AP → ↑ BP.**[^18]

### Munro Kellie doctrine[^ety-doctrine]

> [!info] **Volume of CSF + Volume of CBF + Volume of brain tissue = Constant.**
> - **A rise in any one of these compresses the other two.**

```
Eg:  (↑) CSF ──┬──► ↓ Blood flow
               └──► Compresses brain tissue
```

- 🧠 *Plain words:* the adult skull cannot stretch, so a growing haematoma, tumour, oedema or blocked CSF can only make room by squeezing out CSF and venous blood; once those reserves are used up, intracranial pressure shoots up and brain tissue is compressed or herniates.[^19]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> The brain is a **high-demand organ in a rigid box behind a selective wall**. High demand: 2 % of body weight takes 14 % of the cardiac output and 20 % of the resting O₂, so flow must be held constant (autoregulation 65–140 mmHg) and is matched to metabolism (CO₂, temperature). Rigid box: CSF + blood + brain volumes are fixed (Monro–Kellie), so CSF production is steady and the body regulates CSF **at the level of absorption**. Selective wall: endothelial tight junctions, astrocyte end-feet and the P-glycoprotein pump admit only lipid-soluble molecules, gases and carrier-borne nutrients — except at the circumventricular organs, deliberately left open so the brain can sense blood-borne hormones and toxins.
>
> Nearly every clinical question here is one of these three ideas in disguise: a CSF leak or LP removes the cushion; a raised volume in the box compresses the rest; a drug or toxin is or is not let through the barrier.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> 550 ml/day of CSF is secreted by the choroid plexus and absorbed by arachnoid villi at a matching rate, keeping 150 ml at 70–180 mm H₂O; CBF stays at 750 ml/min despite BP swings between 65 and 140 mmHg; the BBB keeps the neuronal environment stable.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A slowly expanding mass or obstructed CSF outflow first displaces CSF into the spinal canal and squeezes venous blood out (Monro–Kellie compensation) — ICP stays nearly normal and symptoms are mild (morning headache). A small CSF leak causes postural headache; a dural tear after skull-base fracture causes clear rhinorrhoea that is β₂-transferrin positive.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Once the buffer is used up, small extra volumes cause steep rises in ICP: severe headache, vomiting, papilloedema, falling consciousness, Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing) and herniation. Below an autoregulation limit, CBF falls with BP and the most vulnerable areas (hippocampus, thalamus, inferior colliculus, basal ganglia) infarct first.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → added volume (bleed, tumour, oedema, hydrocephalus) → CSF and venous blood displaced (compensation) → compliance exhausted → steep ICP rise → ↓ cerebral perfusion pressure → ischaemia → herniation
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Brief hyperventilation (↓ pCO₂ → ↓ CBF → ↓ blood volume), CSF drainage, osmotherapy and therapeutic hypothermia all work by removing volume from the box or lowering demand. LP must not be done when a mass raises ICP, because draining CSF from below can provoke herniation.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **CSF rhinorrhoea / otorrhoea:** "Clear salty fluid from the nose after head injury", "halo sign on gauze", "β₂-transferrin positive" → dural tear (anterior or middle cranial fossa fracture).
>
> **Post-LP headache:** "Headache on sitting up, relieved lying flat, 1–2 days after spinal anaesthesia" → CSF leak through the puncture → treat with an epidural blood patch if it persists.
>
> **Meningitis CSF:** "↑ protein, ↓ glucose (< 2/3 of blood glucose), neutrophils" → bacterial infection.
>
> **Caisson's disease:** "Diver surfacing too fast — joint pain, confusion, paralysis" → nitrogen bubbles (decompression sickness).
>
> **Neuromyelitis optica:** "Optic neuritis + long transverse myelitis, anti-AQP4 antibody" → Devic's disease.
>
> **Area postrema:** "Chemoreceptor trigger zone, vomiting from blood-borne toxins/drugs" → circumventricular organ without BBB.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal fourth = aqueductal stenosis; all ventricles dilated = communicating hydrocephalus (absorption failure at arachnoid villi).
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> CSF pressure > 180 mm H₂O = raised; CBF autoregulation lost below ~65 or above ~140 mmHg MAP; CSF glucose < 2/3 of plasma suggests bacterial/TB infection.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Thinking CSF volume is controlled by its production**
>
> **What students think:** If CSF pressure rises, the choroid plexus makes less.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** CSF secretion is an active, nearly constant process that barely changes with pressure. Absorption through the arachnoid villi is pressure-dependent (more pressure → more absorption), so the page's rule is "regulation at the level of absorption" — and hydrocephalus usually means impaired flow or absorption, not over-production.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Tap runs steadily; the drain decides the level."
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up what is higher and lower in CSF**
>
> **What students think:** CSF is just plasma without protein.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** CSF is a secretion, not a filtrate: Cl⁻ and Mg²⁺ are **higher** than in plasma, osmolality and bicarbonate are about **equal**, and glucose (≈ 2/3) and protein are **lower** (K⁺ and Ca²⁺ are also lower).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "CSF is salty with chloride and magnesium, sweet and protein-poor."
>
> **Trap 3: Saying the kidney or brain gets the largest share of cardiac output**
>
> **What students think:** The brain or the kidney receives the highest percentage of cardiac output.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page's note: the **liver** receives the highest share (≈ 25–28 %, portal + hepatic artery); the kidneys receive slightly less (≈ 20–25 %) but the most **per gram**; the brain gets about 14 %.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Highest total = liver; highest per gram = kidney (carotid body highest of all)."
>
> **Trap 4: Thinking circumventricular organs are a defect**
>
> **What students think:** Areas without a BBB are weak points of no use.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** They are windows built on purpose: the SFO and OVLT sense angiotensin II and plasma osmolality (thirst, ADH), the area postrema detects toxins (vomiting) and angiotensin II (↑ BP), and the posterior pituitary and median eminence release hormones into the blood.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "SOAP — Sense Or Act on Plasma."
>
> **Trap 5: Believing all small molecules cross freely**
>
> **What students think:** H⁺ and dopamine are small, so they cross the BBB.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Charge, not size, decides. H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ cross very slowly, which is why central chemoreceptors respond to blood CO₂ (which crosses and makes H⁺ in the CSF) rather than to blood H⁺. Dopamine, a polar catecholamine, has no carrier; L-DOPA rides the large neutral amino-acid transporter.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Fat-soluble or carried = in; charged and uncarried = out."

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. CSF function **lubrication & cushion**; made by **ependymal cells of the choroid plexus** (glial cells that line it and secrete CSF).
> 2. CSF **volume 150 ml**, **production 550 ml/day**, **turnover 3.7 times/day**, **pressure 70–180 mm H₂O (average 112)**.
> 3. CSF regulated **at the level of absorption** — ↓ absorption → ↑ volume → ↑ pressure.
> 4. Pathway: lateral ventricles → **foramen of Monro** → III ventricle → **aqueduct of Sylvius** → IV ventricle → **Luschka & Magendie** → subarachnoid space → **arachnoid villi** → **venous sinuses**.
> 5. CSF vs plasma: **higher Cl⁻, Mg²⁺**; **equal osmolality, HCO₃⁻**; **lower glucose (2/3) and protein**; ↑ protein = **infection**.
> 6. CSF leak marker = **β₂-transferrin**; **rhinorrhoea** (nose) and **otorrhoea** (ear).
> 7. Post-LP headache: CSF loss → cushion lost → **traction on brain** → headache.
> 8. CBF **750 ml/min = 14 % of CO**; grey > white matter; supply from the **circle of Willis**; brain uses **20 % of resting O₂**.
> 9. Most hypoxia-sensitive: **hippocampus, thalamus, inferior colliculus, basal ganglia**. **Liver** gets the highest % of CO.
> 10. **Autoregulation 65–140 mmHg**; **↑ pCO₂ → ↑ CBF**; **↓ 1 °C → ↓ CBF 7 %** (therapeutic hypothermia).
> 11. BBB = **endothelial tight junctions + astrocyte foot processes**; crosses: lipid-soluble (steroids), gases, glucose (**GLUT-1**), water (**AQP4**); does not: **protein hormones, H⁺, dopamine**.
> 12. **Caisson's** (↑ N₂), **Devic's NMO** (anti-AQP4 → demyelination), **L-DOPA crosses** (Parkinson's); gatekeeper **P-glycoprotein** — blockers concentrate drugs (brain tumours).
> 13. **CVOs (no BBB) — SOAP**: SFO, OVLT, area postrema, posterior pituitary; angiotensin II → SFO (thirst), AP (BP). **Monro–Kellie:** CSF + blood + brain = constant.

[^1]: CSF also gives **buoyancy** — a 1400 g brain weighs only about 50 g in its CSF bath — and removes metabolites. About two-thirds of CSF is secreted by the choroid plexuses; the rest comes from the ependymal lining and brain interstitial fluid.
[^2]: The choroid plexus epithelium is modified ependyma; its tight junctions form the blood–CSF barrier. In the rest of the brain the barrier lies at the capillary endothelium (blood–brain barrier) instead.
[^3]: Guyton: about 150 ml of CSF, of which only ~25 ml lies in the ventricles; production ≈ 500 ml/day (≈ 0.35 ml/min). Lumbar pressure in the lying (lateral decubitus) position is 70–180 mm H₂O (about 5–13 mmHg); Ganong gives an average of 112 mm H₂O, at which filtration (formation) equals absorption.
[^4]: Arachnoid villi act as one-way valves that open when CSF pressure exceeds venous pressure by a few mm H₂O (absorption starts at ≈ 68 mm H₂O); absorption rises steeply with pressure while formation hardly changes — hence regulation at absorption.
[^5]: Normal CSF protein is about 15–45 mg/dL (plasma ≈ 7 g/dL). Protein also rises in Guillain–Barré syndrome (albuminocytological dissociation), tumours and spinal block (Froin syndrome); bacterial and tubercular meningitis also lower CSF glucose. The page writes "mg⁻"; the ion is **Mg²⁺**. Textbooks give CSF bicarbonate slightly below plasma (≈ 22 vs 25 mmol/L) — "equal" on the page.
[^6]: β₂-transferrin (asialo-transferrin) is made when brain neuraminidase strips sialic acid from transferrin; apart from CSF it is found only in perilymph and aqueous humour, so it is the confirmatory test for CSF rhinorrhoea or otorrhoea. β-trace protein is an alternative marker.
[^7]: Post-dural puncture headache is postural (worse upright, relieved lying), usually begins within 48–72 h, and is commoner with large cutting needles; pencil-point (atraumatic) needles reduce it, and an epidural blood patch treats persistent cases.
[^8]: Guyton: normal CBF ≈ 50–65 ml per 100 g per minute, ≈ 750–900 ml/min in total, about 15 % of the resting cardiac output; grey matter receives roughly 3–4 times the flow of white matter per gram. The brain is about 2 % of body weight.
[^9]: Selectively vulnerable neurons include the CA1 pyramidal cells of the hippocampus, cerebellar Purkinje cells, cortical layers 3, 5 and 6, the basal ganglia and the thalamus. The inferior colliculus has the highest blood flow and glucose use per gram in the brain, which is why it appears in the page's list.
[^10]: Ganong's resting distribution: liver ≈ 27 %, kidneys ≈ 23 %, skeletal muscle ≈ 16 %, brain ≈ 14 %, heart ≈ 4 %. Per 100 g of tissue the kidney (and highest of all, the carotid body) receives the most.
[^11]: Cooling lowers the cerebral metabolic rate by about 6–7 % per °C, and flow falls in step. Its main use is **neuroprotection** — less O₂ demand during cardiac surgery with circulatory arrest, after cardiac arrest and in neonatal hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy; the page gives "to ↓ blood loss" as the neurosurgical significance.
[^12]: Between a PaCO₂ of about 20 and 80 mmHg, CBF changes by roughly 2–4 % per mmHg. Severe hypoxia (PaO₂ < 50 mmHg) also dilates cerebral vessels. In chronic hypertension the autoregulation curve shifts to the right, so a "normal" BP can underperfuse the brain.
[^13]: The BBB is a neurovascular unit: endothelial tight junctions (claudin-5, occludin), a basement membrane, pericytes and astrocyte end-feet; the astrocytes induce the endothelium to form tight junctions and carry the aquaporin-4 water channels.
[^14]: GLUT-1 is an insulin-independent glucose transporter in the BBB endothelium; its genetic deficiency (De Vivo disease) causes infantile seizures treated with a ketogenic diet. CO₂ crosses the BBB freely while H⁺ and HCO₃⁻ do not, the basis of the central chemoreceptor response.
[^15]: In Caisson's disease (decompression sickness), nitrogen dissolved in tissues at high pressure comes out as bubbles when the diver or tunnel worker surfaces too fast, blocking vessels in joints ("the bends"), lungs and spinal cord. At depth, the lipid-soluble N₂ that has crossed into the brain also causes nitrogen narcosis ("rapture of the deep").
[^16]: The anti-aquaporin-4 IgG (NMO-IgG) was described in 2004; NMO typically causes optic neuritis and longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis (≥ 3 vertebral segments), and is treated differently from multiple sclerosis.
[^17]: P-glycoprotein (MDR1, ABCB1) is an ATP-binding cassette efflux pump on the luminal membrane of brain capillary endothelium that pumps many lipophilic drugs (and anticancer agents) back into the blood. P-gp inhibitors to increase CNS drug delivery — e.g. for brain tumours — are largely experimental.
[^18]: The full list of circumventricular organs also includes the **median eminence** and the **pineal gland** (both labelled on the page's MRI as ME and PG; NH = neurohypophysis). The area postrema is the chemoreceptor trigger zone for vomiting; the OVLT carries the osmoreceptors for thirst and ADH; angiotensin II at the area postrema raises sympathetic outflow and BP.
[^19]: Named after Alexander Monro *secundus* (1783) and George Kellie (1824). [^ety-subfornical]: **subfornical organ** — Latin *sub-*, "under" + *fornix*, "arch, vault" → the organ under the fornix, at the roof of the third ventricle; senses angiotensin II → thirst. The page spells it "sub fornicial".
[^ety-ovlt]: **organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT)** — Latin *organum*, "organ, instrument" + *vasculosum*, "full of small vessels" + *lamina*, "thin plate" + *terminalis*, "at the end" → the vessel-rich organ in the thin end-plate of the third ventricle; senses plasma osmolality.
[^ety-area-postrema]: **area postrema** — Latin *area*, "open space" + *postremus*, "last, hindmost" → the hindmost area, at the lower end of the fourth ventricle floor; the chemoreceptor trigger zone for vomiting.
[^ety-pituitary]: **pituitary** — Latin *pituita*, "phlegm, slime" → Galen thought the gland drained mucus from the brain; the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis) has no BBB so its hormones enter the blood.
[^ety-angiotensin]: **angiotensin** — Greek *angeion*, "vessel" + Latin *tensio*, "stretching, tension" + *-in* → the peptide that tenses vessels (vasoconstrictor); angiotensin II also drives thirst at the SFO.
[^ety-doctrine]: **doctrine** — Latin *doctrina*, "teaching" (*docere*, to teach) → a principle taught as established; here the Monro–Kellie principle of the fixed intracranial volume.
