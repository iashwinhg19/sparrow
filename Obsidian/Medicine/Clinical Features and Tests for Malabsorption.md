# 🔬 Clinical Features and Tests for Malabsorption

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Malabsorption[^ety-malabsorption][^pre-mal][^suf-tion] is recognised by what the body loses — fat in the stool, and the vitamins and minerals that travel with it — and confirmed by tests that each interrogate one site: the lumen (fecal fat), the mucosa (D-xylose), the brush border or colonic bacteria (breath tests), the lymphatics (fecal α-1 antitrypsin) and the ileum, stomach and pancreas (Schilling test).
> The questions this note answers: *which deficiency sign points to which nutrient*, *which test is gold standard for what*, and *why each test gives false results*.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Three steps, one site per test
The page's three steps are the whole method: confirm the diagnosis (clinically or by investigation), confirm global vs selective, confirm the etiology. Every test answers "is *this* site working?", so a positive test names a site, and the false-positive list of each test is simply the list of ways the marker can go missing *without* that site being diseased.

```
 STEP 1  Confirm malabsorption ──► clinically (steatorrhea, weight loss, deficiency signs)
                                   or fecal fat ≥ 7 g/day (GOLD STANDARD)
 STEP 2  Global vs selective ───► D-xylose (mucosa intact?) · breath H2 (one enzyme? bacteria?)
 STEP 3  Etiology ──────────────► Schilling phases (IF / antibiotics / pancreatic enzymes / ileum)
                                   stool elastase (pancreas) · α-1 antitrypsin (lymphangiectasia) · biopsy
```

## 🧪 Investigations

**FAT MALABSORPTION**
- Qualitative test: Sudan III[^ety-sudan].
- Quantitative test **(Gold standard)**: Fecal fat excretion **≥ 7 g/day**.[^4]

> [!success] Note
> Fecal fat excretion test is the **gold standard investigation for malabsorption.**

**CARBOHYDRATE MALABSORPTION**

**1. D-Xylose test**[^ety-xylose]
- Best and most specific test.
- Composition of D-xylose: Pentose[^pre-pent] monosaccharide[^ety-saccharide][^pre-mono].
- Used to assess intact small intestinal mucosa[^ety-mucosa] (D-xylose absorbed here).
- Procedure:

```
 25 g of D-xylose given orally after fasting (early morning)
                 │
                 ▼
 Collect urine sample (ENTIRE volume passed) after 4–6 hours & check D-xylose levels
                 │
      ┌──────────┴──────────┐
   > 4.5 g               < 4.5 g (Positive)
      │                      │
 Interpretation:        Interpretation:
 Normal                 Small intestinal mucosal pathology
```

- False positives seen in (Etiology is not in small intestine):

| Clinical scenario | Reason for false positive |
|---|---|
| Renal failure | Defective excretion[^ety-excretion][^pre-ex] |
| Rapid intestinal transit (Hyperthyroidism, Vomiting) | Reduced time for absorption in small intestine |
| SIBO (Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth) | Organisms utilise D-xylose → Reduced absorption |
| Ascites | Sequestration[^ety-sequestration] of D-xylose into the third space → Reduced absorption |

**2. Lactose tolerance test:** Obsolete.

**3. Lactose H2 breath test**
- Done for lactase deficiency.
- Deficiency of lactase → Accumulation of lactose in large intestine → Bacterial action on lactose → H2 production.
- Diagnosis: ↑ in breath H2 **> 20 ppm.**

**4. Lactulose H2 breath test**[^ety-lactulose]
- Metabolised in large intestine to produce H2.
- H2 produced:
  - Within 90 minutes (Early peaking): SIBO.
  - > 90 minutes: Normal physiology.

**OBSOLETE TESTS FOR MALABSORPTION**

| Test | Etiology |
|---|---|
| Acid steatocrit test | Fat malabsorption |
| C-14 Triolein breath test | Fat malabsorption |
| C-13 Egg white breath test (Phenol and p-cresol in urine) | Protein malabsorption |

> [!note] Note
> - Stool elastase, chymotrypsin: Pancreatic insufficiency.
> - Fecal α-1 antitrypsin:[^pre-anti] Primary intestinal lymphangiectasia[^ety-lymphangiectasia][^suf-ectasia].
> - NIRA (Near infrared reflectance analysis): Newer test for malabsorption.
> - Diseases diagnosed on biopsy:[^ety-biopsy] 1. Whipple's disease[^ety-whipple]. 2. Agammaglobulinemia[^suf-emia]. 3. Abetalipoproteinemia.

**Schilling test**[^ety-schilling][^5]

Phase I:
```
 1000 µg of unlabelled Vitamin B12 I.M to saturate stores
                 │
                 ▼
 Give 1 µg of oral radiolabelled Vitamin B12
                 │
                 ▼
 Check Vitamin B12 level in urine
        ┌────────┴────────┐
      < 7 %             7–10 %
        │                 │
  Positive test         Normal
  (malabsorption etiology)
```

Phase II:
```
                         Administer
        ┌────────────────────┼────────────────────┐
  Intrinsic factor      Antibiotics         Pancreatic enzymes
        │                    │                     │
  If > 10 % urine B12   If > 10 %             If > 10 %
        │                    │                     │
  Pernicious anemia (IF)    SIBO            Chronic pancreatitis
```

After phase I & II, if urine B12 < 7 % = Ileal disease.

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Malabsorption is diagnosed by *what is lost* and localised by *which marker fails to appear*. Fat is the most demanding nutrient to absorb, so stool fat (≥ 7 g/day) is the gold standard for the diagnosis itself; every other test is a probe of one site — D-xylose for the mucosa, breath hydrogen for a missing brush-border enzyme or misplaced bacteria, fecal α-1 antitrypsin for leaking lymph, the Schilling phases for stomach, bacteria, pancreas and ileum.
>
> Because each marker has to be absorbed, carried, and excreted, each test is falsely positive whenever the marker goes missing for a reason outside the site under test (kidney failure, rapid transit, bacteria eating it, ascites hiding it). Learn the test as "site + marker + where the marker can be lost" and the false-positive tables write themselves.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Fat, carbohydrate and protein are absorbed in the proximal small intestine; bile acids, vitamin B12 and Mg2+ in the ileum; calcium binds oxalate in the lumen and the complex is excreted. Stool fat < 7 g/day, D-xylose urine > 4.5 g, breath H2 < 20 ppm, Schilling urine B12 7–10 %.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Absorption falls but reserves hide it. Bloating and dyspepsia after carbohydrate meals, a mildly bulky stool, borderline fecal fat. Micronutrients leak first: an unexplained iron or folate deficiency (proximal disease) or B12 deficiency (distal disease), early perifollicular bleeding or easy bruising. Weight is kept; there is no pain, no fever, no constipation — the three symptoms the page says malabsorption does *not* cause.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Steatorrhea with fecal fat ≥ 7 g/day and weight loss; osmotic diarrhoea for > 4 weeks that improves on fasting; protein loss with edema and ascites; the deficiency syndromes appear in full — tetany and fractures (vitamin D/Ca), night blindness and xerophthalmia (vitamin A), neuropathy and ataxia (B12/E), glossitis and cheilosis (B12), growth retardation in a coeliac child, calcium-oxalate stones from enteric hyperoxaluria.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → [mucosal, luminal or lymphatic lesion] → food-related bloating → isolated micronutrient deficiency → steatorrhea + weight loss → multi-system deficiency syndrome (bone, skin, nerve, eye, kidney)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> At stage 2 a breath test or D-xylose picks the site and a diet or enzyme fixes it; at stage 3 you need the full ladder — fecal fat to prove it, D-xylose to split mucosa from lumen, Schilling or stool elastase to name the organ, biopsy for the three diseases that are 100 % diagnostic on histology — and replacement of every vitamin already lost.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Mucosal disease (coeliac, Whipple, tropical sprue):** "steatorrhea + D-xylose urine < 4.5 g" → the pentose is absorbed by passive mucosal uptake with no enzyme needed, so a low value means the mucosa itself is damaged.
>
> **Pancreatic insufficiency:** "steatorrhea + *normal* D-xylose + low stool elastase/chymotrypsin; Schilling corrected by pancreatic enzymes" → lipase missing, mucosa fine.
>
> **SIBO:** "lactulose breath H2 peak within 90 minutes; D-xylose falsely low; Schilling corrected by antibiotics" → bacteria high in the small bowel consume the markers and deconjugate bile salts.
>
> **Lactase deficiency:** "bloating after milk, lactose breath H2 > 20 ppm, acidic stool" → selective brush-border enzyme loss; lactose tolerance test is obsolete.
>
> **Pernicious anaemia:** "Schilling < 7 % corrected by intrinsic factor" → gastric, not intestinal.
>
> **Ileal disease (Crohn's, TB, resection):** "Schilling still < 7 % after IF, antibiotics and pancreatic enzymes" → the absorptive site itself is gone.
>
> **Primary intestinal lymphangiectasia:** "edema + ascites + hypoalbuminemia + elevated fecal α-1 antitrypsin" → protein-losing enteropathy.
>
> **Enteric hyperoxaluria:** "fat malabsorption + calcium-oxalate kidney stones" → calcium soaps leave oxalate free to be absorbed in the colon.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Perifollicular haemorrhage with corkscrew hairs = vitamin C. Diffuse hyperpigmentation = B12 or niacin. Acral/periorificial dermatitis = zinc / essential fatty acids. Follicular hyperkeratosis and xerophthalmia = vitamin A. Carpopedal spasm = tetany from vitamin D / calcium loss. Diseases diagnosed on biopsy: Whipple's (PAS-positive macrophages), agammaglobulinemia (no plasma cells), abetalipoproteinemia (vacuolated enterocytes).
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Fecal fat ≥ 7 g/day (steatorrhea) · D-xylose urine < 4.5 g after 25 g dose (positive) · breath H2 > 20 ppm (positive) · lactulose H2 peak < 90 min (SIBO) · Schilling urine B12 < 7 % (positive), 7–10 % (normal), > 10 % after a phase II agent (that agent's organ is the cause) · osmotic diarrhoea > 4 weeks (chronic).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Treating a low D-xylose as proof of mucosal disease**
>
> **What students think:** D-xylose < 4.5 g in the urine always means coeliac disease or another mucosal lesion.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The urinary value only reflects the mucosa if the sugar is absorbed, stays in the circulation and is excreted by the kidney. Renal failure (not excreted), rapid transit in hyperthyroidism or vomiting (no time to absorb), SIBO (bacteria eat it) and ascites (sequestered in the third space) all lower the urine value with a perfectly healthy mucosa.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Before calling the test positive, run the four-item checklist — kidney, transit, bacteria, ascites — and remember a *normal* D-xylose with steatorrhea points to the pancreas or the lymphatics.
>
> **Trap 2: Confusing the lactose and lactulose breath tests**
>
> **What students think:** Both measure hydrogen, so both diagnose lactase deficiency.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Lactose is a dietary sugar that *should* be absorbed; hydrogen appears only if lactase is missing (> 20 ppm = lactase deficiency). Lactulose is a synthetic sugar that is *never* absorbed and always reaches bacteria; what matters is *when* the hydrogen appears — within 90 minutes means bacteria are in the small bowel (SIBO), after 90 minutes is normal colonic fermentation.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Lactose asks "how much H2?"; lactulose asks "how early?".
>
> **Trap 3: Reading the Schilling test as a single number**
>
> **What students think:** A low urinary B12 means pernicious anaemia.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Phase I only says B12 is malabsorbed (< 7 %). The cause is found in phase II by what *corrects* it: intrinsic factor → pernicious anaemia; antibiotics → SIBO; pancreatic enzymes → chronic pancreatitis (pancreatic proteases are needed to free B12 from R-binder); nothing corrects it → ileal disease.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Memorise the four answers to "what fixed it?" and the two thresholds (< 7 % positive, 7–10 % normal, > 10 % corrected).
>
> **Trap 4: Letting abdominal pain, fever or constipation steer you to malabsorption**
>
> **What students think:** Any chronic GI complaint with weight loss is malabsorption until proven otherwise.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page is explicit that abdominal pain, constipation and fever are *not* features of malabsorption. Their presence points to the differentials — intestinal TB (weight loss, long-standing diarrhoea, edema/ascites, often with fever and pain) or an inflammatory diarrhoea — and nephrotic syndrome mimics the protein loss without any gut disease.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Pain, fever, constipation → look elsewhere first; check urine protein before blaming the gut for low albumin.
>
> **Trap 5: Ordering an obsolete test**
>
> **What students think:** The lactose tolerance test, acid steatocrit, C-14 triolein and C-13 egg-white breath tests are current options.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** All four are listed as obsolete; they have been replaced by the lactose H2 breath test, the quantitative fecal fat test and stool elastase. Exam options that name them are usually the distractor.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Current = fecal fat (gold standard), Sudan III (qualitative), D-xylose, lactose/lactulose H2, stool elastase, fecal α-1 antitrypsin, biopsy, NIRA (newer).

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)
> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Three steps: confirm malabsorption (clinically / by investigation) → global vs selective → etiology.
> 2. Osmotic diarrhoea = **most consistent symptom**: small intestine, > 4 weeks, ↑ stool osmotic gap, improves on fasting.
> 3. Cutaneous signs: B12/niacin → **hyperpigmentation**; vitamin C → **perifollicular haemorrhage, curly/spiral hair**; vitamin K → bruising, ecchymoses; Zn/EFA → acrodermatitis; vitamin A → follicular hyperkeratosis.
> 4. **Musculoskeletal**: tetany, fracture, bone pain from impaired vitamin D and Ca2+ absorption; coeliac disease → **growth retardation**.
> 5. Anaemia by site: distal pathology → B12 deficiency; proximal pathology → folate/iron deficiency.
> 6. Kidney stones: fatty acid + Ca2+ → free oxalate → absorbed in colon → oxaluria → calcium-oxalate stones.
> 7. **Not** seen in malabsorption: abdominal pain, constipation, fever. Differentials: TB, nephrotic syndrome.
> 8. Fecal fat excretion **≥ 7 g/day = gold standard** for malabsorption; Sudan III is the qualitative test.
> 9. **D-xylose** (pentose monosaccharide): best and most specific test of mucosal integrity; 25 g orally fasting → urine 4–6 h; **< 4.5 g positive**.
> 10. D-xylose false positives: renal failure, rapid transit (hyperthyroidism, vomiting), SIBO, ascites.
> 11. Lactose H2 breath test positive at **> 20 ppm**; lactulose H2 peak **within 90 min = SIBO**; lactose tolerance test obsolete.
> 12. Obsolete: acid steatocrit, C-14 triolein (fat), C-13 egg white (protein). Newer: NIRA.
> 13. Stool elastase/chymotrypsin → pancreatic insufficiency; fecal α-1 antitrypsin → primary intestinal lymphangiectasia; biopsy-diagnostic: Whipple's, agammaglobulinemia, abetalipoproteinemia.
> 14. **Schilling**: 1000 µg B12 IM, then 1 µg oral radiolabelled; urine **< 7 % positive, 7–10 % normal**; phase II corrected by IF → pernicious anaemia, antibiotics → SIBO, pancreatic enzymes → chronic pancreatitis; still < 7 % → ileal disease.

```

A system-by-system table drawn as rows with icons: Gastrointestinal — osmotic diarrhoea (small intestine, > 4 weeks, ↑ stool osmotic gap, improves on fasting, most consistent symptom), fat malabsorption → steatorrhea, carbohydrate → bloating and dyspeptic symptoms, protein → foul smelling stools, ascites, edema, unexplained weight loss; Musculoskeletal — fat malabsorption (impaired vitamin D and Ca2+) → tetany, fracture, bone pain; Cutaneous — B12/niacin → hyperpigmentation, vitamin C → perifollicular haemorrhage, curly hair, spiral hair, vitamin K → bruisability, ecchymoses, Zn & essential fatty acid → acrodermatitis, vitamin A → follicular hyperkeratosis; Miscellaneous — coeliac disease → growth retardation; anaemia: distal pathology → B12 deficiency, proximal pathology → folate/iron deficiency; B12/E → peripheral neuropathy, ataxia; B12 → glossitis, cheilosis, stomatitis; A → night blindness, xerophthalmia; kidney stones: normal oxalate + Ca2+ → excreted, malabsorption fatty acid + Ca2+ → free oxalate → absorbed in colon → oxaluria → Ca-oxalate stones. A grey box: 'not seen in malabsorption: abdominal pain, constipation, fever; differentials: TB (weight loss, long-standing diarrhoea, edema/ascites), nephrotic syndrome (protein loss)'.

> **Panel 2 — 'Investigations: fat and the gold standard'.** Qualitative test Sudan III; quantitative test (gold standard) fecal fat excretion ≥ 7 g/day; banner 'fecal fat excretion test = gold standard investigation for malabsorption'. Obsolete tests mini-table: acid steatocrit (fat), C-14 triolein breath test (fat), C-13 egg white breath test with phenol and p-cresol in urine (protein). Notes: stool elastase, chymotrypsin → pancreatic insufficiency; fecal α-1 antitrypsin → primary intestinal lymphangiectasia; NIRA (near infrared reflectance analysis) newer test; diseases diagnosed on biopsy: Whipple's disease, agammaglobulinemia, abetalipoproteinemia.
>
> **Panel 3 — 'Investigations: carbohydrate'.** D-xylose test: best and most specific, pentose monosaccharide, assesses intact small intestinal mucosa; procedure flow 25 g D-xylose orally after fasting (early morning) → collect entire urine volume after 4–6 hours → > 4.5 g normal / < 4.5 g positive (small intestinal mucosal pathology); false-positive table: renal failure (defective excretion), rapid intestinal transit — hyperthyroidism, vomiting (reduced time for absorption), SIBO (organisms utilise D-xylose), ascites (sequestration into the third space). Lactose tolerance test: obsolete. Lactose H2 breath test: lactase deficiency → lactose accumulates in large intestine → bacterial action → H2; positive > 20 ppm. Lactulose H2 breath test: metabolised in large intestine → H2 within 90 minutes (early peaking) = SIBO, > 90 minutes = normal.

> **Panel 4 — 'Schilling test'.** Phase I flow: 1000 µg unlabelled vitamin B12 IM to saturate stores → 1 µg oral radiolabelled vitamin B12 → check urine B12 → < 7 % positive (malabsorption etiology) / 7–10 % normal. Phase II: administer intrinsic factor → if > 10 % urine B12 → pernicious anaemia (IF); antibiotics → if > 10 % → SIBO; pancreatic enzymes → if > 10 % → chronic pancreatitis. Footer line: 'after phase I & II, if urine B12 < 7 % = ileal disease'.

[^1]: The spiral or "corkscrew" hairs of scurvy are coiled hairs with perifollicular bleeding; vitamin C is needed for the hydroxylation of collagen, so the hair shaft and the follicle's capillaries are both weak.
[^2]: The page spells it "cheliosis"; the standard spelling is cheilosis (Greek *cheilos*, lip) — fissuring and scaling of the lips and angles of the mouth.
[^3]: This is enteric hyperoxaluria. Normally luminal calcium binds oxalate into an insoluble complex that is lost in stool; in fat malabsorption the calcium is taken by unabsorbed fatty acids as soaps, so oxalate stays free, is absorbed in the colon and is excreted by the kidney, where it crystallises with calcium.
[^4]: The quantitative test is the 72-hour fecal fat collection on a 100 g/day fat diet; the diarrhoea note gives the full procedure (normal < 7 g/day; steatorrhea > 7 g/day over days 3, 4 and 5, or excretion ≥ 7 % of intake).
[^5]: The Schilling test is now rarely available because the radiolabelled vitamin B12 is no longer manufactured in most countries; it stays in the syllabus because its logic (what corrects the malabsorption) still explains the differential of B12 deficiency.

[^ety-malabsorption]: **malabsorption** — Latin *male*, "badly" + *ab-*, "away" + *sorbēre*, "to suck in" + *-tion*, the process → defective uptake of nutrients across the mucosa. **Differentiate:** *maldigestion* (luminal enzymes or bile missing: pancreas, bile acids) vs *malabsorption* (mucosa or beyond); *global* (all nutrients, mucosal disease) vs *selective* (one nutrient, one enzyme or transporter).
[^ety-etiology]: **etiology** — Greek *aitia*, "cause" + *-logia*, "study" → the cause of a disease. **Differentiate:** *etiology* (cause) → *pathogenesis* (mechanism) → *pathology* (structural result); *idiopathic* = cause unknown.
[^ety-osmotic]: **osmotic** — Greek *ōsmos*, "a push, impulse" (Thomas Graham, 1854, for the movement of solvent across a membrane) + *-ic* → driven by solute concentration; *osmotic diarrhoea* = water drawn into the lumen by unabsorbed solute, so it stops with fasting. **Differentiate:** *osmotic* (↑ osmotic gap > 100, improves on fasting, pH < 5.5) vs *secretory* (gap < 50, no change on fasting, pH > 6).
[^ety-diarrhoea]: **diarrhoea** — Greek *dia-*, "through" + *rhein*, "to flow" → a flowing-through of the bowel; stool water > 200 mL/24 h.
[^ety-steatorrhoea]: **steatorrhoea** — Greek *stear, steat-*, "tallow, fat" + *rhoia*, "flow" → fat flowing out in the stool (pale, bulky, greasy, malodorous); the hallmark of malabsorption, defined as > 7 g/day.
[^ety-dyspepsia]: **dyspepsia** — Greek *dys-*, "bad, difficult" + *pepsis*, "digestion" + *-ia* → "bad digestion", upper-abdominal discomfort, bloating and early satiety. **Differentiate:** *dyspepsia* (symptom) vs *dysphagia* (difficulty swallowing) vs *dyschezia* (painful defecation).
[^ety-ascites]: **ascites** — Greek *askites*, from *askos*, "wine-skin, leather bag" → the belly distended like a bag of fluid; in malabsorption from protein loss and low albumin.
[^ety-edema]: **edema / oedema** — Greek *oidēma*, "a swelling", from *oidein*, "to swell" → fluid in the interstitial space; here from hypoalbuminemia.
[^ety-tetany]: **tetany** — Greek *tetanos*, "stretched, rigid" (from *teinein*, "to stretch") + *-y* → sustained muscle spasm (carpopedal spasm) from low ionised calcium; in malabsorption from loss of vitamin D and calcium. Don't confuse with **tetanus** the clostridial disease.
[^ety-ecchymosis]: **ecchymosis** — Greek *ek-*, "out" + *chymos*, "juice" + *-osis*, "condition" → juice (blood) poured out into the skin; a bruise larger than 1 cm. **Differentiate by size:** *petechia* (< 3 mm) → *purpura* (3 mm–1 cm) → *ecchymosis* (> 1 cm).
[^ety-acrodermatitis]: **acrodermatitis** — Greek *akron*, "extremity, tip" + *derma*, "skin" + *-itis*, "inflammation" → inflammation of the skin of the extremities and around orifices; *acrodermatitis enteropathica* is the zinc-deficiency form ("of the intestine", because it followed malabsorption).
[^ety-hyperkeratosis]: **hyperkeratosis** — Greek *hyper-*, "over" + *keras, kerat-*, "horn" + *-osis*, "condition" → thickening of the horny layer; *follicular* hyperkeratosis plugs the hair follicles (phrynoderma, "toad skin") in vitamin A deficiency.
[^ety-coeliac]: **coeliac / celiac** — Greek *koiliakos*, "of the belly", from *koilia*, "belly, hollow"; coeliac disease = gluten-sensitive enteropathy; in children the growth retardation may be the only sign.
[^ety-ataxia]: **ataxia** — Greek *a-*, "without" + *taxis*, "order" + *-ia* → loss of co-ordination; here from vitamin B12 (dorsal column) or vitamin E (spinocerebellar) deficiency.
[^ety-glossitis]: **glossitis** — Greek *glōssa*, "tongue" + *-itis* → inflammation of the tongue; the smooth, beefy-red tongue of B12 deficiency.
[^ety-cheilosis]: **cheilosis** — Greek *cheilos*, "lip" + *-osis* → fissuring and scaling of the lips; *angular cheilitis* when it is at the corners of the mouth.
[^ety-stomatitis]: **stomatitis** — Greek *stoma, stomat-*, "mouth" + *-itis* → inflammation of the mouth lining. Don't confuse with **-stomy** a surgical opening (ileostomy).
[^ety-xerophthalmia]: **xerophthalmia** — Greek *xēros*, "dry" + *ophthalmos*, "eye" + *-ia* → dry eye from vitamin A deficiency; the sequence is night blindness → conjunctival xerosis → Bitot's spots → corneal xerosis → keratomalacia.
[^ety-sudan]: **Sudan III** — a red azo dye of the Sudan family (named by the German dye industry in the 1890s after the Sudan region, then much in the news) that dissolves in neutral fat; a stool smear stained with it shows orange-red fat globules — the qualitative fat test. Sudan IV and Oil Red O are the same idea.
[^ety-xylose]: **D-xylose** — Greek *xylon*, "wood" + *-ose*, sugar → a five-carbon sugar first obtained from wood (1881); "D" is its stereochemical series. It is absorbed by the proximal small-intestinal mucosa without any enzyme or bile, so it tests the mucosa alone.
[^ety-saccharide]: **saccharide** — Greek *sakcharon*, "sugar" (from Sanskrit *śarkarā*, "gravel, sugar") + *-ide* → a sugar unit. **Differentiate:** *monosaccharide* (one unit: glucose, galactose, fructose, xylose) → *disaccharide* (two: lactose, sucrose, maltose, trehalose; need a *disaccharidase*) → *polysaccharide* (many: starch).
[^ety-mucosa]: **mucosa** — Latin *mucosus*, "slimy" → the mucus-coated inner lining of the gut, the layer D-xylose crosses and the layer destroyed in coeliac disease, Whipple's and tropical sprue.
[^ety-excretion]: **excretion** — Latin *ex-*, "out" + *cernere*, "to sift, separate" + *-tion* → the separating-out of waste from the body (urine, stool). **Differentiate:** *excretion* (waste out of the body: kidney, stool) vs *secretion* (a useful product released by a gland: lipase, bicarbonate) vs *exudation* (fluid oozing out of vessels).
[^ety-sequestration]: **sequestration** — Latin *sequestrare*, "to set aside, place in safe keeping" (from *sequester*, a trustee) + *-tion* → a substance locked away where it cannot act; D-xylose "sequestered" in ascitic fluid never reaches the urine. Also used for dead bone (*sequestrum*) and for blood pooled in the spleen.
[^ety-lactulose]: **lactulose** — *lact-* (Latin *lac, lactis*, milk) + *-ulose* (a ketose sugar) → a synthetic disaccharide of galactose and fructose made from lactose; it cannot be digested by any human enzyme, so it always reaches bacteria — which is why the *timing* of its hydrogen peak locates the bacteria. **Differentiate:** *lactose* (natural milk sugar, digested by lactase; its breath test detects lactase *deficiency*) vs *lactulose* (never digested; its breath test detects *SIBO* by an early peak).
[^ety-lymphangiectasia]: **lymphangiectasia** — Latin *lympha*, "clear water" + Greek *angeion*, "vessel" + *ektasis*, "stretching out" + *-ia* → dilated intestinal lymphatics leaking protein-rich lymph into the lumen; fecal α-1 antitrypsin is its specific test.
[^ety-biopsy]: **biopsy** — Greek *bios*, "life" + *opsis*, "sight" → examining tissue from the living (Besnier, 1879). Don't confuse with **autopsy** (examination after death).
[^ety-whipple]: **Whipple's disease** — George Hoyt Whipple (1878–1976), American pathologist (Nobel Prize 1934 for liver therapy of anaemia), who described "intestinal lipodystrophy" in 1907; PAS-positive foamy macrophages in the lamina propria make the biopsy diagnostic.
[^ety-schilling]: **Schilling test** — Robert F. Schilling (1919–2014), American haematologist at the University of Wisconsin, who described the radiolabelled-B12 urinary excretion test in 1953.
