# ❤️ Cardiac and Smooth Muscles

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Cardiac**[^ety-cardiac][^suf-ac] and **smooth muscle** are the two **involuntary**[^ety-involuntary][^pre-in] muscles. Cardiac muscle is **striated, works as one functional syncytium and can never be tetanised**; smooth muscle is **non-striated, Ca²⁺–calmodulin driven, plastic and energy-efficient (latch bridge)**. Everything in the chapter answers:
> - ✅ How does the muscle get its Ca²⁺ and switch on? (**CICR + troponin** in the heart · **calmodulin + MLCK** in smooth muscle)
> - ✅ How does it switch off? (**SERCA / phospholamban** · **MLCP / Rho kinase**)
> - ✅ What special feature fits its job? (Frank–Starling, no tetanus · plasticity, latch bridge)

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — An Orchestra and a Hydraulic Lock

The heart is an **orchestra with one conductor**: the SA node beats time, gap junctions pass the beat instantly so every cell plays the same note together (functional syncytium), and a long rest bar after each note (the absolute refractory period) means the music can never become one continuous blast (no tetanus). Smooth muscle is a **hydraulic door-closer with a latch**: slow to move, but once latched it holds the door shut for hours on almost no energy, and it can be stretched to a new length without springing back (plasticity).

```
CARDIAC  AP → ECF Ca²⁺ in (T-tubule) → RyR → SR Ca²⁺ out (CICR) → troponin C → CONTRACTION
         SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back (brake = phospholamban; NE releases the brake) → RELAXATION
         Long ARP → no summation → NO TETANUS  ·  gap junctions → functional syncytium
SMOOTH   Ca²⁺ + calmodulin → MLCK → myosin-P → CONTRACTION
         MLCP removes P → RELAXATION  (Rho kinase blocks MLCP; fasudil blocks Rho kinase)
         no titin → PLASTICITY  ·  latch bridge → sustained tone on low ATP
```

## 🫀 Cardiac Muscle

- **Cardiac muscles are found in the heart.**

### Properties

- **Large nucleus**[^ety-nucleus].
- **Plenty of mitochondria**[^ety-mitochondria][^suf-ion] **→ to generate ATP.**[^1]
- **Histology**[^ety-histology][^suf-logy]:
  - Presence of **intercalated disc**[^ety-intercalated][^pre-inter] containing **gap junctions**.
  - Gap junctions contain proteins: **Connexins**[^ety-connexin][^pre-con].[^2]
- **Type: Striated**[^ety-striated] **muscle.**
- **Mode of contraction: Involuntary.** Needs a **pacemaker: SA node**.[^3]
- **Regulatory proteins: Troponin & Tropomyosin**[^ety-troponin].
- **2 types of myosin heavy chain:**
  - **α-myosin heavy chain → Atrium.**
  - **β-myosin heavy chain → Ventricles.**[^4]

![Cardiac muscle — branching fibres joined by intercalated discs (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/1020_Cardiac_Muscle.jpg/800px-1020_Cardiac_Muscle.jpg)
*Cardiac muscle — branched, striated fibres with central nuclei joined end to end by intercalated discs, the page's "Cardiac muscle" figure (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY).*

![Cardiac sarcomere structure — actin, myosin, troponin, tropomyosin](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Cardiac_sarcomere_structure.png/800px-Cardiac_sarcomere_structure.png)
*The cardiac sarcomere — actin with the regulatory proteins troponin (I, C, T) and tropomyosin, and myosin with its β-myosin heavy chain (Wikimedia Commons).*

### Functional syncytium[^ety-syncytium][^pre-syn]

- **Specific to cardiac muscle.**

```
Presence of gap junction
        ↓
Rapid transmission of impulses from one cell to another
        ↓
All cells contract & relax at the same time
        ↓
Generates large amount of force to pump out blood
```

🧠 The cells are separate (each has its own membrane), but because current flows freely through gap junctions they **behave** as if they were one giant cell — hence "functional", not "true", syncytium.[^5]

### Titin in cardiac muscle

- **Provides elasticity to cardiac muscle → ability to recoil.**
- **Mutation of titin: Dilated**[^ety-dilated][^pre-dis] **cardiomyopathy**[^ety-cardiomyopathy][^pre-myo][^suf-pathy] **(DCM).**[^6]

```
Mutation of titin
      ↓
Muscles stretch without recoil
      ↓
Dilated cardiac chambers (DCM)
```

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - normal heart vs DCM.png]]
*Normal heart vs dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) — in DCM the chambers are dilated and the walls thin, because titin no longer lets the muscle recoil (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Enlarged heart — hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy compared](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Illustration_of_an_enlarged_heart_(Cardiomegaly).png/800px-Illustration_of_an_enlarged_heart_(Cardiomegaly).png)
*Two kinds of enlarged heart — hypertrophic (thick walls) and dilated cardiomyopathy (big chambers, thin walls) — the second is the titin picture of the page (Wikimedia Commons).*

### Unique features

#### Length–tension relationship

- **Blood enters the chambers during diastole**[^ety-diastole][^pre-dia] **: End diastolic volume (EDV).**
- **EDV → stretches the chambers → change in length.**
- **Tension: d/t force of contraction.**

#### Frank–Starling law[^ety-frank-starling]

> [!info] **Force of contraction ∝ initial length of cardiac muscle fibre — within physiological limits.**
> More blood in (↑ EDV) → fibres start longer → stronger beat → more blood out. The heart pumps what it receives.[^7]

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - Frank-Starling pressure volume curve.png]]
*Pressure–volume relationship — pressure rises with EDV up to a plateau and then falls once the fibres are stretched beyond physiological limits (cropped from the handwritten page).*

#### Ca²⁺ induced Ca²⁺ release (CICR)

```
Action potential
      ↓
Extracellular Ca²⁺ influx
      ↓
Release of intracellular Ca²⁺ (iCa²⁺) from sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) through RyR
      ↓
Contraction
      ↓
iCa²⁺ returns back to SR by SERCA pump
      ↓
Relaxation
```

- **Extracellular**[^pre-extra] Ca²⁺ is only the trigger; the bulk comes from the **intracellular**[^pre-intra] store in the **sarcoplasmic reticulum**[^ety-sarcoplasmic][^pre-sarco][^suf-plasm] through the **RyR**[^ety-ryanodine] (ryanodine receptor), and the **SERCA**[^ety-serca] pump takes it back.[^8]

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - calcium induced calcium release.png]]
*CICR — the action potential runs down the T-tubule, opens a sarcolemmal[^suf-lemma] Ca²⁺ channel, and that Ca²⁺ opens the RyR on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, releasing the Ca²⁺ store (cropped from the handwritten page).*

#### Diad vs triad

| Muscle | Arrangement |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac** | **1 T tubule + 1 sarcoplasmic reticulum → Diad**[^ety-diad][^pre-di] **arrangement** |
| **Skeletal** (note) | **1 T tubule + 2 sarcoplasmic reticulum → Triad**[^pre-tri] **arrangement** |

🧠 Fewer SR cisterns per T tubule is one reason cardiac muscle leans on extracellular Ca²⁺ to start each beat.[^9]

#### Phospholamban and Norepinephrine

- **Phospholamban**[^ety-phospholamban][^pre-phospho] **⊣ (−) SERCA pump.**
- **NE ⊣ (−) Phospholamban (by phosphorylation**[^ety-phosphorylation]**) → ↑ Ca²⁺ storage.**

```
NE (norepinephrine)
   ⊣ (−) by phosphorylation
Phospholamban
   ⊣ (−)
SERCA pump  →  works fully  →  ↑ Ca²⁺ storage in SR
```

> [!tip] **Norepinephrine**[^ety-norepinephrine][^pre-epi]**: ↑ Ca²⁺ storage by adequate function of SERCA**
> - **Enhances relaxation → +ve lusitropy**[^ety-lusitropy][^suf-tropy].
> - **Ensures adequate Ca²⁺ for future contraction → +ve inotropy**[^ety-inotropy].[^10]

![Frank–Starling curves and inotropy](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/Frank-Starling_Law_and_Inotropy.png/800px-Frank-Starling_Law_and_Inotropy.png)
*Frank–Starling curves at different inotropic states — a positive inotrope such as norepinephrine lifts the curve, giving more force at the same end-diastolic volume (Wikimedia Commons).*

#### Absence of tetanisation

- **Evolutionary adaptation of cardiac muscle: it cannot be tetanised.**

```
Longer duration of ARP (Absolute refractory period)
        ↓
No summation possible
        ↓
No tetany
```

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - absolute refractory period and contraction.png]]
*Cardiac action potential (green) with its long absolute refractory period (red bar, labelled "APR" on the page) covering almost the whole contraction (black) — the relative refractory period (RRP) comes only as the muscle relaxes (cropped from the handwritten page).*

🧠 By the time the muscle can respond to a new stimulus (end of the **absolute**[^ety-absolute] **refractory**[^ety-refractory][^pre-re] period), the contraction is already over — so a second twitch cannot ride on the first. A heart in tetanus could not fill, so this is a life-saving design.[^11]

## 🌀 Smooth Muscle

### Properties

- **Classical location: GIT.**
- **Hallmark: Absence of striations.**
- **Mode of contraction: Involuntary.**
- **Pacemaker: Cajal**[^ety-cajal] **cells.**
- **Dense bodies: Functional equivalent of Z lines.**
- **Ca²⁺ binding protein: Calmodulin**[^ety-calmodulin].
- **Inelastic d/t absence of titin.**[^12]

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - smooth muscle of GIT.png]]
*Smooth muscle of the GIT — spindle-shaped cells with one central nucleus each and no striations, threaded by autonomic nerve fibres (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![Skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscle compared (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/414_Skeletal_Smooth_Cardiac.jpg/800px-414_Skeletal_Smooth_Cardiac.jpg)
*The three muscle types side by side — striated skeletal, non-striated smooth and striated, branched cardiac muscle (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY; micrographs University of Michigan Medical School).*

### Types of smooth muscle

```
                       TYPES
             ┌───────────┴────────────┐
 Single unit smooth muscle     Multiunit smooth muscle
```

|---|---|---|
| **AKA / nature** | **Unitary smooth muscles** | **Individual subunits** |

| **Eg** | **Uterus**[^ety-uterus], **urinary bladder**, **ureter**[^ety-ureter], **GIT** | **Vascular smooth muscle** (blood vessel)[^13] |

### Control of smooth muscles

- **No well-defined neuro-muscular junction; regulated by autonomic**[^ety-autonomic][^pre-auto] **nervous system.**

#### Varicosities[^ety-varicosity]

- **Enlargement of nerve endings.**
- **Contain neurotransmitters:**[^ety-neurotransmitter][^pre-neuro]
  - **Acetylcholine**[^ety-acetylcholine] **→ Parasympathetic**[^ety-sympathetic][^pre-para].
  - **Norepinephrine → Sympathetic**[^pre-sym].

![[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles - nerve control of smooth muscle.png]]
*Nerve control of smooth muscle — an autonomic nerve fibre runs over the cells and releases transmitter from its varicosities (synapse en passant) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

#### Synapse en passant[^ety-en-passant]

- **One nerve fibre → innervates multiple smooth muscle cells.**

### Contraction & relaxation

#### Parasympathetic nervous system

- **↑ Ca²⁺ in the cell → Contraction.**

```
                 ┌─→ Extracellular
Source of Ca²⁺ ──┤
                 └─→ Intracellular: released through ─→ • RyR
                                                       • IP₃ receptor
```

- **IP₃**[^ety-ip3] receptor and **RyR** on the SR both release stored Ca²⁺.

**Mechanism of contraction:**

```
Ca²⁺ + calmodulin  (Ca²⁺–calmodulin complex)
        ↓
Activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)
        ↓
Activates myosin
        ↓
Myosin actin overlap
```

- **Myosin light chain kinase**[^ety-kinase][^suf-ase] **(MLCK)** phosphorylates the myosin head — in smooth muscle the switch is on **myosin**, not on actin (no troponin).

**Mechanism of relaxation:** **by activation of myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP).**

> [!note] Note — Rho kinase and fasudil
> - **Rho kinase**[^ety-rho] **→ inhibits MLCP → inhibits relaxation.**
> - **Fasudil ⊣ (−) Rho kinase → (+) MLCP → Relaxation.**[^14]

![Smooth muscle contraction — dense bodies pulled together (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/1028_Smooth_Muscle_Contraction.jpg/800px-1028_Smooth_Muscle_Contraction.jpg)
*Smooth muscle contraction — actin–myosin bundles anchored to dense bodies (the Z-line equivalents) pull the cell into a shorter, fatter shape (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY).*

#### Sympathetic nervous system

- **Inhibition of MLCK → inhibition of contraction.**[^15]

### Unique features

#### Plasticity[^ety-plasticity][^suf-ity]

- **Smooth muscles lack titin → plasticity (stretch without recoil).**
- **Eg:**
  - **Uterus keeps stretching without recoil as fetus grows.**
  - **Urinary bladder stretches to store urine.**[^16]

#### Latch bridge phenomenon[^ety-latch]

- **Energy efficiency of smooth muscle.**
- **Sustained contraction with low ATP.**
- **Eg: Sustained contraction of GIT for digestion**[^ety-digestion].[^17]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **Both muscles are run by Ca²⁺, but they read it with different switches built for different jobs.** The heart must beat, fill and beat again for life: gap junctions make it one functional syncytium, a little extracellular Ca²⁺ triggers a large SR release (CICR) onto troponin, SERCA (braked by phospholamban, released by NE) clears it for relaxation, and a long absolute refractory period forbids tetanus so the heart can always refill. Stretch (EDV) tunes the force — Frank–Starling.
>
> Smooth muscle must hold hollow organs for hours and accept big volume changes: Ca²⁺–calmodulin turns on MLCK at the myosin head, MLCP switches it off, cross-bridges can latch to hold tone on little ATP, and with no titin it accepts stretch without recoil (plasticity).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> SA node paces a functional syncytium; each AP lets ECF Ca²⁺ in, which releases SR Ca²⁺ through RyR; SERCA recaptures it. ↑ venous return → ↑ EDV → stronger beat (Frank–Starling). Smooth muscle in gut, bladder and uterus contracts as a single unit through gap junctions; tone is held by latch bridges.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A failing ventricle dilates to use Frank–Starling: a bigger EDV keeps stroke volume up; sympathetic NE phosphorylates phospholamban for +ve inotropy and lusitropy. The patient may have few symptoms. In vessels, Rho-kinase–mediated Ca²⁺ sensitisation raises tone.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Beyond physiological limits the fibres are over-stretched and the curve falls ("fall in pressure"); with titin mutation the chambers stretch without recoil → dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Persistent smooth-muscle hypercontraction gives vasospasm (e.g. cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage, treated with the Rho-kinase blocker fasudil).
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → volume / pressure load or titin defect → ventricular dilatation → Frank–Starling and NE compensation → over-stretch beyond the plateau → dilated failing heart (DCM)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Inotropes (β-agonists, which act through phospholamban and Ca²⁺ channels), diuretics that bring EDV back onto the ascending limb, and Rho-kinase inhibitors for vasospasm all act on the switches described in this chapter.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Phospholamban activates SERCA"**
>
> **What students think:** Phospholamban is the SERCA activator, so NE stimulates it.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Phospholamban **inhibits** SERCA. NE (via β1 → cAMP → PKA) **phosphorylates** phospholamban, which removes its inhibition — a double negative that ends in faster Ca²⁺ re-uptake (+ve lusitropy) and a fuller SR for the next beat (+ve inotropy).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Write it as two blocks: NE ⊣ phospholamban ⊣ SERCA = SERCA ON.
>
> **Trap 2: Diad vs triad**
>
> **What students think:** Cardiac muscle has triads like skeletal muscle.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** On the page, cardiac = **1 T tubule + 1 SR → diad**; skeletal = **1 T tubule + 2 SR → triad**. The heart's lesser SR is why it needs extracellular Ca²⁺ (CICR) to trigger each beat.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Di = duo = heart; Tri = three = skeletal."
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking the heart can be tetanised by a fast rate**
>
> **What students think:** Very rapid stimulation of the heart causes tetanus as in skeletal muscle.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The cardiac ARP lasts almost as long as the contraction itself, so no second AP can start until the muscle is relaxing — summation, and therefore tetany, is impossible. Fast rates only shorten filling.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Long ARP → no summation → no tetany" — the page's three-line chain.
>
> **Trap 4: Smooth muscle switch = troponin**
>
> **What students think:** Ca²⁺ binds troponin in smooth muscle too.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Smooth muscle has no troponin; Ca²⁺ binds **calmodulin**, which activates **MLCK** to phosphorylate myosin. Relaxation needs **MLCP**. Rho kinase inhibits MLCP (keeps contraction), fasudil blocks Rho kinase (relaxes).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Cardiac/skeletal = actin-side switch (troponin); smooth = myosin-side switch (MLCK).
>
> **Trap 5: Sympathetic always relaxes smooth muscle**
>
> **What students think:** The page says "sympathetic → inhibition of MLCK", so sympathetic stimulation always relaxes smooth muscle.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** That holds for β2 receptors (bronchi, GIT, uterus): cAMP → PKA phosphorylates MLCK and lowers its affinity for Ca²⁺–calmodulin. Vascular smooth muscle has α1 receptors, and sympathetic NE there **contracts** it (vasoconstriction).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Always ask "which receptor?" — α1 contracts, β2 relaxes.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Cardiac muscle: **large nucleus, plenty of mitochondria, intercalated discs with gap junctions (connexins), striated, involuntary, pacemaker SA node**.
> 2. Regulatory proteins: **troponin & tropomyosin**; **α-MHC → atria, β-MHC → ventricles**.
> 3. **Functional syncytium — specific to cardiac muscle**: gap junctions → rapid impulse spread → all cells contract together → large force.
> 4. **Titin** gives cardiac elasticity/recoil; **titin mutation → dilated cardiomyopathy** (stretch without recoil → dilated chambers).
> 5. **Frank–Starling law: force of contraction ∝ initial fibre length (EDV), within physiological limits**; beyond → plateau → fall in pressure.
> 6. **CICR**: AP → ECF Ca²⁺ influx → SR Ca²⁺ release through **RyR** → contraction → **SERCA** returns Ca²⁺ → relaxation.
> 7. **Cardiac = diad (1 T tubule + 1 SR); skeletal = triad (1 T tubule + 2 SR).**
> 8. **Phospholamban ⊣ SERCA; NE ⊣ phospholamban (phosphorylation) → ↑ Ca²⁺ storage → +ve lusitropy and +ve inotropy.**
> 9. **Cardiac muscle cannot be tetanised: long ARP → no summation → no tetany.**
> 10. Smooth muscle: **GIT, absence of striations, involuntary, Cajal cells (pacemaker), dense bodies = Z lines, calmodulin, no titin**.
> 11. **Single unit** (gap junctions +): uterus, urinary bladder, ureter, GIT; **multiunit** (no gap junctions): vascular smooth muscle.
> 12. No well-defined NMJ — **varicosities** (ACh parasympathetic, NE sympathetic), **synapse en passant** (one fibre → many cells).
> 13. **Ca²⁺–calmodulin → MLCK → myosin → contraction; MLCP → relaxation; Rho kinase ⊣ MLCP; fasudil ⊣ Rho kinase.**
> 14. **Plasticity** (no titin; uterus, bladder) and **latch bridge** (sustained contraction with low ATP; GIT).

[^1]: Mitochondria occupy roughly a third of a ventricular myocyte's volume — the heart never rests and runs almost entirely on aerobic metabolism. Cardiac myocytes usually have one (sometimes two) centrally placed nuclei, unlike the many peripheral nuclei of a skeletal muscle fibre.
[^2]: Six connexin molecules form a connexon (hemichannel); two connexons from neighbouring cells dock to make one gap-junction channel. Connexin 43 is the main ventricular connexin. Besides gap junctions, intercalated discs carry desmosomes and fasciae adherentes that hold the cells together mechanically.
[^3]: The SA node is the pacemaker because its spontaneous (phase 4) depolarisation is the fastest in the heart; the AV node and Purkinje fibres have slower intrinsic rates and are suppressed ("overdrive suppression") — see [[Cardiac Action Potentials]].
[^4]: α-MHC (gene *MYH6*) has a fast ATPase, β-MHC (*MYH7*) a slower, more economical one; mutations of *MYH7* are a leading cause of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Cardiac isoforms of troponin I and T are the basis of the troponin test for myocardial infarction.
[^5]: Guyton describes two functional syncytia — atrial and ventricular — separated by the fibrous AV ring, so the impulse passes from atria to ventricles only through the AV node and bundle.
[^6]: Truncating variants of the titin gene (*TTN*) are the commonest single genetic cause of familial dilated cardiomyopathy.
[^7]: The cellular basis: stretching the sarcomere toward ~2.2 µm improves actin–myosin overlap and increases the Ca²⁺ sensitivity of troponin C. Cardiac muscle normally works on the **ascending** limb of its length–tension curve, unlike skeletal muscle which sits at its optimum. The falling limb on the page represents fibres over-stretched beyond physiological limits, as in a grossly dilated failing heart.
[^8]: The trigger Ca²⁺ enters through L-type (dihydropyridine-sensitive) channels in the T-tubule during the AP plateau and opens RyR2 on the SR. In skeletal muscle the DHP receptor opens RyR1 mechanically, so skeletal muscle can contract without extracellular Ca²⁺ while the heart cannot. Most Ca²⁺ is re-sequestered by SERCA2a; the rest leaves the cell by the Na⁺–Ca²⁺ exchanger.
[^9]: Cardiac T-tubules lie at the Z lines and are much wider than skeletal ones (Guyton: about 5 times the diameter), with mucopolysaccharides inside that store extra Ca²⁺ — compensating for the less developed SR.
[^10]: Mechanism: NE → β1 receptor → Gs → cAMP → protein kinase A, which phosphorylates phospholamban (removing its brake on SERCA2a), L-type Ca²⁺ channels (more Ca²⁺ entry) and troponin I (faster Ca²⁺ release from troponin C → faster relaxation).
[^11]: Guyton: the ventricular refractory period is 0.25–0.30 s (about the length of the AP and of systole), followed by a relative refractory period of about 0.05 s. A stimulus in the RRP can give an extrasystole, but never a fused contraction.
[^12]: Smooth muscle filaments run obliquely and anchor to dense bodies (made of α-actinin, the same protein as the Z line) and to the cell membrane. The interstitial cells of Cajal generate the slow waves of GIT smooth muscle (see [[Gastrointestinal Motility]]).
[^13]: Guyton's classic examples of multiunit smooth muscle are the ciliary muscle and iris of the eye, the piloerector muscles and the smooth muscle of large arteries; small arterioles behave more like single-unit (visceral) muscle. The page's example "vascular smooth muscle" is kept.
[^14]: Rho kinase is activated by RhoA (downstream of receptors for angiotensin II, endothelin and others) and raises contraction at a given Ca²⁺ level ("Ca²⁺ sensitisation"). Fasudil, a Rho-kinase inhibitor, is used in Japan and China for cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid haemorrhage.
[^15]: This applies to β2-adrenergic receptors (bronchi, GIT, uterus, skeletal-muscle arterioles): cAMP → PKA phosphorylates MLCK and lowers its affinity for Ca²⁺–calmodulin. Through α1 receptors (most blood vessels) sympathetic NE does the opposite — Gq → IP₃ → Ca²⁺ → contraction.
[^16]: This is "stress relaxation": after a sudden stretch the tension first rises and then falls back towards the original value as the filaments rearrange. It lets the bladder fill to roughly 300–400 mL with little rise in pressure, and it lets the uterus enlarge throughout pregnancy.
[^17]: In the latch state, dephosphorylated myosin heads stay attached to actin and cycle very slowly, so tone is held for long periods; Guyton estimates smooth muscle needs only 1/10 to 1/300 of the energy skeletal muscle would use to hold the same tension.
