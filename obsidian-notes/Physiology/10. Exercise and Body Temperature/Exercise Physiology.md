# 🏃 Exercise Physiology

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Exercise[^ety-exercise][^pre-ex] physiology is how the body **supplies energy (ATP) to working muscle** and **re-routes blood, oxygen and ventilation** to match a demand that can rise 20-fold within minutes. The whole chapter answers three questions:
> - ✅ **Where does the ATP come from?** — phosphocreatine (seconds) → glycogen–lactic acid (a minute and a half) → aerobic (unlimited).
> - ✅ **How do heart and vessels deliver more blood to muscle?** — ↑ cardiac output (mainly ↑ HR), redistribution, exercise hyperemia; BP response depends on isotonic vs isometric work.
> - ✅ **How do lungs and tissues supply more O₂?** — ↑ ventilation, ↑ O₂ consumption (MET, VO₂ max), O₂ debt/EPOC, right-shifted ODC, ↑ A–V O₂ difference — while blood gases stay normal.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Car With Three Fuel Tanks and a Traffic Police

A sprinter's muscle is a car with **three fuel tanks**: a tiny **nitro tank** (phosphocreatine — huge power for 8–10 s), a **turbo tank** that leaves black smoke behind (glycogen → lactic acid — 1.3–1.6 min, then fatigue) and the **main engine** (aerobic — slower but unlimited). Meanwhile the **traffic police** (sympathetic system + local metabolites) **close the side roads** (kidney, gut) and **open the highway** (heart, muscle, later skin), while **the hospital road** (brain) is never touched. After the race the car keeps idling fast to **refill the tanks** — that is EPOC.

```
ENERGY      Phosphocreatine (8–10 s, 100 m) → Glycogen–lactic acid (1.3–1.6 min, ↑ lactate → fatigue) → Aerobic (unlimited)
BLOOD       ↓ renal & splanchnic · ↑ heart & muscle · brain constant (autoregulation) · skin ↓ then ↑ (heat)
HEART       CO ↑ 4× (non-athlete) / 7× (athlete) — HR keeps rising, SV plateaus · BP: isotonic DBP ↓, isometric DBP ↑
LUNGS       ↑ RR & ventilation · blood gases NORMAL · O2 use 250 ml/min (1 MET) → ↑ MET · O2 debt → EPOC
TISSUE      ODC shifts RIGHT · A–V O2 difference ↑ (tissues extract more O2)
```

## 📖 Significance and Type of Exercise

**Significance of exercise:**
1. **Release of endorphins**[^ety-endorphin]**: mood enhancers.**
2. **Cardiopulmonary**[^ety-cardiopulmonary][^pre-cardio] **exercise testing (CPET): to diagnose heart & lung diseases.**
3. **Lifestyle modifications: prescribed as treatment for diabetes mellitus**[^ety-diabetes-mellitus][^pre-dia]**.**[^1]

**Type of exercise:**

| | **Isotonic**[^ety-isotonic][^pre-iso] | **Isometric**[^ety-isometric] |
|---|---|---|
| **Muscle tension** | **Constant** | **Changes** |
| **Muscle length** | **Changes** | **Constant** |
| **Example** | **Running on a treadmill** | **Pushing a wall** |

> [!tip] Read the Greek
> *Iso-tonic* = "equal tension" — the muscle shortens against a steady load (dynamic work). *Iso-metric* = "equal length" — the muscle pulls hard but nothing moves (static work). This one difference decides the blood-pressure response below.

## ⚡ Sources of Energy

Muscle stores only a few seconds' worth of ATP, so it re-makes ATP from three systems that differ in **speed** and **duration**:

| System | How ATP is made | Duration | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Phosphocreatine system / phosphagen**[^ety-phosphagen] **system** | **ATP from stored creatinine phosphate**[^ety-phosphocreatine][^2] | **8–10 sec** | **100 m sprints** |
| **2. Glycogen**[^ety-glycogen][^pre-glyco][^suf-gen] **lactic acid**[^ety-lactic] **system** | **Anaerobic**[^ety-anaerobic][^pre-an] **ATP production → ↑ lactic acid → fatigue**[^ety-fatigue] | **1.3–1.6 min** | 400 m run[^3] |
| **3. Aerobic**[^ety-aerobic] **system** | **Unlimited energy (ATP) for unlimited time** | Unlimited | Marathon[^3] |

```
1. PHOSPHOCREATINE / PHOSPHAGEN      stored creatine phosphate → ATP           8–10 s     (100 m sprint)
2. GLYCOGEN–LACTIC ACID              ANAEROBIC ATP production
                                         ↓
                                     ↑ lactic acid
                                         ↓
                                     FATIGUE                                   1.3–1.6 min
3. AEROBIC                           unlimited energy (ATP) for unlimited time
```

> [!note] Note — activities requiring endurance[^ety-endurance]
> **Carbohydrate**[^ety-carbohydrate][^pre-hydro]**-rich diet → ↑ muscle glycogen storage → provides energy for up to 4 hrs → improves endurance.**[^4]

## 🔥 Changes During Exercise

### 💪 Skeletal Muscle Changes

- **Contraction**[^ety-contraction][^pre-con] **of skeletal muscle generates tension.**
- **Maximum contractile force: 3–4 kg/cm².**[^5]

#### Blood flow

| | At rest | During exercise |
|---|---|---|
| **Muscle blood flow** | **3 ml/100 g/min** | **80 ml/100 g/min (↑ by 20 times)**[^6] |

**Organ level blood flow during exercise:**

| Organ | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal & splanchnic**[^ety-splanchnic] **circulation (GIT)** | **↓** | **Redistribution**[^ety-redistribution][^pre-re][^pre-dis] |
| **Heart and skeletal muscles** | **↑** | **Redistribution** |
| **Cerebral**[^ety-cerebral] **circulation** | **Always maintained constant** | **D/t autoregulation**[^ety-autoregulation][^pre-auto][^7] |
| **Skin** | **Initial ↓ → later ↑** | **For heat dissipation**[^ety-dissipation] |

```
↓ blood flow in renal & splanchnic circulation (GIT) ┐
                                                      ├──► REDISTRIBUTION
↑ blood flow to heart and skeletal muscles           ┘
Cerebral circulation → always constant (autoregulation)
Skin → initial ↓ → later ↑ (heat dissipation)
```

#### Exercise hyperemia[^ety-hyperemia][^pre-hyper][^suf-emia]

The working muscle opens its own vessels: its metabolic waste products are vasodilators, so blood flow automatically follows the amount of work.

```
↑ metabolism → LOCAL ACCUMULATION OF H⁺, K⁺, LACTATE → vasodilation
                    (metabolic end products)                 ↓
                                     HYPEREMIA  ◄──  ↑ blood flow
```

- **↑ Metabolism**[^ety-metabolism][^pre-meta][^suf-ism] → **local accumulation of H⁺, K⁺, lactate** (metabolic end products) → **vasodilation**[^ety-vasodilation][^pre-vaso] → **↑ blood flow** → **hyperemia**.[^8]

### ❤️ Cardiovascular Changes

#### 1. Cardiac output (C.O)

- **↑ 7 fold in athletes.**
- **↑ 4 fold in non-athletes.**[^9]
- **CO = SV × HR.**

| As cardiac output rises | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Heart rate (HR)** | **Constantly increases** |
| **Stroke volume (SV)** | **Reaches plateau** |
| **Conclusion** | **↑ CO d/t ↑ HR** |

![[Exercise Physiology - stroke volume and heart rate vs cardiac output.png]]
*Stroke volume (ml/beat, left axis) and heart rate (beats/min, right axis) plotted against cardiac output (5 → 30 L/min) during graded exercise: SV rises from ~105 to ~165 ml and then reaches a plateau, while HR rises steadily (~50 → 180/min) — so the late rise in CO is due to ↑ HR (cropped from the handwritten page; Guyton-style figure).*

#### 2. Anticipatory tachycardia[^ety-anticipatory][^pre-ante][^ety-tachycardia][^pre-tachy][^suf-cardia]

- **Anticipatory ↑ in heart rate even before starting the exercise.**
- **Cause: joint proprioceptors**[^ety-proprioceptor] **—impulse**[^ety-impulse]**→ brain.**[^10]

#### 3. Blood pressure

| | **Isotonic** | **Isometric** |
|---|---|---|
| **Systolic**[^ety-systolic][^pre-syn] **BP** | **↑** | **↑** |
| **Diastolic BP** | **↓** | **↑** |
| **Cause** | **Local accumulation of metabolic end products → vasodilation → ↓ TPR & ↓ DBP** | **Sustained muscle contraction → ↑ tension → compresses blood vessels (arterioles**[^ety-arteriole][^suf-ole]**) → ↑ TPR & ↑ DBP** |

- **TPR: total peripheral**[^ety-peripheral][^pre-peri] **resistance**[^ety-resistance]**.**
- **DBP: diastolic blood pressure.**[^11]

```
ISOTONIC  (running)        local accumulation of metabolic end products → vasodilation → ↓ TPR & ↓ DBP   (SBP ↑)
ISOMETRIC (pushing a wall) sustained muscle contraction → ↑ tension → compresses arterioles → ↑ TPR & ↑ DBP  (SBP ↑)
```

#### 4. Preload[^ety-preload][^pre-pre]

Sympathetic stimulation squeezes the veins (the body's blood reservoir), pushing more blood back to the heart — more filling = more preload = a stronger beat (Frank–Starling).

```
Sympathetic nervous system stimulation
          ↓  releases
Norepinephrine
          ↓
Venoconstriction
          ↓
↑ Mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP)
          ↓
↑ Preload
```

- **Sympathetic**[^ety-sympathetic] **nervous system stimulation** → releases **norepinephrine**[^ety-norepinephrine][^pre-epi] → **venoconstriction**[^ety-venoconstriction][^pre-veno] → **↑ mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP)** → **↑ preload**.[^12]

### 🫁 Changes in Respiratory System

#### 1. ↑ Respiratory rate (RR) & pulmonary ventilation[^ety-ventilation]

**Mechanism:**
- **Exercising skeletal muscles → release K⁺ → stimulate chemoreceptors**[^ety-chemoreceptor][^pre-chemo] **→ ↑ ventilation.**
- **↑ Body temp during exercise → ↑ ventilation.**

> [!note] Note
> **During exercise: blood gases (pO₂, pCO₂, pH) remain normal.**[^13]

#### 2. Anticipatory tachypnea[^ety-tachypnea][^suf-pnoea]

- **↑ RR before exercise.**
- **Due to proprioceptors in joints.**

#### 3. ↑ Oxygen consumption

- **At rest: 250 ml/min → 1 MET (metabolic equivalent).**[^14]
- **During exercise: ↑ O₂ consumption → ↑ MET.**
- **Peak VO₂: O₂ consumed at the end of exercise.**
- **VO₂ max: maximum possible O₂ consumption (theoretical value).**[^15]

![[Exercise Physiology - oxygen debt and EPOC.png]]
*O₂ consumption (L/min) before, during and after exercise: at rest ~0.25; at the onset of exercise consumption rises slowly to 0.75, and the shaded gap is the **O₂ debt** — covered by borrowing from O₂ storage sites (Hb & myoglobin); after the end of exercise consumption stays high (**EPOC** — excess post-exercise oxygen consumption: ↑ RR and ↑ O₂ consumption after exercise → replenish Hb & myoglobin stores) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Part of the curve | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Onset of exercise — O₂ debt**[^16] | **Borrowing from O₂ storage sites (Hb & myoglobin**[^ety-myoglobin][^pre-myo]**)** |
| **Duration of exercise** | O₂ consumption at a steady high level (0.75 L/min in the figure) |
| **End of exercise — EPOC** | **↑ RR and ↑ O₂ consumption after exercise → replenish Hb & myoglobin stores** |

- **EPOC: excess post exercise oxygen consumption.**

#### 4. Diffusion[^ety-diffusion] of gases

- **Release of O₂ to tissues to meet the demand.**
- **Oxygen dissociation**[^ety-dissociation] **curve: shifts to right.**[^17]

#### 5. Arteriovenous (A–V O₂) difference

- **Arteriovenous**[^ety-arteriovenous] **oxygen difference ↑ during exercise.**
- **Cause: tissues extract more O₂.**[^18]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Exercise is a **supply-chain problem solved in layers of time**. ATP is made first from what is already stored (phosphocreatine, 8–10 s), then from fast but dirty anaerobic glycolysis (1.3–1.6 min, lactic acid → fatigue), and finally from the slow, clean, unlimited aerobic engine — whose fuel tank (muscle glycogen) is what a carbohydrate-rich diet enlarges to give 4 h of endurance.
>
> To feed the aerobic engine, the body moves **blood and O₂, not pressure**: CO rises 4–7-fold (mostly by HR, since SV plateaus), metabolites (H⁺, K⁺, lactate) dilate the working muscle's own vessels while sympathetic tone shuts kidney and gut, and the lungs raise ventilation enough to keep blood gases normal. Whenever supply lags (onset), stored O₂ in Hb and myoglobin is borrowed (O₂ debt) and repaid afterwards (EPOC).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> At rest the body uses 250 ml O₂/min (1 MET) with a muscle blood flow of 3 ml/100 g/min. Even before the start, anticipatory tachycardia and tachypnea (joint proprioceptors → brain) prepare the system.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> In steady exercise the reserves are recruited: CO ↑ (HR keeps rising, SV plateaus), muscle flow ↑ ~20-fold by exercise hyperemia, renal/splanchnic flow ↓, ventilation ↑ with normal blood gases, ODC right-shifted, A–V O₂ difference ↑. O₂ consumption rises in METs up to the person's VO₂ max.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> When demand exceeds aerobic capacity, anaerobic glycolysis takes over → lactic acid accumulates → fatigue within 1.3–1.6 min; a large O₂ debt is followed by a long EPOC. In a diseased system the limit arrives early: a failing heart cannot raise SV or CO (exercise dyspnoea, angina — the basis of CPET and treadmill testing), isometric strain sharply raises DBP and afterload, and a smoker's narrowed, mucus-filled, cilia-paralysed airways cut exercise and work capacity.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Rest (1 MET) → anticipation (HR, RR ↑) → onset (phosphagen, O₂ debt) → steady state (aerobic, redistribution, ↑ CO) → exceeding VO₂ max (lactic acid, fatigue) → recovery (EPOC)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> CPET and stress tests measure how far CO, ventilation and VO₂ can rise; exercise is prescribed for diabetes; patients with heart disease are advised isotonic (walking) rather than isometric (heavy lifting) exercise because isometric work raises DBP and TPR.

>

>

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Ventilation rises because arterial PCO₂ rises in exercise"**
>
> **What students think:** Exercise makes the blood hypercapnic and hypoxic, and that drives breathing.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In moderate exercise **blood gases (pO₂, pCO₂, pH) remain normal**. Ventilation rises because of K⁺ released by muscles acting on chemoreceptors, the rise in body temperature, and neural signals (joint proprioceptors, anticipatory drive). Gases go wrong only at extreme, anaerobic intensities.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Breathing in exercise is driven by signals, not by bad gases."
>
> **Trap 2: Thinking stroke volume carries the whole rise in cardiac output**
>
> **What students think:** CO rises because the heart pumps more per beat all the way up.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** SV rises early and then **reaches a plateau** (filling time shortens at high HR); HR **constantly increases**. So at high workloads the extra CO is due to ↑ HR.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "SV flattens, HR climbs."
>
> **Trap 3: Diastolic BP in isotonic exercise**
>
> **What students think:** All exercise raises both systolic and diastolic BP.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Systolic BP rises in both, but in **isotonic** exercise metabolic vasodilation lowers TPR, so **DBP falls**; in **isometric** exercise the sustained contraction compresses arterioles, TPR rises and **DBP rises**.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Isometric = squeeze the pipes → DBP up; isotonic = open the pipes → DBP down."
>
> **Trap 4: Peak VO₂ vs VO₂ max**
>
> **What students think:** They are the same.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** **Peak VO₂** is the O₂ actually consumed at the end of a given test; **VO₂ max** is the maximum possible O₂ consumption — a theoretical ceiling reached only when consumption plateaus despite more work.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Peak = what was reached; max = what could be reached.
>
> **Trap 5: Cerebral blood flow in exercise**
>
> **What students think:** Brain flow falls (redistribution) or rises (more CO).
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Cerebral circulation is **always maintained constant** by autoregulation; only renal/splanchnic flow falls, heart/muscle flow rises and skin flow first falls then rises for heat loss.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Brain = constant; skin = down then up."

[^1]: Regular aerobic exercise increases GLUT-4 translocation to the muscle membrane independently of insulin and improves insulin sensitivity — the reason it is first-line lifestyle therapy in type 2 diabetes mellitus.
[^2]: ⚠ The page writes "creatinine phosphate"; the high-energy store is **creatine phosphate (phosphocreatine)**. *Creatinine* is its non-enzymatic breakdown product, excreted in urine and used to estimate GFR.
[^3]: Example added from Guyton, which gives the systems' approximate durations as 8–10 s (phosphagen), 1.3–1.6 min (glycogen–lactic acid) and unlimited (aerobic) — the same values as the page — and their maximal ATP rates as roughly 4 : 2.5 : 1 (M of ATP/min).
[^4]: Guyton's classic comparison: muscle glycogen and endurance are highest on a high-carbohydrate diet (about 4 h of endurance) and lowest on a high-fat diet (under 1.5 h) — the basis of "carbohydrate loading" before endurance events.
[^5]: Guyton: the maximal contractile force of all types of muscle is about 3–4 kg/cm² of cross-sectional area; training increases strength mainly by hypertrophy (more cross-section).
[^6]: 3 → 80 ml/100 g/min is about a 25-fold rise; the page rounds it to "20 times" (Guyton: resting flow 3–4 ml/100 g/min, up to 50–80 in a well-conditioned athlete).
[^7]: Cerebral blood flow is held at ~50–55 ml/100 g/min by pressure autoregulation across a mean arterial pressure of roughly 60–150 mmHg.
[^8]: Other local vasodilators of active muscle: ↓ O₂, ↑ CO₂, adenosine, phosphate and ↑ osmolality. *Active* (exercise) hyperemia is caused by ↑ metabolism; *reactive* hyperemia follows a period of blocked flow.
[^9]: Guyton gives maximal cardiac output of about 30 L/min in a marathon runner (about 6× the resting ~5 L/min) and about 23 L/min in an untrained young man (about 4×); the page's 7-fold for athletes is kept.
[^10]: Most texts attribute the anticipatory rise in HR (and ventilation) to *central command* — impulses from the motor cortex and limbic system before movement, a feedforward response (see [[Homeostasis]]); impulses from joint and muscle proprioceptors add to the rapid rise at the onset. The page credits the joint proprioceptors.
[^11]: The sharp rise of DBP and TPR in isometric (static) work is the *pressor response*; it raises afterload on the heart, which is why heavy straining is discouraged in coronary disease and heart failure.
[^12]: Mean systemic filling pressure (normal ≈ 7 mmHg) is the pressure that would exist everywhere in the circulation if the heart stopped; venous return depends on MSFP minus right atrial pressure, so venoconstriction (↑ MSFP) increases venous return and preload.
[^13]: In moderate exercise arterial PO₂, PCO₂ and pH stay almost constant despite a 10–20-fold rise in ventilation; the drive is largely neurogenic (motor cortex collaterals, joint/muscle receptors), helped by K⁺, temperature and lactate acting on chemoreceptors. Only at very high intensity does lactic acidosis lower pH.
[^14]: 1 MET is usually defined as 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min — about 250 ml/min for a 70-kg adult; brisk walking ≈ 3–4 METs, running ≈ 8–12 METs.
[^15]: VO₂ max is about 35–45 ml/kg/min in untrained young adults and 70–85 ml/kg/min in elite endurance athletes; it is the best single index of aerobic fitness.
[^16]: Terminology: many textbooks call the early gap the *oxygen deficit* and the post-exercise excess the *oxygen debt* (= EPOC). Guyton's oxygen debt (~11.5 L after strenuous exercise) is repaid by restoring the ~2 L of stored O₂ (lungs, body fluids, haemoglobin, myoglobin) and ~9 L used to rebuild the phosphagen system and remove lactic acid.
[^17]: The curve shifts right because exercising muscle is warmer, more acidic (H⁺, lactate), has more CO₂ and more 2,3-BPG (the Bohr effect): haemoglobin's affinity falls and it unloads more O₂ for the same PO₂.
[^18]: At rest the A–V O₂ difference is about 5 ml O₂/100 ml blood (250 ml/min ÷ 5 L/min); in heavy exercise it can triple to about 15 ml/100 ml as venous O₂ saturation in muscle falls.
[^19]: Guyton: nicotine constricts the terminal bronchioles, and the irritants in smoke increase bronchial fluid secretion and epithelial swelling and paralyse the cilia — together they raise airway resistance and cut a smoker's maximal exercise capacity; carbon monoxide also occupies haemoglobin and lowers O₂ delivery.
[^ety-exercise]: **exercise** — Latin *exercere*, "to keep busy, to train", from *ex-*, "out" + *arcere*, "to shut in, restrain" → to drive out of rest; planned physical activity. **Differentiate:** *exercise* (structured training) · *physical activity* (any movement) · *work* (force × distance).
[^ety-endorphin]: **endorphin** — *endo(genous)* "made within" + *(mo)rphine* (from Morpheus, Greek god of dreams) → the body's own morphine; opioid peptides released in prolonged exercise ("runner's high").
[^ety-cardiopulmonary]: **cardiopulmonary** — Greek *kardia*, "heart" + Latin *pulmo*, "lung" + *-ary*, "relating to" → relating to heart and lungs; CPET measures VO₂, VCO₂ and ventilation during graded exercise.
[^ety-diabetes-mellitus]: **diabetes mellitus** — Greek *diabainein*, "to pass through" (*dia-* + *bainein*, to go — the siphon-like passing of urine) + Latin *mellitus*, "honey-sweet" → sweet urine passing through. **Differentiate:** *diabetes mellitus* (sweet urine, insulin problem) vs *diabetes insipidus* (*insipidus*, tasteless — ADH problem).
[^ety-isotonic]: **isotonic** — Greek *isos*, "equal" + *tonos*, "tension, stretching" + *-ic* → equal tension; in muscle physiology the load (tension) is constant while the muscle shortens. Same word in fluids means "equal osmotic pressure" (isotonic saline) — context decides.
[^ety-isometric]: **isometric** — Greek *isos*, "equal" + *metron*, "measure" + *-ic* → equal measure (length); the muscle develops tension without changing length.
[^ety-phosphagen]: **phosphagen** — *phospho-* (Greek *phōsphoros*, "light-bearer" → phosphorus) + *-gen*, "producer" → a phosphate store that produces ATP; creatine phosphate in vertebrate muscle.
[^ety-phosphocreatine]: **phosphocreatine (creatine phosphate)** — *phospho-*, phosphate + Greek *kreas*, "flesh, meat" (→ *creatine*, first isolated from meat by Chevreul, 1832) → the meat compound carrying a phosphate. **Differentiate:** *creatine* (the compound) · *creatine phosphate* (its energy-rich form) · *creatinine* (its waste anhydride in urine).
[^ety-glycogen]: **glycogen** — Greek *glykys*, "sweet" + *-gen*, "producer" → the sugar-producer; the branched glucose polymer stored in liver and muscle, named by Claude Bernard (1850s).
[^ety-lactic]: **lactic acid / lactate** — Latin *lac, lactis*, "milk" → first isolated from sour milk (Scheele, 1780); the end product of anaerobic glycolysis.
[^ety-anaerobic]: **anaerobic** — Greek *an-*, "without" + *aēr*, "air" + *bios*, "life" + *-ic* → life without air (oxygen). **Differentiate:** *anaerobic* (no O₂, glycolysis → lactate, 2 ATP/glucose) vs *aerobic* (with O₂, Krebs cycle + oxidative phosphorylation, ~30–32 ATP/glucose).
[^ety-fatigue]: **fatigue** — Latin *fatigare*, "to tire out" (*ad fatim*, "to bursting, enough") → muscle fatigue: falling force with continued activity, mainly from glycogen depletion and accumulation of H⁺/lactate and phosphate.
[^ety-aerobic]: **aerobic** — Greek *aēr*, "air" + *bios*, "life" → living with air (oxygen); the slow, unlimited ATP system.
[^ety-endurance]: **endurance** — Old French *endurer*, from Latin *in-* + *durare*, "to harden, to last" (*durus*, hard) → the capacity to last; depends on aerobic capacity and muscle glycogen.
[^ety-carbohydrate]: **carbohydrate** — Latin *carbo*, "coal, carbon" + Greek *hydōr*, "water" + *-ate* → "hydrate of carbon" (Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ); sugars and starches.
[^ety-contraction]: **contraction** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *trahere*, "to draw, pull" + *-ion* → drawing together. **Differentiate:** *isotonic contraction* (shortening at constant load) · *isometric contraction* (tension without shortening) · *contracture* (sustained shortening without action potentials).
[^ety-splanchnic]: **splanchnic** — Greek *splanchna*, "the inward parts, viscera" → relating to the abdominal viscera (gut, liver, spleen, pancreas); the splanchnic bed is a large blood reservoir.
[^ety-redistribution]: **redistribution** — Latin *re-*, "again" + *dis-*, "apart" + *tribuere*, "to allot" + *-ion* → allotting apart again; shifting cardiac output from inactive to active organs.
[^ety-cerebral]: **cerebral** — Latin *cerebrum*, "brain" + *-al* → relating to the brain. **Differentiate:** *cerebral* (cerebrum) · *cerebellar* (cerebellum, "little brain") · *cerebrovascular* (brain vessels).
[^ety-autoregulation]: **autoregulation** — Greek *autos*, "self" + Latin *regula*, "rule, straight stick" + *-ation* → self-ruling: an organ keeps its own blood flow constant despite changes in perfusion pressure (myogenic and metabolic mechanisms). Best in brain, kidney and heart.
[^ety-dissipation]: **dissipation** — Latin *dis-*, "apart" + *supare*, "to throw, scatter" → scattering apart; heat dissipation = losing body heat to the surroundings.
[^ety-hyperemia]: **hyperemia / hyperaemia** — Greek *hyper-*, "over" + *haima*, "blood" + *-ia* → excess blood in a part. **Differentiate:** *active hyperemia* (arteriolar dilation from ↑ metabolism — exercise) · *reactive hyperemia* (after occlusion is released) · *congestion* (passive — venous outflow blocked).
[^ety-metabolism]: **metabolism** — Greek *metabolē*, "change" (*meta-*, change + *ballein*, to throw) + *-ism* → the sum of chemical changes in the body. **Differentiate:** *anabolism* (*ana-*, up — building) vs *catabolism* (*kata-*, down — breaking).
[^ety-vasodilation]: **vasodilation** — Latin *vas*, "vessel" + *dilatare*, "to widen" + *-ion* → widening of a vessel; lowers resistance (TPR) and raises flow. Opposite *vasoconstriction*.
[^ety-anticipatory]: **anticipatory** — Latin *ante-*, "before" + *capere*, "to take" → taking (acting) beforehand; a feedforward response before the stimulus. Watch the prefix: *ante-* (before) vs *anti-* (against).
[^ety-tachycardia]: **tachycardia** — Greek *tachys*, "swift" + *kardia*, "heart" + *-ia* → fast heart rate (> 100/min). Opposite *bradycardia* (*bradys*, slow).
[^ety-proprioceptor]: **proprioceptor** — Latin *proprius*, "one's own" + *capere*, "to take" (→ *receptor*) → a receptor that senses one's own body position and movement (joint receptors, muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs). **Differentiate:** *proprioceptor* (self) · *exteroceptor* (outside world) · *interoceptor* (viscera).
[^ety-impulse]: **impulse** — Latin *in-*, "into, against" + *pellere*, "to drive, push" → a push; a nerve impulse is a propagated action potential.
[^ety-systolic]: **systolic / diastolic** — Greek *systolē*, "a drawing together, contraction" (*syn-* + *stellein*, to place) and *diastolē*, "a drawing apart, dilation" (*dia-*, apart) → BP at ventricular contraction vs during relaxation and filling.
[^ety-arteriole]: **arteriole** — Latin *arteria*, "artery" (Greek *artēria*, windpipe — ancients thought arteries carried air) + *-ole*, "small" → small artery; the resistance vessels that set TPR.
[^ety-peripheral]: **peripheral** — Greek *peri-*, "around" + *pherein*, "to carry" → *periphereia*, circumference → the outer parts; TPR is the resistance of the whole systemic circulation outside the heart.
[^ety-resistance]: **resistance** — Latin *re-*, "against, back" + *sistere*, "to stand" → standing against; vascular resistance (R ∝ 1/r⁴, Poiseuille) mostly at arterioles.
[^ety-preload]: **preload / afterload** — English *pre-*, "before" + *load* → the load (stretch, end-diastolic volume) on the muscle before it contracts; *afterload* = the load it meets after it starts contracting (aortic pressure, TPR).
[^ety-sympathetic]: **sympathetic** — Greek *syn-* (*sym-*), "together" + *pathos*, "feeling" → feeling together (Galen thought organs "sympathised" through these nerves); the fight-or-flight system that drives exercise tachycardia, venoconstriction and gut/renal vasoconstriction.
[^ety-norepinephrine]: **norepinephrine / noradrenaline** — *nor-* (German *N ohne Radikal*, "nitrogen without a radical" — no methyl group on N) + Greek *epi-*, "upon" + *nephros*, "kidney" + *-ine* → the de-methylated hormone of the gland "upon the kidney"; Latin version *ad-* + *renes* = *adrenaline*.
[^ety-venoconstriction]: **venoconstriction** — Latin *vena*, "vein" + *con-*, "together" + *stringere*, "to draw tight" + *-ion* → tightening of veins; it shifts blood from the venous reservoir (~64 % of blood volume) toward the heart.
[^ety-ventilation]: **ventilation** — Latin *ventus*, "wind" → *ventilare*, "to fan, expose to air" + *-ation* → moving air in and out of the lungs. **Differentiate:** *pulmonary (minute) ventilation* (RR × tidal volume) · *alveolar ventilation* (minus dead space) · *respiration* (gas exchange, also cellular O₂ use).
[^ety-chemoreceptor]: **chemoreceptor** — Greek *chēmeia*, "alchemy, chemistry" + Latin *receptor*, "receiver" → a receptor stimulated by chemicals (O₂, CO₂, H⁺, K⁺): peripheral (carotid and aortic bodies) and central (medulla).
[^ety-tachypnea]: **tachypnea** — Greek *tachys*, "swift" + *pnoē*, "breathing" → fast breathing. **Differentiate:** *tachypnea* (fast) · *hyperpnea* (deep, matched to metabolism — exercise) · *hyperventilation* (beyond need, ↓ PaCO₂) · *dyspnea* (difficult breathing).
[^ety-myoglobin]: **myoglobin** — Greek *mys, myos*, "muscle" + Latin *globus*, "ball" + *-in* → the globular O₂-binding protein of muscle; one haem, high affinity (hyperbolic curve), stores O₂ for the start of exercise.
[^ety-diffusion]: **diffusion** — Latin *dis-*, "apart" + *fundere*, "to pour" → pouring apart; passive movement down a concentration (partial-pressure) gradient. **Differentiate:** *diffusion* (passive, down a gradient) · *osmosis* (water across a membrane) · *active transport* (against a gradient, uses ATP).
