# 💾 Learning and Memory

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Learning** is acquiring new information; **memory** is storing and retrieving it. Both depend on **synaptic plasticity** — synapses that get stronger (potentiation) or weaker (depression) with use. The chapter answers:
> - ✅ How do the two **cerebral hemispheres**[^ety-hemisphere][^pre-hemi] divide the work (categorical vs representational)?
> - ✅ What are the **types of memory**, where is each stored, and how does **long-term potentiation** in the hippocampus turn short-term into long-term memory?
> - ✅ Which **speech areas** produce which **aphasia**?

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — The Notebook, the Filing Room and the Archive

Think of memory as office work. **Short-term (working) memory** is a sticky note — it holds a phone number while you dial, but only while you pay **attention**. The **hippocampus** is the filing clerk who decides what gets filed: with **LTP** it strengthens the synapses that fired together and **consolidates** the note into the **long-term archive** in the cortex. Without the clerk (hippocampal lesion), old files are still in the archive and skills still work, but **nothing new can be filed** — anterograde amnesia.

```
TIME:        immediate / working (short-term, needs attention) ──consolidation = LTP──► long-term
AWARENESS:   explicit (semantic, episodic → hippocampus, medial temporal lobe)
             implicit (procedural → striatum · priming → neocortex · conditioning → amygdala, cerebellum)
PLASTICITY:  potentiation (PTP, LTP, sensitization) · depression (LTD, habituation)
LTP:         glutamate → AMPA (Na⁺) → depolarisation → Mg²⁺ leaves NMDA → Ca²⁺ in → proteins → stronger synapse
SPEECH:      Broca 44 (output) · Wernicke 22 (comprehension) · arcuate fasciculus (repetition) · angular gyrus 39 (naming)
```

### Long term memory — Synaptic plasticity[^ety-synapse][^ety-plasticity][^pre-syn][^suf-ity]

- **Modification of strength of synaptic transmission.**
- **Based on activity.**

```
                                     TYPES
              ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
         POTENTIATION                                        DEPRESSION
   ┌──────────┼──────────────┐                        ┌──────────┴──────────┐
Post tetanic   Long term     Sensitization      Long term depression   Habituation
potentiation   potentiation (LTP)                     (LTD)
```

| | Potentiation[^ety-potentiation] (synapse stronger) | Depression[^ety-depression][^pre-de] (synapse weaker) |
|---|---|---|
| Members | **Post tetanic**[^ety-tetanic][^pre-post] **potentiation** · **Long term potentiation (LTP)** · **Sensitization**[^ety-sensitization][^suf-ation] | **Long term depression (LTD)** · **Habituation**[^ety-habituation] |

- 🧠 *Two everyday examples:* **habituation** — you stop hearing the ceiling fan; **sensitization** — after one loud bang you jump at every small sound.[^5]

#### Long term potentiation (LTP)

**Occurs in the hippocampus**[^ety-hippocampus] **/ cornu ammonis**[^ety-cornu-ammonis] **(CA) / Ammon's horn.**

```
Fn:   Short term memory ──── Consolidation / LTP ────► Long term memory
```

- **Consolidation**[^ety-consolidation] = the process that turns a fragile short-term memory into a stable long-term one — LTP is its synaptic mechanism.
- **Cornu ammonis I (CA1) neurons = memory neurons.**

**Pathway:**

```
                      Sensory inputs
                            ↓  Perforant pathways
                     Entorhinal cortex
             ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
 DIRECT   CA1 neurons            Dentate part (Hippocampus)    INDIRECT
 pathway                                    ↓                   pathway
                                       CA3 neurons
                                            ↓  Schaffer's collateral
                                       CA1 neurons
```

- **Direct pathway:** **entorhinal**[^ety-entorhinal] **cortex → CA1 neurons**.
- **Indirect pathway:** **entorhinal cortex → dentate**[^ety-dentate] **part (hippocampus) → CA3 neurons →(Schaffer's collateral**[^ety-schaffer][^ety-collateral]**)→ CA1 neurons**.
- Sensory inputs reach the entorhinal cortex first and enter the hippocampus by the **perforant**[^ety-perforant][^pre-per] **pathways**.[^6]

**Mechanism — Hebb's rule**[^ety-hebb]**:** **repeated activation of hippocampal synapses → synaptic strengthening.**

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Pre-synaptic**[^pre-pre] **neuron (CA3)** | **Synaptic vesicles** release **NT: glutamate**[^ety-glutamate]; the terminal also makes **nitric oxide**[^ety-nitric-oxide] |
| **2. AMPA**[^ety-ampa] **receptor (Na⁺ channel)** | Glutamate opens it → Na⁺ in → **depolarization**[^ety-depolarization] |
| **3. NMDA**[^ety-nmda] **receptor (Ca²⁺ channel)** | At rest **Mg²⁺ blocks NMDA** |
| **4. Depolarization removes the Mg²⁺ block** | **NMDA free from Mg²⁺ block** → **Ca²⁺ (2nd messenger)** enters the post-synaptic neuron (CA1) |
| **5. Ca²⁺ → produces proteins** | → **synaptic strengthening** (LTP) |

![[Learning and Memory - LTP mechanism at the synapse.png]]
*LTP at a CA3 → CA1 synapse — pre-synaptic neuron with synaptic vesicles releasing glutamate and nitric oxide; on the post-synaptic neuron the AMPA receptor (Na⁺ channel) depolarizes the membrane, which removes the Mg²⁺ block of the NMDA receptor (Ca²⁺ channel); Ca²⁺ as 2nd messenger produces proteins → synaptic strengthening (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- 🧠 *Why NMDA is the "coincidence detector":* it opens only when **two things happen together** — glutamate is bound (the pre-synaptic cell fired) **and** the post-synaptic membrane is depolarised (the post-synaptic cell fired). That is Hebb's rule in a single molecule: "cells that fire together wire together".[^7]

**Applied aspect:** **Lesions in hippocampus → anterograde**[^ety-anterograde][^pre-antero] **amnesia**[^ety-amnesia].
- **Inability to form new long term memories.**
- **Intact memories:** **recent, short term, procedural, past.**[^8]

#### Long term depression (LTD)
- **Occurs in the cerebellum.**
- **Significance:**

```
LTD ──► Faulty synapses: DEPRESSED ──► Correct synaptic connections: ESTABLISHED
```

- 🧠 *Plain words:* while you practise a skill, the cerebellum **weakens** the synapses that produced the wrong movement, leaving only the correct ones — motor learning by subtraction.[^9]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Memory is not stored in one place but **in the strength of synapses**, and the brain changes those strengths with activity. **Potentiation** (LTP in the hippocampus) writes new information by strengthening synapses that fire together — the NMDA receptor, needing both glutamate and post-synaptic depolarisation, is the molecular coincidence detector of Hebb's rule. **Depression** (LTD in the cerebellum, habituation) erases what is wrong or irrelevant.
>
> Different kinds of memory use different circuits: **explicit** facts and events need the **hippocampus / medial temporal lobe** to be consolidated, while **implicit** skills, priming and conditioning live in the striatum, neocortex, amygdala and cerebellum — which is why a hippocampal lesion stops new facts being stored (anterograde amnesia) but leaves skills and old memories intact. Language is a left-hemisphere network: Broca (output) ↔ arcuate fasciculus ↔ Wernicke (comprehension) ↔ angular gyrus (vision–word link).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Attention holds information in short-term (immediate/working) memory; repetition drives LTP at CA3 → CA1 synapses so it is consolidated into long-term memory; the dominant hemisphere handles language, the non-dominant visuospatial and holistic tasks, and the corpus callosum shares memories between them.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Early Alzheimer's: cholinergic neurons of the nucleus basalis and entorhinal–hippocampal neurons are lost first; patients forget recent events (episodic memory) but compensate with notes and routine, while procedural skills and remote memories survive. Chronic alcoholics with early thiamine deficiency show subtle recent-memory gaps.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Bilateral hippocampal damage (surgery, hypoxia, herpes encephalitis) → dense anterograde amnesia. Wernicke–Korsakoff → amnesia with confabulation. Dominant-hemisphere strokes → Broca's, Wernicke's, conduction or anomic aphasia, with dyscalculia; non-dominant parietal strokes → hemispatial neglect, astereognosis, agnosia.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → injury (cholinergic loss / thiamine deficiency / hypoxia / stroke) → failure to consolidate recent memories → compensation (notes, intact procedural memory) → global amnesia or aphasia → dependence
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The type of memory lost localises the lesion (explicit → medial temporal lobe; procedural → striatum/cerebellum), and the aphasia pattern localises a stroke to a specific arterial territory (Broca's — superior division of the left MCA; Wernicke's — inferior division). Cholinesterase inhibitors in Alzheimer's follow directly from the loss of nucleus basalis acetylcholine.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Hippocampal lesion (anterograde amnesia):** "Cannot remember anyone he met after the surgery but plays the piano perfectly" → explicit memory lost, procedural memory spared (patient H.M.).
>
> **Broca's aphasia:** "Telegraphic, effortful speech, understands commands, frustrated" → inferior frontal gyrus, area 44.
>
> **Wernicke's aphasia:** "Fluent word salad, does not understand, unaware of the deficit" → superior temporal gyrus, area 22.
>
> **Conduction aphasia:** "Fluent, understands, cannot repeat a sentence" → arcuate fasciculus.
>
> **Anomic aphasia:** "Can describe a pen's use but cannot name it" → angular gyrus.
>
> **Right parietal lesion:** "Ignores the left side, shaves half the face, cannot identify a key in the hand by touch" → hemispatial neglect, astereognosis.
>
> **Alzheimer's disease:** "Progressive memory loss, nucleus basalis of Meynert, acetylcholine" → cholinergic degeneration.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Hippocampal atrophy on MRI (medial temporal lobe) = Alzheimer's; atrophic mammillary bodies = Wernicke–Korsakoff.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Serial sevens (100 − 7 five times) and digit span test immediate memory and attention; inability to recall three objects after 5 minutes = short-term → long-term consolidation failure.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Thinking "dominant" means "more important"**
>
> **What students think:** The non-dominant hemisphere is the minor one.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Dominance refers only to language. The right (representational) hemisphere is specialised for visuospatial representation, creativity, music and intuition, and its lesions cause disabling neglect and agnosia. That is why the old names were replaced by *categorical* and *representational*.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Left = Language & Logic; Right = Representation & spatial."
>
> **Trap 2: Putting all memory in the hippocampus**
>
> **What students think:** Hippocampal damage wipes out every memory.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The hippocampus is needed to **consolidate new explicit** memories, not to store old ones or skills. Procedural memory (striatum), priming (neocortex) and conditioning (amygdala, cerebellum) are spared, as are remote memories already filed in the cortex.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Hippocampus = the filing clerk, not the archive."
>
> **Trap 3: Mixing up LTP and LTD sites**
>
> **What students think:** LTD is the reverse of LTP in the same place and plays no useful role.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page's high-yield pairing is LTP — **hippocampus** (memory formation) and LTD — **cerebellum** (motor learning by depressing faulty parallel fibre–Purkinje synapses). LTD is a learning mechanism, not just forgetting.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Potentiate in the Hippocampus; Depress in the Cerebellum."
>
> **Trap 4: Confusing which receptor carries Ca²⁺**
>
> **What students think:** AMPA receptors let in the Ca²⁺ that triggers LTP.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** AMPA receptors are Na⁺ channels that depolarise the membrane; the **NMDA** receptor is the Ca²⁺ channel, blocked by Mg²⁺ at rest until depolarisation expels the Mg²⁺. Ca²⁺ is the second messenger that triggers protein synthesis and synaptic strengthening.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "AMPA = Activator (Na⁺); NMDA = Needs depolarisation, lets in Ca²⁺."
>
> **Trap 5: Mixing up the aphasias**
>
> **What students think:** Wernicke's patients cannot speak.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Wernicke's (sensory) aphasia is **fluent** — output is increased but meaningless, and comprehension is lost. Broca's (motor) aphasia is **non-fluent** with comprehension preserved. Conduction aphasia spares both fluency and comprehension but destroys repetition; anomic aphasia affects only naming.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Broca = Broken speech; Wernicke = Wordy, but no understanding."

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Left = dominant / categorical** (96 % of right-handers, 70 % of left-handers) — language, speech, learning & memory; logic; lesions → **aphasias, dyscalculias**.
> 2. **Right = non-dominant / representational** — visuospatial, creativity, music; intuition; lesions → **astereognosis**, agnosia, **hemispatial neglect**.
> 3. **Memory engram** = collection (trace) of memories.
> 4. **Explicit (declarative)**: semantic (facts, places) and episodic (events) → **hippocampus, medial temporal lobe**.
> 5. **Implicit (non-declarative)**: **procedural → striatum**, **priming → neocortex**, **conditioning → amygdala, cerebellum**.
> 6. **Short-term** = needs **attention** — immediate (100 − 7 test) and working (phone numbers); **long-term** via **consolidation / LTP**.
> 7. Plasticity: **potentiation** (post-tetanic, **LTP**, sensitization) vs **depression** (**LTD**, habituation).
> 8. **LTP in hippocampus (cornu ammonis, CA1 = memory neurons)**; pathway entorhinal → dentate → CA3 → **Schaffer collateral** → CA1 (indirect) or entorhinal → CA1 (direct).
> 9. **Hebb's rule**: repeated activation → strengthening. Glutamate → **AMPA (Na⁺)** → depolarisation → **Mg²⁺ block of NMDA removed** → **Ca²⁺** → proteins → stronger synapse; **NO** from the terminal.
> 10. **Hippocampal lesion → anterograde amnesia** (no new long-term memories).
> 11. **LTD in cerebellum** — depresses faulty synapses so correct connections are established.
> 12. **Mamillary bodies** → recollective memory (**Wernicke–Korsakoff**); **anterior thalamus** → recent memory; **nucleus basalis of Meynert (ACh)** → **Alzheimer's**; amygdala → emotion; entorhinal → smell; **corpus callosum** → transfer.
> 13. **Broca 44 → non-fluent**; **Wernicke 22 → fluent, comprehension lost**; **arcuate fasciculus → conduction**; **angular gyrus 39 → anomic aphasia**.

LESIONS: hippocampus → anterograde amnesia · mammillary → Korsakoff · Meynert (ACh) → Alzheimer's · LTD → cerebellum
SPEECH: Broca 44 non-fluent · Wernicke 22 fluent, no comprehension · arcuate → conduction · angular 39 → anomic

[^1]: Textbooks give the left hemisphere as language-dominant in about 95–99 % of right-handed and about 60–70 % of left-handed people; the page's 96 % and 70 % fall within these ranges. Ganong introduced the terms *categorical* (sequential, analytic, language) and *representational* (visuospatial, holistic) hemisphere.
[^2]: The word *engram* was coined by the German zoologist Richard Semon (1904) for the physical trace a memory leaves in neural tissue; the page defines it as the "collection of memories".
[^3]: Classical conditioning of emotional responses (fear) depends on the amygdala; conditioning of discrete motor responses (e.g. eyeblink to a tone) depends on the cerebellum.
[^4]: Textbook time scales differ from the page: short-term memory lasts **seconds to minutes** (up to hours for "intermediate long-term" memory), and long-term memory lasts **months to a lifetime**. The page's "minutes to seconds" and "weeks to months" are kept in the body. The bedside "serial sevens" (subtracting 7 from 100 five times) chiefly tests attention and concentration; immediate recall is classically tested with the digit span.
[^5]: Eric Kandel's work on the sea slug *Aplysia* showed the cellular basis of habituation (less transmitter release) and sensitization (serotonin-enhanced release) — Nobel Prize 2000. Post-tetanic potentiation lasts seconds to minutes and is caused by Ca²⁺ accumulating in the pre-synaptic terminal after a high-frequency (tetanic) burst. LTP was discovered by Bliss and Lømo (1973) in the rabbit hippocampus.
[^6]: The full **trisynaptic circuit**: entorhinal cortex (layer II) → perforant path → dentate gyrus granule cells → mossy fibres → CA3 → Schaffer collaterals → CA1 → subiculum → back to the entorhinal cortex. The direct (temporo-ammonic) path runs from entorhinal layer III straight to CA1. The page does not name the mossy fibres between the dentate gyrus and CA3.
[^7]: Donald Hebb proposed the rule in *The Organization of Behavior* (1949). Ca²⁺ entering through NMDA receptors activates CaMKII and protein kinase C, which insert more AMPA receptors (early LTP); late LTP needs gene transcription (CREB) and new proteins. Nitric oxide is thought to act as a retrograde messenger that increases pre-synaptic glutamate release.
[^8]: The classic case is patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), whose medial temporal lobes were removed in 1953 for epilepsy: he could not form new declarative memories but kept working memory, procedural learning and remote memories. Textbooks add that memories from the period just before the lesion are often lost too (temporally graded retrograde amnesia); the page lists "recent" among the intact memories.
[^9]: In the cerebellum, when parallel fibres and the climbing fibre fire together (the climbing fibre signalling an error), the parallel fibre–Purkinje synapses are depressed — the Marr–Albus–Ito theory of motor learning. LTD also occurs in the hippocampus and neocortex.
[^10]: Loss of cholinergic neurons in the nucleus basalis of Meynert (the main source of cortical acetylcholine) is the basis for treating Alzheimer's disease with cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil.
[^11]: Roger Sperry's split-brain studies (Nobel Prize 1981) showed that, after the corpus callosum is cut, information learned by one hemisphere is not available to the other.
[^12]: Broca's area occupies Brodmann areas 44 (pars opercularis) and 45 (pars triangularis) of the dominant inferior frontal gyrus; the page gives 44. Wernicke's area is the posterior part of area 22. The supramarginal gyrus is area 40, the primary auditory cortex areas 41 and 42. Global aphasia (both areas) is non-fluent with lost comprehension and repetition.
