# 🩹 Vascular Injury

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> When a vessel is injured the body runs **hemostasis**[^ety-haemostasis][^pre-haemo][^suf-stasis] — vasoconstriction, a platelet plug and a fibrin clot — and then holds it in check with **anticoagulation**[^ety-anticoagulation][^pre-anti] so the clot stays local and is later dissolved.
> - ✅ The chapter answers: **how a clot forms** (platelets + 13 clotting factors + endothelium), **how we test it** (APTT, PT, platelet count, BT, CT), and **how it is stopped** (protein C/S, antithrombin III + heparin, TFPI, plasmin, vitamin K & warfarin).

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Burst Water Main

A water main bursts. First the **valve upstream is turned down** (vasoconstriction). Then workers **stuff rags into the hole** (platelet plug — quick but weak). Then a **concrete patch** is poured over it (fibrin clot — slow but strong), mixed by a chain of trucks each calling the next (the cascade). Finally **safety officers** stop the concrete spreading down the whole pipe (protein C/S, antithrombin) and later **jack-hammer it away** once the pipe has healed (plasmin).

```
INJURY
  ↓
1. VASOCONSTRICTION       → temporary stop in flow (serotonin, endothelin)
  ↓
2. PRIMARY PLUG           → collagen + vWF exposed → platelets adhere (GPIb/IX) → aggregate (GPIIb/IIIa)
  ↓
3. DEFINITIVE CLOT        → clotting factors: intrinsic + extrinsic → common → fibrin (positive feedback)
  ↓
4. ANTICOAGULATION        → protein C/S (V, VIII) · AT III + heparin (IX, X, XI, XII) · TFPI · plasmin
```

### Hemostasis

### 1. Vasoconstriction

- **Temporary stop in blood flow.**
- **Mediators:**
  - **Serotonin**[^ety-serotonin] **(from platelets).**
  - **Endothelin**[^suf-in] (from the damaged endothelium).[^2]

### Coagulation cascade[^ety-cascade]

> [!warning] **Positive feedback mechanism**
> Each activated factor activates the next, and **thrombin**[^ety-thrombin][^pre-thrombo] turns on its own upstream helpers (V, VIII, XI, XIII), so a trickle of thrombin becomes a flood — the "C" of CLAPS in [[Homeostasis]].[^3]

![[Vascular Injury - coagulation cascade.png]]
*The coagulation cascade as drawn on the page — **intrinsic**[^ety-intrinsic][^pre-intra] **pathway (contact activation in labs)** (light blue), **extrinsic**[^pre-extra] **pathway** started by **tissue injury → tissue factor** (orange), and the **common pathway** (dark blue) ending in the **stable fibrin clot**; Ca²⁺ is needed at several steps (cropped from the handwritten page).*

```
INTRINSIC (contact activation in labs)          EXTRINSIC
Negatively charged surface                       Tissue injury
  + kininogen / kallikrein                            ↓
  ↓                                              Tissue factor (III → IIIa)
XII → XIIa                                            ↓
  ↓                                              VII → VIIa
XI → XIa                                              │
  ↓   (Ca²⁺)                                          │
IX → IXa                                              │
  + VIIIa (VIII activated by thrombin) ── Ca²⁺ ───────┤
                        ↓                             ↓
COMMON              X ──────────────→ Xa ←──────────── X
                                      + Va (V activated by thrombin) + Ca²⁺
                                      ↓
                    Prothrombin (II) → IIa (THROMBIN)
                                      ↓
                    Fibrinogen (I) → Ia (FIBRIN)
                                      + XIIIa (XIII activated by thrombin) + Ca²⁺
                                      ↓
                              STABLE FIBRIN CLOT
```

- **Intrinsic pathway** — starts on **negatively charged surface molecules** with **kininogen**[^ety-kininogen] **/ kallikrein**; XII → XIIa → XI → XIa → IX → IXa (+ VIIIa).[^4]
- **Extrinsic pathway** — **tissue injury** releases **tissue factor** (III) → activates **VII → VIIa**.
- **Common pathway** — **X → Xa** (+ Va, Ca²⁺) → **prothrombin**[^ety-prothrombin][^pre-pro] **(II) → IIa (thrombin)** → **fibrinogen**[^ety-fibrin] **(I) → Ia (fibrin)** → **XIIIa** cross-links it → **stable fibrin clot**.

### Coagulation profile

**1–2. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) vs Prothrombin time (PT):**

| | **APTT** | **PT** |
|---|---|---|
| **Substances used** | 1. **Kaolin**[^ety-kaolin]: XII → XIIa · 2. **Cephalin**[^ety-cephalin]: substitute for platelet **phospholipids**[^ety-phospholipid] · 3. Ca²⁺ | 1. **Exogenous**[^ety-exogenous][^pre-exo] **tissue thromboplastin** · 2. Ca²⁺ |
| **Pathway assessed** | **Intrinsic & common pathway** | **Extrinsic & common pathway** |
| **Normal range** | **26–40 s** | **11–16 s** |
| **Defect / deficiency of factors** | VIII, IX, XI, XII, V, X, fibrinogen & prothrombin | VII, V, X, fibrinogen & prothrombin |

🧠 *In plain words:* a long **APTT** points to the intrinsic side (VIII, IX, XI, XII); a long **PT** points to the extrinsic side (VII); a factor of the common pathway (X, V, II, I) lengthens both.[^9]

## 🛡️ Anticoagulation

### Anticoagulation pathways — mechanism

```
1. THROMBIN + THROMBOMODULIN
          ┌────────────── activates (+) ──────────────┐
          ↓                                           ↓
   Protein C, Protein S                    tPA (tissue plasminogen activator)
          ↓ inactivates                               ↓
   Factors V, VIII                    Plasminogen → Plasmin / fibrinolysin

2. ANTITHROMBIN III + HEPARIN
          ↓ inactivates
   Factors IX, X, XI, XII

3. TISSUE FACTOR PATHWAY INHIBITOR (TPI)
```

1. **Thrombin + thrombomodulin**[^ety-thrombomodulin] — the same thrombin that makes the clot, once bound to thrombomodulin on healthy endothelium, switches sides:
   - activates (⊕) **Protein C** & **Protein S** → **inactivate factors V, VIII**.[^11]
   - activates (⊕) **tPA (tissue plasminogen**[^ety-plasmin][^suf-gen] **activator)** → **plasminogen → plasmin / fibrinolysin**[^ety-fibrinolysis][^suf-lysis].
2. **Antithrombin III + heparin**[^ety-heparin] → **inactivates factors IX, X, XI, XII**.[^12]
3. **Tissue factor pathway inhibitor**[^ety-inhibitor] **(TPI).**[^13]

> [!note] Note
> **Thrombomodulin is absent in cerebral**[^ety-cerebral] **circulation.**

### Note — Role of vitamin K in coagulation

```
                        Warfarin
                           │ ⊖
                           ↓
Glutamic acid ──Vitamin K──→ γ-carboxyglutamic acid ──Activates──→ Factors: II, VII, IX, X
                                                                     Protein C & protein S
```

- **Vitamin K**[^ety-vitamin] converts **glutamic acid → γ-carboxyglutamic**[^ety-carboxyglutamic] **acid** on the factors, which lets them bind Ca²⁺ and phospholipid — only then can they work.
- **Vitamin K–dependent: factors II, VII, IX, X; protein C & protein S.**
- **Warfarin**[^ety-warfarin] **⊖** blocks this step.[^15]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Hemostasis is a **positive-feedback explosion kept on a short leash**. Injury exposes collagen and tissue factor; platelets plug the hole within minutes and the cascade amplifies a few molecules of factor into a burst of thrombin and fibrin. Because positive feedback would otherwise clot the whole tree, healthy endothelium next to the injury turns thrombin into an anticoagulant (thrombomodulin → protein C/S), antithrombin III mops up free factors, TFPI shuts the extrinsic trigger, and plasmin later dissolves the clot.
>
> Every lab test and every bleeding or clotting disorder is a question about which part of this balance has failed: platelets (count, BT), the intrinsic + common pathway (APTT), the extrinsic + common pathway (PT), or the vitamin K step (PT first, warfarin).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> A cut vessel constricts (serotonin, endothelin), platelets adhere via vWF–GPIb/IX and aggregate via GPIIb/IIIa into a primary plug, and the cascade lays down fibrin cross-linked by XIIIa. Platelet count 1.5–4 lakh/mm³, BT 2–8 min, CT 3–6 min, APTT 26–40 s, PT 11–16 s.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A mild factor deficiency (mild haemophilia, early vitamin K deficiency, XII deficiency) may give no spontaneous bleeding: the lab shows the defect first — a long APTT (intrinsic factors) or a long PT (VII, the factor with the shortest half-life, falls first in vitamin K deficiency or on warfarin).
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Severe deficiency gives the clinical picture: haemophilia A/B → deep bleeding into joints and muscles (haemarthrosis) with normal BT; platelet or vWF defects → mucosal bleeding, petechiae and a long BT; afibrinogenaemia → both. On the other side, loss of anticoagulants (protein C/S, antithrombin deficiency, factor V Leiden) → venous thrombosis.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → factor/platelet defect → abnormal lab test only → bleeding after trauma or surgery → spontaneous bleeding (joints, muscles, mucosa)
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The pattern of tests pinpoints the replacement: factor VIII or IX concentrate in haemophilia, vitamin K or plasma for vitamin K deficiency and warfarin excess, platelets for thrombocytopenia; APTT monitors heparin, PT/INR monitors warfarin.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Haemophilia A:** "boy with haemarthrosis, maternal uncle affected, isolated long APTT, normal PT and BT" → factor VIII (anti-haemophilic factor A) deficiency, X-linked.
>
> **Christmas disease:** "same picture as haemophilia, factor VIII normal" → factor IX deficiency (haemophilia B).
>
> **PTA deficiency:** "bleeding after surgery, long APTT, autosomal" → factor XI (anti-haemophilic factor C).
>
> **Hageman factor deficiency:** "very long APTT, no bleeding at all" → factor XII — contact activation matters in the test tube, not in the body.
>
> **Vitamin K deficiency / warfarin:** "newborn without vitamin K, malabsorption, long antibiotic course, warfarin" → ↓ II, VII, IX, X, protein C & S → PT prolonged first.
>
> **Platelet adhesion defect:** "GPIb/IX" → Bernard–Soulier; "GPIIb/IIIa" → Glanzmann thrombasthenia; "vWF" → von Willebrand disease.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> A loose clump of platelets on exposed collagen = primary hemostatic plug; the same clump wrapped in fibrin strands = definitive clot.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Platelet count < 1.5 lakh/mm³ = thrombocytopenia; BT > 8 min = platelet/vWF problem; APTT > 40 s = intrinsic/common defect; PT > 16 s = extrinsic/common defect or vitamin K lack.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Mixing up APTT and PT**
>
> **What students think:** Both tests check the whole cascade, so either picks up any factor.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** **APTT** starts the intrinsic pathway with kaolin + cephalin → it tests **intrinsic + common** (VIII, IX, XI, XII, V, X, II, I). **PT** adds tissue thromboplastin → it tests **extrinsic + common** (VII, V, X, II, I). Factor VII is seen only by PT; VIII, IX, XI, XII only by APTT.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**P**lay **T**ennis outside (PT – extrinsic); **P**lay **T**able **T**ennis inside (PTT – intrinsic)" — and heparin is monitored by APTT, warfarin by PT.
>
> **Trap 2: Thinking factor XII deficiency bleeds**
>
> **What students think:** Any factor deficiency causes haemorrhage.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Hageman factor starts contact activation, which is how the **lab** triggers clotting (kaolin, glass); in the body tissue factor bypasses it. XII deficiency gives a strikingly long APTT but no bleeding.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Long APTT + no bleeding = XII (or a lupus anticoagulant)."
>
> **Trap 3: Forgetting proteins C and S are vitamin K–dependent**
>
> **What students think:** Vitamin K and warfarin affect only II, VII, IX, X.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Protein C and protein S also need γ-carboxylation. Protein C has a short half-life, so in the first days of warfarin it falls before II and X → a transient pro-thrombotic state (warfarin skin necrosis); hence heparin cover when warfarin is started.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "1972 + C + S" — factors 10, 9, 7, 2 and proteins C and S.
>
> **Trap 4: Calling coagulation a negative feedback system**
>
> **What students think:** Like most body systems, coagulation is self-limiting by negative feedback.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The cascade itself is **positive feedback** (thrombin activates V, VIII, XI, XIII and platelets). Its limits come from separate anticoagulant systems — protein C/S, antithrombin III, TFPI, plasmin — not from the cascade turning itself off.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Cascade = accelerator; anticoagulants = brakes."
>
> **Trap 5: Antithrombin works alone**
>
> **What students think:** Heparin is the anticoagulant.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Heparin has almost no anticoagulant action of its own; it binds **antithrombin III** and speeds its inhibition of IXa, Xa, XIa, XIIa (and thrombin) by about a thousand-fold. In antithrombin deficiency heparin fails ("heparin resistance").
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Heparin is the key; antithrombin is the lock that does the work."

[^1]: Adhesion: von Willebrand factor bridges exposed sub-endothelial collagen to the platelet's **GPIb/IX** receptor (absent in Bernard–Soulier syndrome; vWF lacking in von Willebrand disease). Activation: the platelet changes shape and releases ADP and thromboxane A₂, which recruit more platelets. Aggregation: fibrinogen bridges **GPIIb/IIIa** receptors of neighbouring platelets (absent in Glanzmann thrombasthenia).
[^2]: The page lists endothelin as a mediator without a source; it is released by the injured endothelium. Platelets also release thromboxane A₂, another vasoconstrictor. The spasm lasts minutes to hours, long enough for the plug and clot to form.
[^3]: Thrombin activates factors V, VIII, XI and XIII and also activates platelets — every one of these speeds its own production. See [[Homeostasis]] for positive feedback (CLAPS).
[^4]: High-molecular-weight kininogen and prekallikrein help factor XII bind the negatively charged surface (glass, kaolin) and activate it. In the body the extrinsic (tissue factor) route starts clotting; the intrinsic loop amplifies it, which is why XII deficiency does not bleed.
[^5]: The page colours Ca²⁺ dark blue (common pathway) but its own cascade figure shows Ca²⁺ needed in the intrinsic, extrinsic and common steps. Removing Ca²⁺ with citrate, oxalate or EDTA is how blood is kept unclotted in the lab and the blood bank.
[^6]: "Factor VI" was the name once given to activated factor V (Va); it was found not to be a separate protein and the number was retired.
[^7]: Haemophilia A (VIII) and B (IX) are X-linked recessive — boys affected, mothers carriers; haemophilia A is about 80–85 % of cases. PTA (factor XI) deficiency, sometimes called haemophilia C, is autosomal and commonest in Ashkenazi Jews; it gives milder, injury-related bleeding.
[^8]: The page reads "Hageman **triad**" ⚠; standard texts call factor XII deficiency the **Hageman trait** — a prolonged APTT with no bleeding tendency.
[^9]: "Partial" thromboplastin time: cephalin supplies only the phospholipid part of thromboplastin, not tissue factor, so the test bypasses the extrinsic pathway. Normal ranges vary by laboratory; PT is reported as the INR to monitor warfarin, and APTT is used to monitor unfractionated heparin.
[^10]: Bleeding time (Duke, Ivy) tests platelet number and function and the vessel wall; clotting time (Lee–White) tests the coagulation factors. A platelet count of 1.5–4 lakh/mm³ = 150 000–400 000/µL.
[^11]: Activated protein C, with protein S as its cofactor, cleaves factors Va and VIIIa. Factor V Leiden (a mutant factor V that resists activated protein C) is the commonest inherited cause of venous thrombosis.
[^12]: Antithrombin III inactivates thrombin (IIa) and Xa most strongly, and also IXa, XIa and XIIa; heparin speeds it about a thousand-fold. The page lists IX, X, XI, XII; textbooks add thrombin itself — hence the name *anti-thrombin*.
[^13]: The usual abbreviation is **TFPI**. It binds factor Xa and then inhibits the tissue factor–VIIa complex, shutting off the extrinsic trigger once a little Xa has formed.
[^14]: PAI-1 blocks tPA (and urokinase); α2-antiplasmin neutralises free plasmin; TAFI (thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor) trims the lysine residues on fibrin where plasminogen and tPA bind — all three protect the clot from being dissolved too early.
[^15]: Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), so vitamin K cannot be recycled to its active form and new factors are made without γ-carboxyglutamate. Its effect is reversed by vitamin K (slowly) or by fresh frozen plasma / prothrombin complex concentrate (at once).
[^ety-haemostasis]: **haemostasis / hemostasis** — Greek *haima*, "blood" + *stasis*, "standing still" → stopping the flow of blood. **Differentiate:** *haemostasis* (arrest of bleeding) · *homeostasis* (*homoios*, similar — constancy of the internal environment) · *haemostat* (a clamp that stops bleeding).
[^ety-anticoagulation]: **anticoagulation** — Greek *anti-*, "against" + Latin *coagulare*, "to curdle" + *-ation* → the process of opposing clot formation; the body's own anticoagulants are protein C/S, antithrombin III, TFPI and plasmin.
[^ety-coagulation]: **coagulation** — Latin *co-*, "together" + *agere*, "to drive" → *coagulare*, "to curdle, to drive together" + *-ation*, the process → turning liquid blood into a solid fibrin clot. **Differentiate:** *coagulation* (fibrin clot) · *agglutination* (cells clumped by antibodies — *ad-* + *gluten*, glue) · *aggregation* (platelets sticking to each other) · *thrombosis* (a clot inside an intact vessel).
[^ety-constriction]: **constriction** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *stringere*, "to draw tight" + *-ion* → drawing tight; *vasoconstriction* narrows the injured vessel to slow bleeding. **Differentiate:** *vasoconstriction* (active, by muscle) vs *stenosis* (fixed narrowing).
[^ety-collagen]: **collagen** — Greek *kolla*, "glue" + *-gen*, "producing" → "glue-producer" (boiled collagen gives glue and gelatin) — the fibrous protein under the endothelium that platelets stick to when it is exposed.
[^ety-von-willebrand]: **von Willebrand factor** — Erik Adolf von Willebrand (1870–1949), Finnish physician, who in 1926 described an inherited bleeding disorder in a family from the Åland Islands; vWF carries factor VIII in plasma and anchors platelets to collagen.
[^ety-platelet]: **platelet** — English *plate* + *-let*, "small" → a small plate (disc-shaped cell fragment); also *thrombocyte* — Greek *thrombos*, "clot, lump" + *kytos*, "cell". Made from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow; lifespan ~7–10 days.
[^ety-adhesion]: **adhesion / aggregation** — Latin *ad-*, "to" + *haerere*, "to stick" → sticking **to** a surface (platelet to collagen via vWF–GPIb/IX); *ad-* + *grex, gregis*, "a flock" → *aggregare*, "to flock together" (platelet to platelet via fibrinogen–GPIIb/IIIa). **Differentiate:** *adhesion* (to the wall) · *aggregation* (to each other) · *agglutination* (cells clumped by antibody) · *activation* (shape change and release).
[^ety-glycoprotein]: **glycoprotein (GP)** — Greek *glykys*, "sweet" (sugar) + *prōteios*, "primary, of the first rank" → a protein carrying sugar chains; platelet receptors GPIb/IX and GPIIb/IIIa are numbered by their position on a gel.
[^ety-conformational]: **conformational change** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *formare*, "to shape" + *-ation* → a change in the three-dimensional shape of a protein (here GPIIb/IIIa), which switches it from inactive to able to bind fibrinogen; the platelet also changes from disc to spiky sphere.
[^ety-endothelium]: **endothelium / endothelin** — Greek *endon*, "within" + *thēlē*, "nipple" + *-ium*, a tissue — the word was coined (Wilhelm His, 1865) by analogy with *epithelium*, originally the thin skin over the nipple-like papillae of the lip; the single cell layer lining vessels. *Endothelin* (+ *-in*) is the peptide it releases (discovered 1988), the most powerful vasoconstrictor of its time.
[^ety-serotonin]: **serotonin** — Latin *serum* + Greek *tonos*, "tension, tone" + *-in* → a substance in serum that raises vascular tone (named in 1948); chemically 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT). Platelets store it but do not make it.
[^ety-cascade]: **cascade** — Italian *cascata*, "waterfall" (*cascare*, to fall) → a series in which each step triggers the next and the signal grows at every step; the coagulation "waterfall" model of 1964.
[^ety-thrombin]: **thrombin** — Greek *thrombos*, "a clot, a lump" + *-in*, a protein → the enzyme (factor IIa) that turns fibrinogen into fibrin. **Differentiate:** *thrombin* (the enzyme) · *thrombus* (a clot inside a vessel) · *thrombosis* (the process of forming one) · *embolus* (a clot that has travelled).
[^ety-intrinsic]: **intrinsic / extrinsic** — Latin *intrinsecus*, "on the inside" (*intra* + *secus*, "beside") vs *extrinsecus*, "from outside" (*extra* + *secus*) → the intrinsic pathway uses only factors already inside the blood; the extrinsic needs tissue factor from outside the blood.
[^ety-kininogen]: **kininogen / kallikrein** — Greek *kinein*, "to move" + *-gen* → producer of *kinins* (peptides that move smooth muscle, e.g. bradykinin); *kallikrein* from Greek *kallikreas*, "pancreas" (where it was first found in quantity) → the enzyme that releases kinins and activates factor XII.
[^ety-prothrombin]: **prothrombin** — Greek *pro-*, "before" + *thrombin* → the precursor of thrombin (factor II); made in the liver, vitamin K–dependent.
[^ety-fibrin]: **fibrin / fibrinogen** — Latin *fibra*, "fibre" + *-in* → the fibrous protein of the clot; + *-gen*, "producer" → *fibrinogen* (factor I), the soluble plasma precursor that thrombin cuts into fibrin.
[^ety-thromboplastin]: **thromboplastin** — Greek *thrombos*, "clot" + *plassein*, "to mould, to form" + *-in* → a clot-forming substance; *tissue thromboplastin* = tissue factor (factor III) + phospholipid.
[^ety-proaccelerin]: **proaccelerin / proconvertin** — Latin *pro-*, "before" + *accelerare*, "to hasten" + *-in* → precursor of the "accelerator" (factor V); *pro-* + *convertere*, "to turn around" → precursor of the "convertin" that speeds conversion of prothrombin (factor VII). Factor V deficiency was described by Paul Owren (1947) — "Owren's disease".
[^ety-haemophilia]: **haemophilia** — Greek *haima*, "blood" + *philia*, "love, tendency to" → a tendency to bleed; the name dates from the 1820s. *Anti-haemophilic factor* = the factor whose lack causes haemophilia (A = VIII, B = IX, C = XI).
[^ety-christmas]: **Christmas factor / disease** — named after **Stephen Christmas**, the first patient (a young boy) in whom factor IX deficiency was distinguished from haemophilia A; reported in 1952 — in the Christmas issue of the *British Medical Journal*.
[^ety-stuart-prower]: **Stuart-Prower factor** — named after the two patients in whom factor X deficiency was found in the 1950s, Mr Rufus Stuart and Miss Audrey Prower.
[^ety-hageman]: **Hageman factor** — named after **John Hageman**, the patient in whom Oscar Ratnoff found factor XII deficiency in 1955 on routine pre-operative testing; he had a very long clotting time but no bleeding (he later died of a pulmonary embolism).
[^ety-afibrinogenaemia]: **afibrinogenaemia / hypoprothrombinaemia / hypoconvertinaemia** — Greek *a-*, "without" + fibrinogen + *-aemia*, "condition of the blood" → no fibrinogen in the blood; *hypo-*, "under" + prothrombin / convertin + *-aemia* → too little prothrombin / proconvertin. **Differentiate:** *a-* (absent) · *hypo-* (reduced) · *dys-* (present but faulty — dysfibrinogenaemia).
[^ety-deficiency]: **deficiency** — Latin *de-*, "away, down" + *facere*, "to do, make" → *deficere*, "to fail, to fall short" + *-ency* → a shortfall.
[^ety-parahaemophilia]: **parahaemophilia** — Greek *para-*, "beside, resembling" + haemophilia → a bleeding disorder that resembles haemophilia — factor V deficiency (Owren's disease, 1947).
[^ety-antecedent]: **antecedent** — Latin *ante-*, "before" + *cedere*, "to go" → that which goes before; plasma thromboplastin antecedent (PTA) = factor XI, acting before thromboplastin is formed.
[^ety-kaolin]: **kaolin** — from Chinese *Gaoling*, "high ridge", the hill near Jingdezhen where the white china clay was mined → a negatively charged clay used to activate factor XII (contact activation) in the APTT.
[^ety-cephalin]: **cephalin** — Greek *kephalē*, "head" + *-in* → a phospholipid (phosphatidylethanolamine) first extracted from brain tissue; in the APTT it stands in for platelet phospholipid.
[^ety-phospholipid]: **phospholipid** — Greek *phōsphoros*, "light-bearer" (phosphorus) + *lipos*, "fat" + *-id* → a fat carrying a phosphate group; the platelet membrane surface on which clotting complexes assemble.
[^ety-exogenous]: **exogenous** — Greek *exō*, "outside" + *-genēs*, "produced" + *-ous* → produced outside (the reagent supplies tissue thromboplastin from outside the sample). **Differentiate:** *exogenous* (from outside) vs *endogenous* (made within).
[^ety-thrombomodulin]: **thrombomodulin** — Greek *thrombos*, "clot" + Latin *modulus*, "a small measure" (*modulari*, to regulate) + *-in* → the endothelial receptor that modulates thrombin, changing it from a clotting enzyme into an activator of protein C.
[^ety-plasmin]: **plasmin / plasminogen** — Greek *plasma*, "something moulded" (the fluid of blood) + *-in* → the plasma enzyme that digests fibrin; + *-gen*, "producer" → *plasminogen*, its inactive precursor; *tPA* = tissue plasminogen activator, made by endothelium.
[^ety-fibrinolysis]: **fibrinolysis / fibrinolysin** — Latin *fibra* + *-in* + Greek *lysis*, "loosening, dissolving" → dissolving fibrin; *fibrinolysin* is the old name for plasmin. **Differentiate:** *fibrinolysis* (dissolving fibrin) · *thrombolysis* (dissolving a whole thrombus with drugs such as alteplase) · *haemolysis* (bursting red cells).
[^ety-heparin]: **heparin** — Greek *hēpar*, "liver" + *-in* → first isolated from dog liver (Jay McLean and William Howell, 1916–1918); made by mast cells and basophils. It works only by binding antithrombin III.
[^ety-inhibitor]: **inhibitor** — Latin *in-*, "in" + *habere*, "to hold" → *inhibere*, "to hold back, restrain" + *-or*, the agent → that which holds a reaction back.
[^ety-cerebral]: **cerebral** — Latin *cerebrum*, "brain" + *-al* → of the brain. **Differentiate:** *cerebral* (brain as a whole / cerebrum) · *cerebellar* (*cerebellum*, little brain).
[^ety-vitamin]: **vitamin K** — Latin *vita*, "life" + *amine* → "vital amine" (Casimir Funk, 1912; the *-e* was dropped when most vitamins turned out not to be amines); **K** for German *Koagulation*, named by Henrik Dam (1935).
[^ety-carboxyglutamic]: **γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla)** — *carboxy-* (carbon + oxygen: a –COOH group) + *glutamic* (Latin *gluten*, "glue" — glutamic acid was first isolated from wheat gluten) → glutamic acid carrying an extra carboxyl on its γ (third) carbon; the two carboxyls grip Ca²⁺.
[^ety-warfarin]: **warfarin** — **WARF** (Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation, which funded its discovery) + *-arin* from *coumarin* → a coumarin anticoagulant, first a rat poison (1948), then a drug.
[^pre-haemo]: **haemo- / hemo- / haemat-** — blood: hemostasis, haemophilia, haematoma. Don't confuse with **hemi-** half (hemiplegia) · **hepato-** liver.
[^suf-stasis]: **-stasis** — standing still, stopping: haemostasis, homeostasis, cholestasis. Don't confuse with **metastasis** (change of place) · **-static** (bacteriostatic) · **-stat** an instrument that keeps something still (thermostat, haemostat).
[^pre-anti]: **anti-** — against: anticoagulation, antithrombin, antiplasmin, anti-haemophilic factor (the factor *against* haemophilia). Don't confuse with **ante-** before (antecedent) · **ant- / anta-** against before a vowel (antagonist).
[^pre-con]: **con- / com- / co-** — together, with: coagulation (*co-* + *agere*), constriction, conformational. Don't confuse with **contra-** against · **counter-** against.
[^suf-ation]: **-ation / -ion** — the process or result: coagulation, constriction, aggregation, anticoagulation. Don't confuse with **-osis** a state (thrombosis) · **-ism** a state or doctrine.
[^suf-gen]: **-gen / -genous / -genesis** — producer, produced, origin: collagen, fibrinogen, plasminogen, kininogen, exogenous. Don't confuse with **-genic** causing (pathogenic) · **-gnosis** knowledge (diagnosis).
[^pre-ad]: **ad- (ac-, af-, ag-, al- …)** — to, towards; the *d* turns into the next consonant: adhesion, aggregation, agglutination. Don't confuse with **ab-** away from · **a- / an-** without.
[^pre-endo]: **endo-** — within: endothelium, endothelin, endogenous. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (exogenous) · **epi-** upon (epithelium) · **ecto-** outer.
[^suf-in]: **-in** — a neutral substance, usually a protein or peptide: endothelin, thrombin, fibrin, heparin, cephalin. Don't confuse with **-ine** amines/alkaloids (serotonin is an exception by naming) · **-ase** an enzyme · **-ogen** an inactive precursor (fibrinogen).
[^pre-thrombo]: **thrombo-** — clot: thrombin, thromboplastin, thrombomodulin, thrombocyte. Don't confuse with **thymo-** thymus · **thrombus** (the clot itself) vs **embolus** (a travelling clot).
[^pre-intra]: **intra-** — within, inside: intrinsic (*intra* + *secus*), intravascular, intracellular. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **intro-** into.
[^pre-extra]: **extra-** — outside, beyond: extrinsic (*extra* + *secus*), extracellular, extravascular. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exogenous) · **ex-** out of (excretion).
[^pre-pro]: **pro-** — before, in front of, precursor: prothrombin, proaccelerin, proconvertin, proenzyme. Don't confuse with **pre-** before (in time: prenatal) · **post-** after.
[^suf-philia]: **-philia / -phil** — love, tendency, affinity: haemophilia (tendency to bleed), eosinophil, thrombophilia (tendency to clot). Don't confuse with **-phobia** fear · **-phylaxis** protection (prophylaxis).
[^pre-a]: **a- / an- (before a vowel)** — without, absence: afibrinogenaemia, anaemia, aplasia. Don't confuse with **ana-** up, again (anastomosis) · **ab-** away from · **ad-** towards.
[^suf-aemia]: **-aemia / -emia** — condition of the blood: afibrinogenaemia, hypoprothrombinaemia, hypoconvertinaemia. Don't confuse with **-uria** condition of the urine (haematuria) · **haemo-** as a prefix.
[^pre-hypo]: **hypo-** — under, below, deficient: hypoprothrombinaemia, hypoconvertinaemia, hypotension. Don't confuse with **hyper-** over, excessive · **hypno-** sleep.
[^pre-para]: **para-** — beside, resembling, abnormal: parahaemophilia (resembling haemophilia), paracrine, parasympathetic. Don't confuse with **peri-** around · **pre-** before.
[^pre-ante]: **ante-** — before: antecedent, antenatal, anterior. Don't confuse with **anti-** against (antithrombin) · **post-** after.
[^pre-exo]: **exo-** — outside, outer (Greek): exogenous, exocrine, exocytosis. Don't confuse with **ecto-** outer layer (ectoderm) · **extra-** outside (Latin equivalent) · **endo-** within.
[^suf-lysis]: **-lysis / -lytic** — loosening, dissolving, breaking down: fibrinolysis, haemolysis, thrombolysis, glycolysis. Don't confuse with **-lyte** a substance that dissolves (electrolyte) · **-plasty** surgical shaping.
