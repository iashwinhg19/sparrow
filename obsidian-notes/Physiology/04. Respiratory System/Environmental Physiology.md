# 🏔️ Environmental Physiology

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> Environmental physiology studies how the body copes when the **pressure or gravity around it changes**. Everything hangs on one number — at sea level **760 mmHg × 21 % O₂ = PO₂ 160 mmHg** — and what happens when the total pressure falls, rises or the pull of gravity changes:
> - ✅ **High altitude** → **↓ atmospheric pressure → ↓ PO₂ → hypoxia** → **acclimatisation** (↑ ventilation first, then EPO, VEGF, cellular changes, right shift) or **mountain sickness**
> - ✅ **Deep sea** → **pressure ↑ 1 atm per 10 m** → gases compressed → **quick ascent releases N₂ bubbles → decompression (Caisson) sickness** → **hyperbaric chamber**
> - ✅ **Space** → **microgravity** (bone, muscle and RBC loss) and **G forces** (**+G black-out, −G red-out**)

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Soda Bottle on a Mountain, in the Sea and on a Roller-Coaster

Think of the body as a sealed soda bottle. **Carry it up a mountain** and there is less air pressing in — less O₂ gets in (hypoxia). **Take it down into the sea** and the water squeezes extra gas into solution; **open it quickly at the surface** and the gas fizzes out as bubbles (decompression sickness). **Swing it on a roller-coaster** and the liquid sloshes to one end — blood drains from the head (**black-out**) or floods it (**red-out**):

```
ENVIRONMENT          PRESSURE / FORCE                   PROBLEM                         BODY'S ANSWER / Rx
Level land           760 mmHg, O2 21 % → PO2 160         —                               —
HIGH ALTITUDE        ↓ atmospheric pressure → ↓ PO2      HYPOXIA                         ↑ ventilation (earliest) → EPO → VEGF → cytochrome oxidase ↑ → right shift
                                                         AMS (6–12 h), HAPE, HACE        descent, O2, nifedipine, dexamethasone
                                                         Chronic (Monge's): polycythaemia → pulmonary HTN → cor pulmonale   venesection, acetazolamide
DEEP SEA             +1 atm every 10 m (gas compressed)  quick ascent → N2 bubbles       hyperbaric chamber → slow ascent → slow decompression
                                                         euphoria · chokes · bends · air embolism
SPACE                microgravity                        ↓ Ca2+/PO4 bone, ↓ muscle, ↓↓ RBC
                     +G (head → foot)                    blood to feet → BLACK-OUT
                     −G (foot → head)                    blood to head → RED-OUT
```

## 🌍 On Level Lands

- **Atmospheric**[^ety-atmosphere] **pressure = 760 mmHg.**
- **O₂ content: 21 %.**
- **PO₂ = 21 % of 760 = 160 mmHg.**

🧠 The **percentage** of O₂ stays 21 % at every altitude; what changes is the **total pressure**, so the PO₂ that pushes O₂ into the blood changes with it.[^1]

## 🏔️ High Altitude Physiology

- **Atmospheric pressure ↓ → ↓ PO₂ → hypoxia**[^ety-hypoxia][^pre-hypo][^suf-ia].

### Acclimatisation[^ety-acclimatisation][^pre-ad] (compensatory changes)

| Mechanism | Response |
|---|---|
| **↑ ventilation**[^ety-ventilation][^suf-ation] **(earliest change)** (d/t activation of **peripheral**[^ety-peripheral][^pre-peri] **chemoreceptors**[^ety-chemoreceptor]) | • **Respiratory alkalosis**[^ety-alkalosis][^suf-osis] (**CO₂ washout → ↓ PCO₂, ↓ H⁺**). • **Hypocalcemia**[^ety-hypocalcaemia][^suf-aemia] **→ tetany**[^ety-tetany] (**↓ free form of Ca²⁺**). |
| **Erythropoietin**[^ety-erythropoietin][^pre-erythro][^suf-poiesis] **stimulation** | **Polycythemia**[^ety-polycythaemia][^pre-poly] (**↑ RBC count**) |
| **↑ Vascular endothelial**[^ety-endothelium][^pre-endo] **growth factors (VEGF)** | **↑ vascularity (d/t ↑ angiogenesis**[^ety-angiogenesis][^pre-angio][^suf-genesis]**)**; **↑ O₂ diffusion**[^ety-diffusion] |
| **Cellular acclimatisation (↑ cytochrome**[^ety-cytochrome] **oxidase activity)** | **↑ O₂ utilization** |
| **Release of oxygen** | **Right shift on oxygen dissociation curve**[^2] |

🧠 *Order of events:* breathing changes within **minutes to hours** (peripheral chemoreceptors), blood changes over **days to weeks** (EPO → more red cells), and tissue changes (new capillaries, more mitochondrial enzymes) over **weeks**. The right shift comes from **↑ 2,3-DPG** and helps unload O₂ in the tissues.[^3]

#### Clinical aspect

**Normal mechanism:**

```
Respiratory alkalosis → ↑ HCO3−, ↓ PCO2 & ↓ H+
↓ H+ → ↑ Ca2+–albumin binding → ↓ free Ca2+ → TETANY
        (H+ & Ca2+ compete for binding with albumin)
```

- **Respiratory alkalosis → ↑ HCO₃⁻, ↓ PCO₂ & ↓ H⁺.**[^4]
- **↓ H⁺ → ↑ Ca²⁺–albumin**[^ety-albumin] **binding → ↓ free Ca²⁺ → tetany.**
  - **H⁺ & Ca²⁺ compete for binding with albumin.**

**Renal compensation**[^ety-compensation][^pre-com]**:**

```
Respiratory alkalosis → ↑ HCO3− → excreted via kidney → ↓ HCO3− → prevents respiratory alkalosis (& hence prevents tetany)
```

- **Respiratory alkalosis → ↑ HCO₃⁻ → excreted via kidney → ↓ HCO₃⁻ → prevents respiratory alkalosis & hence prevents tetany.**[^5]

### High altitude illness

| | **Acute mountain sickness** | **Chronic mountain sickness / Monge's**[^ety-monge] **disease** |
|---|---|---|
| **Features** | • **6–12 hrs after ascent.** • **C/f: nausea, vomiting, confusion.** • **Complications:** – **High altitude pulmonary**[^ety-pulmonary] **edema**[^ety-oedema] **(HAPE)** – **High altitude cerebral**[^ety-cerebral] **edema (HACE)** | **C/f:** **Prolonged hypoxia (d/t ↑ erythrocytosis**[^ety-erythrocytosis][^suf-cytosis]**) → pulmonary hypertension**[^ety-hypertension][^pre-hyper] **→ cor pulmonale**[^ety-cor-pulmonale] |
| **Rx** | • **Immediate descent.** • **O₂ therapy.** • **Ca²⁺ channel blocker (nifedipine**[^ety-nifedipine]**).** • **Dexamethasone**[^ety-dexamethasone]**.** | • **Venesection**[^ety-venesection]**.** • **Acetazolamide**[^ety-acetazolamide]**.** |

```
CHRONIC MOUNTAIN SICKNESS
Prolonged hypoxia (↑ erythrocytosis) → pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale
```

🧠 *Why each drug:* **nifedipine** lowers the pulmonary artery pressure that hypoxic vasoconstriction has raised (prevents/treats **HAPE**); **dexamethasone** reduces brain oedema (**HACE**) and AMS symptoms; **acetazolamide** makes the kidney excrete HCO₃⁻, causing a mild metabolic acidosis that **drives ventilation** — speeding acclimatisation (and used for AMS prophylaxis).[^6] **Venesection** removes the excess red cells that make the blood thick in Monge's disease.

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Every problem in this chapter comes from **pressure acting on gas** or **gravity acting on blood**. The **fraction** of O₂ never changes (21 %); the **partial pressure** does, because it equals fraction × total pressure. Up a mountain the total falls, PO₂ falls and the body defends O₂ delivery in layers — ventilation first (peripheral chemoreceptors), then blood (EPO), vessels (VEGF), cells (cytochrome oxidase) and haemoglobin unloading (right shift); each layer has a price (alkalosis and tetany, polycythaemia and cor pulmonale).
>
> Under the sea every 10 m adds 1 atm, so more N₂ dissolves (Henry's law); a quick ascent lets it come out as bubbles — exactly like opening a shaken soda — and the treatment is to push the pressure back up and come down slowly. In space and in aircraft, gravity's direction decides where the blood goes: **+G drains the head (black-out), −G floods it (red-out)**.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> At sea level PO₂ is 160 mmHg in dry air (~100 in the alveolus). Ventilation is driven mainly by CO₂; peripheral chemoreceptors are quiet; acid–base and ionised Ca²⁺ are normal.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> On ascent, falling PO₂ fires the peripheral chemoreceptors → hyperventilation (earliest change) → respiratory alkalosis, which can drop free Ca²⁺ enough to cause tingling or tetany; over days the kidney excretes HCO₃⁻ (renal compensation), EPO raises the red-cell count, VEGF adds capillaries, 2,3-DPG shifts the curve right. Divers who ascend slowly let dissolved N₂ leave through the lungs.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> A fast ascent to altitude without acclimatisation produces acute mountain sickness at 6–12 hours, progressing to HAPE (hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, capillary leak) or HACE (brain oedema). Years at altitude cause excessive erythrocytosis, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale (Monge's disease). A diver's rapid ascent releases N₂ bubbles — bends, chokes, neurological deficits, arterial gas embolism and death.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Ascent → ↓ PO₂ → hyperventilation + alkalosis → renal, haematological and tissue acclimatisation → (too fast) AMS → HAPE / HACE; (too long) polycythaemia → pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale. Dive → N₂ loading → rapid ascent → bubbles → DCS → hyperbaric recompression.
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> "Climb high, sleep low", graded ascent and acetazolamide prevent AMS; descent is the definitive treatment of HAPE and HACE; recompression in a hyperbaric chamber is the definitive treatment of decompression sickness; flying soon after diving or rapid cabin depressurisation can trigger DCS.

> Pressure rises 1 atm per 10 m of seawater (2 atm at 10 m, 4 atm at 30 m); AMS that progresses to ataxia (HACE) or breathlessness at rest (HAPE) is an emergency needing immediate descent.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "The O₂ percentage falls at altitude"**
>
> **What students think:** There is less than 21 % O₂ in mountain air.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The atmosphere is 21 % O₂ at every altitude; only the total (barometric) pressure falls, so PO₂ = 0.21 × Pb falls with it. At ~5500 m Pb is about half, so PO₂ is about 80 mmHg.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Same percentage, less pressure."
>
> **Trap 2: Naming polycythaemia as the earliest acclimatisation change**
>
> **What students think:** More red cells are the first response to altitude.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** EPO takes days and the red-cell mass weeks. The **earliest** change is **↑ ventilation** from peripheral chemoreceptor stimulation, within minutes — which is also why respiratory alkalosis appears first.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Order: ventilation (minutes–hours) → kidney HCO₃⁻ (days) → EPO and RBC (days–weeks) → capillaries and enzymes (weeks).
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking alkalosis lowers total calcium**
>
> **What students think:** Tetany at altitude means total serum calcium has fallen.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Total Ca²⁺ is unchanged. With fewer H⁺ ions, more albumin binding sites are free, so more Ca²⁺ binds albumin and the **ionised (free)** fraction falls — and only free Ca²⁺ stabilises nerve membranes.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Alkalosis hides calcium on albumin."
>
> **Trap 4: Mixing up acute and chronic mountain sickness treatment**
>
> **What students think:** Venesection is used for acute mountain sickness.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** AMS / HAPE / HACE are acute hypoxic emergencies — descent, O₂, nifedipine, dexamethasone. Venesection removes the excess red cells of **chronic** mountain sickness (Monge's disease), where the problem is thick blood and pulmonary hypertension; acetazolamide is used in both (prophylaxis of AMS, ventilatory stimulant in CMS).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Acute = get down and give O₂; chronic = remove blood.
>
> **Trap 5: Reversing black-out and red-out**
>
> **What students think:** Positive G sends blood to the head.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In +G (head-to-foot acceleration, e.g. pulling out of a dive) inertia drives blood to the **feet**: venous pooling → ↓ venous return → ↓ cardiac output → ↓ cerebral perfusion → **black-out**. In −G (foot-to-head) blood is driven to the **head** → congestion of the eyes → **red-out**.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Follow the arrow of G: head → foot = blood leaves the head (black); foot → head = blood floods the head (red).

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Level land: **760 mmHg**, **O₂ 21 %**, **PO₂ = 160 mmHg**.
> 2. Altitude: **↓ atmospheric pressure → ↓ PO₂ → hypoxia**.
> 3. **Earliest acclimatisation change: ↑ ventilation** (peripheral chemoreceptors) → **respiratory alkalosis** + **hypocalcaemia → tetany** (↓ free Ca²⁺).
> 4. Other acclimatisation: **EPO → polycythaemia**; **↑ VEGF → angiogenesis, ↑ O₂ diffusion**; **↑ cytochrome oxidase → ↑ O₂ utilisation**; **right shift** of O₂ dissociation curve.
> 5. **↓ H⁺ → ↑ Ca²⁺–albumin binding → ↓ free Ca²⁺ → tetany** (H⁺ and Ca²⁺ compete for albumin).
> 6. **Renal compensation:** HCO₃⁻ excreted → prevents respiratory alkalosis and tetany.
> 7. **Acute mountain sickness** — **6–12 h after ascent**, nausea, vomiting, confusion; complications **HAPE, HACE**; Rx **immediate descent, O₂, nifedipine, dexamethasone**.
> 8. **Chronic mountain sickness (Monge's disease)** — prolonged hypoxia (↑ erythrocytosis) → **pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale**; Rx **venesection, acetazolamide**.
> 9. Deep sea: **pressure ↑ 1 atm per 10 m**; exposed — **divers, military, caisson workers, recreation**.
> 10. **Decompression (Caisson's / diver's) sickness:** quick ascent → **N₂ bubbles** → **euphoria (N₂ narcosis), chokes (lungs), bends (joints), air embolism → death**; Rx **hyperbaric chamber → slow ascent → slow decompression**; also seen in **pilots and mountaineers**.
> 11. **Microgravity:** loss of **Ca²⁺ & PO₄³⁻ from bone (fractures)**, **loss of muscle mass**, **↓↓ RBC count**.
> 12. **+G → ↓ venous return → ↓ CO → ↓ cerebral perfusion → black-out**; **−G → ↑ venous return → ↑ CO → ↑ cerebral perfusion → red-out**.

[^1]: Barometric pressure falls roughly exponentially with height: about 523 mmHg at 3000 m (PO₂ ~110), 349 mmHg at 6000 m (PO₂ ~73), and 253 mmHg on the summit of Everest, 8848 m (PO₂ ~53 mmHg). Inside the airways water vapour still takes 47 mmHg, so the relative loss of alveolar O₂ is even larger than that of air.
[^2]: The right shift at altitude comes from **↑ 2,3-DPG** in the red cells, which favours unloading in the tissues. At extreme altitude the respiratory alkalosis shifts the curve **left** instead, helping O₂ loading in the lungs — the net shift depends on how high and how long.
[^3]: Guyton lists five acclimatisation mechanisms: (1) great increase in pulmonary ventilation, (2) increased red cells, (3) increased diffusing capacity of the lungs, (4) increased vascularity of peripheral tissues, (5) increased ability of tissue cells to use O₂ despite low PO₂ (more mitochondria and oxidative enzymes). Ventilation rises immediately by ~65 % and, over 2–5 days, up to about five-fold as the CSF and renal bicarbonate adjust (the "brake" of alkalosis is released). Red-cell mass rises slowly over weeks; haematocrit can reach ~60 %. HIF-1 (hypoxia-inducible factor) switches on both EPO and VEGF.
[^4]: ⚠ The page writes "↑ HCO₃⁻" in the first line of the clinical aspect. In acute respiratory alkalosis plasma HCO₃⁻ actually falls a little (CO₂ + H₂O ⇄ H₂CO₃ ⇄ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ is pulled to the left as CO₂ is blown off), and the HCO₃⁻/PCO₂ ratio rises — which is what raises the pH. The renal compensation then lowers HCO₃⁻ further. The page's wording is kept in the body.
[^5]: Renal compensation takes 2–3 days: the kidney secretes less H⁺ and excretes more HCO₃⁻, returning pH towards normal. Acetazolamide (a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) produces the same HCO₃⁻ loss deliberately.
[^6]: Acetazolamide 125–250 mg twice daily, started a day before ascent, prevents AMS; dexamethasone treats moderate–severe AMS and HACE; nifedipine (and other pulmonary vasodilators — sildenafil, tadalafil) prevents and treats HAPE by lowering pulmonary artery pressure. HAPE is a non-cardiogenic oedema from uneven, exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction with stress failure of the overperfused capillaries.
[^7]: Seawater: each 10 m (≈ 33 ft) adds 1 atm (760 mmHg), so at 10 m total pressure is 2 atm, at 30 m 4 atm. By **Boyle's law** gas volumes in the body halve at 10 m; by **Henry's law** the amount of N₂ dissolved in body fluids rises in proportion to its partial pressure, over hours of exposure.
[^8]: N₂ is used because it is inert (not consumed) and five times more soluble in fat than in water; it accumulates in fatty tissue and nerves during a dive and leaves only slowly. Divers decompress in stages ("decompression tables"); helium–oxygen mixtures replace N₂ for very deep dives to avoid narcosis.
[^9]: Strictly, **nitrogen narcosis** ("rapture of the deep") is caused by the **high partial pressure of dissolved N₂** at depth (from about 30–40 m), acting like an anaesthetic on neuronal membranes — it occurs during the dive, not from bubbles on ascent. Neurological decompression sickness (bubbles in the spinal cord or brain) is a separate problem. The page lists euphoria among bubble effects; both are kept as the page has them.
[^10]: Pilots of unpressurised aircraft above ~5500–7500 m, or after sudden cabin decompression, and climbers at extreme altitude are suddenly exposed to low barometric pressure, so N₂ already dissolved at sea level can form bubbles — "altitude decompression sickness". Flying within 12–24 hours of scuba diving carries the same risk.
[^11]: Microgravity also causes a fluid shift towards the head (puffy face, "bird legs"), a fall in plasma volume (~10–15 %) with "space anaemia" (↓ RBC), cardiovascular deconditioning with orthostatic intolerance on return, and bone loss of ~1–2 % per month, mainly in weight-bearing bones. Exercise in flight is the main countermeasure.
[^12]: Positive G (+Gz) is the acceleration of pulling out of a dive or a tight turn: at +4 to +6 G vision greys ("grey-out") then fails ("black-out") and consciousness is lost (G-LOC); anti-G suits and the straining manoeuvre push blood back to the heart. Negative G (−Gz), as in an outside loop, drives blood to the head — throbbing headache, conjunctival congestion and "red-out" (from congested lower eyelids drawn over the visual field) — tolerated poorly beyond −2 to −3 G.
