# 🎚️ Regulation of Blood Pressure

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Blood pressure is the single most important factor regulating the CVS** — every organ's blood flow depends on it, so the body defends it with layered controls that differ in **speed**: nervous reflexes in **seconds** (baroreflex[^ety-baroreflex][^pre-baro][^ety-reflex], chemoreflex, CNS ischemic response), fluid shifts and hormones in **hours** (capillary fluid shift, stress relaxation, RAAS), and the kidney in **days** (pressure diuresis).
> - ✅ Exam questions ask: **which mechanism works at which BP level and time scale**, **the baroreflex arc** (receptor, nerve, nucleus, transmitter), **the named cardiac reflexes**, and **which chemicals constrict or dilate**.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Three Guards on Three Shifts

Picture three guards protecting the pressure in a city's water mains. The **night-watchman** (baroreflex) reacts in seconds to every wobble but gets tired — he resets to a new level if the change lasts days. The **day-shift engineer** (capillary shift, stress relaxation, RAAS) takes hours to adjust the pipes and the reservoir. The **city council** (kidney) takes days but never gives up until the volume in the system — and so the pressure — is exactly right.

```
SECONDS — SHORT TERM (CNS, rapid)
   Baroreflex (MAP 70–140)  →  Chemoreflex (MAP 40–70)  →  CNS ischemic response (MAP ≤ 40, most powerful)
HOURS — INTERMEDIATE TERM
   Capillary fluid shift · Stress relaxation · RAAS
DAYS — LONG TERM
   Kidney: pressure diuresis → fluid volume & urine output
PLUS  cardiac reflexes (Bezold–Jarisch, Cushing, Bainbridge) · BP waves (Mayer, Traube–Hering)
      chemicals: constrictors ↑BP (urotensin, NE, endothelin, vasopressin, Ang II) · dilators ↓BP (CGRP, NO, ANP/BNP/CNP, bradykinin)
```

## ⚙️ Mechanisms

**Blood pressure: single most important factor regulating the CVS.**

| 1. Short term | 2. Intermediate term | 3. Long term |
|---|---|---|
| **CNS controlled** | **Activation within hours** | **Activation within days** |
| **Rapid acting** | a. **Capillary fluid shift** | **Regulation by kidney (pressure diuresis**[^ety-diuresis][^pre-dia][^suf-uresis]**)** — **regulates fluid volume & urine output** |
| a. **Baroreflex** | b. **Stress relaxation**[^ety-relaxation] | |
| b. **Chemoreflex**[^pre-chemo] | c. **Renin**[^ety-renin] **Angiotensin**[^ety-angiotensin][^pre-angio] **Aldosterone**[^ety-aldosterone] **system (RAAS)** | |
| c. **CNS ischemic**[^ety-ischaemia] **response** | | |

🧠 *In plain words:* nerves correct BP in seconds but tire and reset; hormones and fluid shifts take hours; the kidney takes days but has the last word, because it sets the blood volume.[^1][^2]

**Capillary fluid shift — what the page's figure shows:**
- **Vasodilation: ↓ pressure.**
- **Vasoconstriction: ↑ pressure.**
- 🧠 *How the shift corrects BP:* when arterial (and so capillary) pressure is **high**, more fluid is filtered out into the tissues → blood volume ↓ → BP comes down; when it is **low**, plasma oncotic pressure draws tissue fluid back in → blood volume ↑ → BP comes up.[^3]

![Capillary exchange — fluid leaves at the arterial end and returns at the venous end (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/08/2108_Capillary_Exchange.jpg/800px-2108_Capillary_Exchange.jpg)
*Capillary fluid shift: the page's figure shows the **arterial end of the capillary**, **tissue cells**, **interstitial fluid** and the **venous end**, with fluid pushed out where capillary **pressure** is high and drawn back where it is low — the same exchange as here (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY 4.0).*

## ⚡ Short Term Regulation

### Baroreflex

> [!info] **↑ BP → (via baroreflex — negative feedback) → ↓ BP**
> The reflex senses stretch of the arterial wall and corrects the pressure in the opposite direction — the classic negative feedback loop of [[Homeostasis]].

| Mechanism | Baroreflex |
|---|---|
| **Stimulus** | **↑ BP** |
| **Receptor** | **Baroreceptor (distension**[^ety-distension]**)** — **carotid** & **aortic** |
| **Afferent (buffer / sinus nerves)** | **CN IX : Hering**[^ety-hering] **nerve** (from the carotid sinus) · **CN X : Cyon's**[^ety-cyon] **nerve** (from the aortic arch)[^4] |
| **Centre** | **Nucleus Tractus Solitarius**[^ety-nts] **(in medulla**[^ety-medulla]**)** |
| **Efferent** | NTS —glutamate→ **caudal ventrolateral medulla (CVLM)**[^ety-caudal] —GABA[^ety-gaba]→ **rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM)** → **sympathetic**[^ety-sympathetic][^pre-syn] **inhibition**; NTS —glutamate→ **nucleus ambiguus**[^ety-ambiguus] → **parasympathetic**[^pre-para] **activation** |
| **Effector** | **Heart & blood vessel** |
| **Response** | **↓ BP**, **↓ HR** |

```
                         ↑ BP
                          ↓
             Baroreceptor (distension)
              • Carotid        • Aortic
                 ↓                 ↓
       CN IX: Hering nerve   CN X: Cyon's nerve
                 └───────┬─────────┘
          Nucleus Tractus Solitarius (in medulla)
             ┌───────────┴────────────┐
         Glutamate                Glutamate
             ↓                        ↓
  Caudal ventrolateral medulla   Nucleus ambiguus
          (CVLM)                      │
             ↓ GABA                   │
  Rostral ventrolateral medulla       │
          (RVLM)                      │
             ↓                        ↓
   Sympathetic inhibition    Parasympathetic activation
             └──────────┬─────────────┘
                Heart & blood vessel
                        ↓
                   ↓ BP · ↓ HR
```

- The NTS works two levers at once: it releases the brake on the vagus (nucleus ambiguus) and, through the CVLM's GABA, puts a brake on the sympathetic pacemaker (RVLM).[^5]

![Baroreceptor reflex — high BP slows the heart and dilates vessels; low BP does the opposite (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4d/2116_Baroreceptor_Reflex_Flow_Art.jpg/800px-2116_Baroreceptor_Reflex_Flow_Art.jpg)
*The baroreceptor reflex as a loop: ↑ BP → baroreceptors fire → cardiovascular centre → ↓ sympathetic, ↑ parasympathetic → ↓ HR, vasodilation → BP back to normal; ↓ BP drives the reverse (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY 4.0).*

- **Marey's**[^ety-marey] **law: ↑ BP leads to reflex ↓ in HR (BP ∝ 1/HR).**[^6]

#### 1. Supine → standing

                                        ↓                                      ↓
                                  SNS activated                       if SNS underactivity
                           Release of norepinephrine                          ↓

### Other baroreceptors

- **Atrial**[^ety-atrium] **& pulmonary**[^ety-pulmonary] **artery baroreceptors.**
- **Low pressure receptor (volume sensing receptor).**
- **2 types of atrial stretch receptors:**

| Type | Activated during |
|---|---|
| **Type A** | **Atrial systole**[^ety-systole] |
| **Type B** | **Atrial diastole** |

- These receptors sense how **full** the low-pressure side is, rather than the arterial pressure.[^10]

### Regulation at different levels of BP

| Line | Receptor / response | Stimulus | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1st line** | **Baroreceptors** | **MAP of 70–140 mmHg** | **Up to MAP of 180 mmHg** |
| **Second line** | **Chemoreceptors** | **MAP of 40–70 mmHg** | Chemoreflex — HR effect variable |
| **Last line / last ditch stand** | **CNS ischemic response** | **MAP ≤ 40 mmHg** | **↑↑ MAP (as high as 250 mmHg) (most powerful)** |

- Each line takes over as the pressure falls below the range of the one before it.[^11]

**Chemoreflex:**

```
              ┌→ Direct effect   → Activates PNS (↓ HR) ┐
Chemoreflex ──┤                                          ├→ Effect on heart rate VARIABLE
              └→ Indirect effect → Activates SNS (↑ HR) ┘
```

- **Direct effect → activates PNS (↓ HR)**; **indirect effect → activates SNS (↑ HR)** → **effect on heart rate variable**.[^12]

**CNS ischemic response — last line of control / last ditch stand mechanism:**

```
MAP ≤ 40 mmHg
     ↓
↓ Cerebral perfusion (d/t low perfusion pressure)
     ↓
Activation of RVLM (SNS)
     ↓
↑↑ MAP (as high as 250 mmHg) — MOST POWERFUL
```

- Stimulus: **MAP ≤ 40 mmHg** → **↓ cerebral perfusion**[^ety-perfusion][^pre-per] **(d/t low perfusion pressure)** → **activation of RVLM (SNS)** → **↑↑ MAP (as high as 250 mmHg) (most powerful)**.[^13]

| Wave | Cause | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Mayer**[^ety-mayer] **waves / vasomotor**[^ety-vasomotor][^pre-vaso] **waves** | **Oscillation of nervous control mechanisms** | Slow rhythmic rise and fall of BP |
| **Traube-Hering**[^ety-traube-hering] **waves** | **Fluctuations in BP synchronised with respiration**[^ety-respiration] | **During inspiration**[^pre-in]: **↓ systolic blood pressure** · **During early part of expiration**[^pre-ex]: **↑ arterial pressure** |

```
                Traube–Hering waves (with respiration)
            ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
     During inspiration                 During early part of expiration
   ↓ Systolic blood pressure                ↑ Arterial pressure
```

- Mayer waves come from the nervous reflexes themselves oscillating;[^14] Traube–Hering waves follow the breathing cycle.[^15]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> BP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance, and the body defends it with **controls stacked by speed and by severity**. Over seconds the medulla adjusts HR and arteriolar tone through the baroreflex (normal range), the chemoreflex (MAP 40–70) and finally the CNS ischemic response (MAP ≤ 40 — the most powerful). Over hours capillary fluid shift, stress relaxation and RAAS change volume and tone; over days the kidney's pressure diuresis sets the volume — and so the pressure — for good.
>
> Every reflex in the chapter is a variation on one arc — **receptor → afferent nerve (IX, X) → NTS → sympathetic/vagal outflow → heart and vessels** — and every chemical either narrows the arterioles (constrictor, ↑ BP) or widens them (dilator, ↓ BP).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> MAP is held near 93–100 mmHg. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch fire with each pulse; the NTS keeps sympathetic tone (via CVLM → RVLM) and vagal tone (nucleus ambiguus) balanced. Standing up is corrected within seconds by norepinephrine-driven tachycardia and vasoconstriction.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Blood loss or a fall in pressure unloads the baroreceptors → tachycardia, vasoconstriction, cool pale skin; RAAS and capillary fluid shift start to restore volume over hours. Pressure is held, but the compensation is visible (HR up, pulse pressure narrow). Low-volume receptors in the atria also sense the drop.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Below MAP 70 the chemoreceptors join in; below 40 the brain itself becomes ischaemic and the CNS ischemic response drives MAP up to 250 mmHg — the last-ditch stand. If autonomic failure removes the reflexes (diabetic neuropathy), standing causes postural hypotension and syncope. Raised ICP triggers Cushing's reflex (hypertension with bradycardia) — a pre-terminal sign of brain herniation.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → fall in BP → baroreflex (seconds) → chemoreflex (MAP < 70) → CNS ischemic response (MAP ≤ 40) → intermediate (hours) and renal (days) restoration — or failure → shock
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The level at which each line works tells you how sick a patient is; vagal manoeuvres (carotid sinus massage) exploit the baroreflex to stop PSVT; drugs act on the same mediators — bosentan (endothelin), terlipressin (V1), nitrates (NO → cGMP), nesiritide (BNP), ACE inhibitors (angiotensin II and bradykinin).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: Hering's nerve vs Cyon's nerve**
>
> **What students think:** Both are branches of the vagus.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The **carotid sinus** sends its signals in **Hering's nerve, a branch of CN IX (glossopharyngeal)**; the **aortic arch** sends them in **Cyon's (aortic depressor) nerve, a branch of CN X (vagus)**. Together they are the "buffer nerves".
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Carotid = cranial nerve **9** (the carotid sits higher, the number is lower); aorta = **10**."
>
> **Trap 2: Clamping below vs above the carotid sinus**
>
> **What students think:** Any carotid clamp lowers BP.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Clamping the common carotid **below** the sinus lowers the pressure *in the sinus* → the baroreceptors fire less → the brain reads "low BP" → **↑ BP & ↑ HR**. Clamping **above** the sinus traps blood in it → sinus pressure **rises** → baroreflex activated → **↓ BP & ↓ HR**.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Ask only "what happens to the pressure inside the sinus?" — the reflex always opposes that.
>
> **Trap 3: Most potent vasoconstrictor and vasodilator**
>
> **What students think:** Endothelin or angiotensin II is the most potent vasoconstrictor; nitric oxide the most potent vasodilator.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In the page's list **urotensin (II)** is the **most potent vasoconstrictor** and **CGRP** the **most potent vasodilator**; endothelin is the most potent *endothelium-derived* constrictor and NO the main physiological dilator.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**U**rotensin **U**p (constrict), **C**GRP **C**alm (dilate)."
>
> **Trap 4: Cushing's reflex is "just bradycardia"**
>
> **What students think:** Raised ICT directly slows the heart.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page shows the end-result (bradycardia). The chain is: ↑ ICT → brainstem ischaemia → massive sympathetic discharge → **hypertension** → the baroreflex then slows the heart. The full Cushing triad is hypertension, bradycardia and irregular respiration.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Remember the order "pressure up first, heart down second".
>
> **Trap 5: The chemoreflex always speeds the heart**
>
> **What students think:** Hypoxia → sympathetic → tachycardia, always.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The **direct** effect of chemoreceptor stimulation is **vagal (↓ HR)**; the **indirect** effect (via increased breathing and SNS) is **↑ HR**. Which wins depends on the situation, so the page writes "effect on heart rate variable".
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Chemoreflex: direct = PNS, indirect = SNS, net = variable."

> The carotid and aortic bodies that drive the chemoreflex are the same peripheral chemoreceptors that drive breathing.
>
> **Related Topic 5: [[Adrenal Gland]]**

>
> **Related Topic 6: [[Collecting Duct and Micturition Reflex]]**
> Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors on vessels and V2 receptors in the collecting duct; ANP opposes it.
>

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **BP = single most important factor regulating the CVS.**
> 2. **Short term** (CNS, rapid): **baroreflex, chemoreflex, CNS ischemic response**; **intermediate** (hours): **capillary fluid shift, stress relaxation, RAAS**; **long term** (days): **kidney — pressure diuresis**.
> 3. Baroreflex = **negative feedback**: ↑ BP → carotid & aortic baroreceptors (**distension**) → **CN IX Hering nerve / CN X Cyon's nerve** → **NTS (medulla)** → glutamate → **CVLM** → GABA → **RVLM** → sympathetic inhibition; glutamate → **nucleus ambiguus** → parasympathetic activation → **↓ BP, ↓ HR**.
> 4. **Carotid sinus = small dilation in the internal carotid artery.**
> 5. **Marey's law:** ↑ BP → reflex ↓ HR.
> 6. Occlusion of common carotid / clamping **below** sinus → baroreflex **inhibited** → ↑ BP & ↑ HR; clamping **above** sinus → baroreflex **activated** → ↓ BP & ↓ HR.
> 7. Standing → ↓ BP → SNS → norepinephrine → ↑ HR + α-vasoconstriction → ↑ TPR; **SNS underactivity → postural hypotension**.
> 8. **Carotid sinus massage (Czermak–Hering test) → ↓ HR → Rx of PSVT.**
> 9. Atrial & pulmonary artery baroreceptors = **low pressure (volume) receptors**; **Type A — atrial systole**, **Type B — atrial diastole**.
> 10. **Baroreceptors: MAP 70–140 (response up to 180)**; **chemoreceptors: MAP 40–70** (HR effect variable); **CNS ischemic response: MAP ≤ 40 → MAP up to 250 — most powerful, last-ditch**.
> 11. **Mayer (vasomotor) waves** — oscillation of nervous control; **Traube–Hering waves** — with respiration: ↓ SBP in inspiration, ↑ in early expiration.
> 12. **Bezold–Jarisch:** MI → serotonin → bradycardia & hypotension; **Cushing:** head injury → ↑ ICT → bradycardia; **Bainbridge:** fluid overload (CCF) → SA node stretch → tachycardia.
> 13. Constrictors: **urotensin (most potent)**, NE (α), endothelin (ETA → PAH → **bosentan**), vasopressin (V1; **terlipressin** for oesophageal varices), angiotensin II.
> 14. Dilators: **CGRP (most potent)**, NO (cGMP; angina), ANP/BNP/CNP (cGMP; **nesiritide = BNP analogue**), bradykinin.

[^1]: RAAS in one line: ↓ renal perfusion → renin from the juxtaglomerular cells → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (ACE, mainly in the lungs) → angiotensin II → arteriolar constriction + aldosterone (Na⁺ and water retention) → BP ↑. It takes about 20 minutes to become fully active and keeps working for hours.
[^2]: Long-term control: a rise in arterial pressure makes the kidney excrete more salt and water (pressure natriuresis and diuresis) until volume and pressure fall back; Guyton calls this renal–body fluid mechanism the one with near-infinite gain (see [[Homeostasis]]). Stress relaxation: when volume rises, the vessels — especially veins — slowly stretch over minutes to hours so the pressure falls back toward normal; after haemorrhage the reverse happens.
[^3]: The explanation is added; the page gives only "vasodilation: ↓ pressure, vasoconstriction: ↑ pressure" beside its capillary figure. Guyton describes the capillary fluid shift as acting within minutes to hours through the Starling forces (see [[Characteristics of Blood Vessels]]).
[^4]: Hering's nerve = the carotid sinus nerve, a branch of the glossopharyngeal; Cyon's nerve = the aortic depressor nerve, running with the vagus. They are called "buffer nerves" because they buffer moment-to-moment swings of BP: cutting them makes BP very labile but hardly changes its average.
[^5]: NTS = nucleus of the tractus solitarius. Glutamate excites the CVLM, whose GABA-ergic neurons inhibit the RVLM — the source of tonic sympathetic outflow to the heart and vessels. The cardio-inhibitory (vagal) neurons lie in the nucleus ambiguus and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus.
[^6]: The page writes "(BP × 1/HR)" ⚠, read here as "BP ∝ 1/HR" — heart rate varies inversely with blood pressure. Étienne-Jules Marey described this relation in the mid-19th century. Exceptions: the Bainbridge reflex, exercise and emotion, where HR and BP rise together.
[^7]: These are classic animal experiments (and the basis of the Czermak–Hering test). The carotid sinus is a dilation at the start of the internal carotid artery, so its pressure is set by flow coming up the common carotid: a clamp below the sinus lowers it, a clamp above the sinus traps blood in it and raises it.
[^8]: Orthostatic hypotension = a fall in SBP ≥ 20 mmHg or DBP ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing. About 500–800 mL of blood pools in the legs on standing. Causes: autonomic neuropathy (diabetes, Parkinson's, Shy–Drager), α-blockers and other drugs, volume depletion, prolonged bed rest.
[^9]: Carotid sinus massage is a vagal manoeuvre that can terminate re-entrant PSVT (AVNRT, AVRT) by slowing AV-nodal conduction. It is avoided in the elderly and in patients with a carotid bruit (risk of stroke); an exaggerated response is "carotid sinus hypersensitivity", a cause of syncope.
[^10]: Type A receptors fire with atrial contraction (pressure); type B receptors fire during atrial filling (late diastole, volume). Stretch of the atria by more volume → ↓ ADH release, ↑ ANP release, renal vasodilation and the Bainbridge reflex — the "volume reflexes".
[^11]: Guyton's ranges differ slightly: baroreceptors respond between ~60 and 180 mmHg (most sensitive near 100); chemoreceptors are strongly stimulated below ~80 mmHg (most active 40–80); the CNS ischemic response begins below ~60 mmHg and is greatest at 15–20 mmHg. The page's ranges are kept in the body.
[^12]: The carotid and aortic bodies sense ↓ O₂, ↑ CO₂ and ↑ H⁺, which build up when low pressure reduces their blood flow. Their *direct* (primary) cardiac effect is vagal bradycardia; the *indirect* effect — through increased breathing (lung-inflation reflex) and sympathetic vasomotor stimulation — speeds the heart, so the net effect varies.
[^13]: When MAP falls so low that the vasomotor centre itself is ischaemic, accumulated CO₂ and lactic acid excite it intensely — Guyton calls it one of the most powerful activators of the sympathetic vasoconstrictor system. Cushing's reflex is a special form of it.
[^14]: Mayer waves are slow oscillations of about 0.1 Hz (one every ~10 s in humans) caused by the baroreceptor and chemoreceptor reflexes "hunting" around their set point — Sigmund Mayer, 1876.
[^15]: During inspiration intrathoracic pressure falls, blood pools in the expanding pulmonary vessels and LV output falls briefly → SBP drops (normally < 10 mmHg; more than that is *pulsus paradoxus*, as in cardiac tamponade); in early expiration that pooled blood reaches the left ventricle → pressure rises.
[^16]: The receptors are vagal C-fibre endings in the left ventricular wall, especially the infero-posterior wall — hence the bradycardia and hypotension of inferior MI. Serotonin, veratrum alkaloids and ischaemia stimulate them. The page writes "Bezold–Jarisch"; it is also called the coronary chemoreflex.
[^17]: ICT on the page = intracranial tension (= intracranial pressure, ICP). Raised ICP compresses the cerebral arteries → brainstem ischaemia → massive sympathetic discharge → hypertension, which the baroreflex answers with bradycardia; irregular breathing completes **Cushing's triad**, a late sign of herniation.
[^18]: Stretch of the right atrium and SA node raises the heart rate: stretch of the node itself adds 10–15 %, and the vagal-afferent Bainbridge reflex adds another 40–60 % (Guyton). It helps the heart pump away extra venous return; it opposes Marey's law, since BP and HR rise together.
[^19]: The page says endothelin is produced "from endothelial cell of lungs"; endothelin-1 is made by endothelial cells throughout the body, with the lungs both a major source and the main site of clearance. ETA receptors on smooth muscle cause constriction; ETB receptors on endothelium release NO and clear endothelin. Bosentan blocks both (dual antagonist); ambrisentan is ETA-selective.
[^20]: Vasopressin acts on V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle (Gq → Ca²⁺ → constriction) and on V2 receptors in the collecting duct (water reabsorption). Terlipressin, a long-acting analogue, constricts the splanchnic arterioles and lowers portal pressure in bleeding oesophageal varices and hepatorenal syndrome.
[^21]: CGRP is the most potent vasodilator peptide (CGRP blockers now treat migraine). NO is made from L-arginine by NO synthase and activates soluble guanylyl cyclase → cGMP; nitrates relieve angina by releasing NO. Natriuretic peptides act on guanylyl cyclase-linked receptors (→ cGMP). Bradykinin is broken down by ACE (kininase II), which is why ACE inhibitors cause cough and angioedema.

[^ety-calcitonin]: **calcitonin** — Latin *calx*, "lime (calcium)" + Greek *tonos*, "tone, tension" + *-in* → the hormone that regulates the tone (level) of calcium (Copp, 1962); *CGRP* = calcitonin gene-related peptide, made from the same gene by alternative splicing in nerves.
[^ety-angina]: **angina** — Latin *angina*, "a strangling, quinsy" (*angere*, "to choke, squeeze") → *angina pectoris*, "strangling of the chest" (William Heberden, 1768) — chest pain of myocardial ischaemia.
[^ety-natriuretic]: **natriuretic** — Latin *natrium*, "sodium" (from Egyptian *ntry*, natron) + Greek *ourēsis*, "urination" + *-tic* → promoting sodium excretion in urine; ANP (atrial), BNP (brain — first found in pig brain, made mainly by ventricles), CNP (type C, endothelium).
[^ety-bradykinin]: **bradykinin** — Greek *bradys*, "slow" + *kinein*, "to move" + *-in* → it makes gut smooth muscle contract *slowly* (Rocha e Silva, 1949); a potent vasodilator broken down by ACE.
[^pre-baro]: **baro-** — weight, pressure: baroreflex, baroreceptor, barotrauma. Don't confuse with **brady-** slow · **bari-** heavy (bariatric).
[^pre-dia]: **dia-** — through, across, apart: diuresis, diastole, dialysis. Don't confuse with **di-** two · **dys-** bad, difficult.
[^suf-uresis]: **-uresis / -uria** — urination, a condition of the urine: diuresis, natriuresis, polyuria, haematuria. Don't confuse with **-aemia** a condition of the blood · **uro-** urine as a prefix (but see *urotensin*, where *uro-* = tail).
[^pre-chemo]: **chemo-** — chemical: chemoreflex, chemoreceptor, chemotherapy. Don't confuse with **cheilo-** lip · **chemosis** (swelling of the conjunctiva — Greek *chēmē*, a gaping shell).
[^pre-angio]: **angio-** — vessel (Greek *angeion*): angiotensin, angiography, angioedema. Don't confuse with **angina** (Latin *angere*, to choke — a different root) · **vaso-** vessel (Latin equivalent).
[^pre-syn]: **syn- / sym-** — together: sympathetic, synapse, systole. Don't confuse with **dia-** apart · **sin-** left.
[^pre-para]: **para-** — beside, beyond, abnormal: parasympathetic, paroxysmal (*para-* + *oxys*), paracrine. Don't confuse with **peri-** around · **pre-** before.
[^pre-ob]: **ob- (oc-, of-, op-)** — against, in the way, completely; the *b* turns into the next consonant: occlusion, obstruction, obesity. Don't confuse with **ab-** away from · **ad-** towards.
[^pre-epi]: **epi-** — upon, over: epinephrine (upon the kidney), epicardium, epithelium. Don't confuse with **endo-** within · **hypo-** under · **ecto-** outer.
[^suf-ine]: **-ine** — an amine, alkaloid or basic substance: norepinephrine, epinephrine, histamine, atropine. Don't confuse with **-in** a neutral protein or peptide (angiotensin, bradykinin) · **-ase** an enzyme.
[^pre-peri]: **peri-** — around: peripheral, pericardium, periosteum. Don't confuse with **para-** beside · **pre-** before · **circum-** around (Latin equivalent).
[^pre-hypo]: **hypo-** — under, below, deficient: hypotension, hypoxia, hypothalamus. Don't confuse with **hyper-** over · **hypno-** sleep.
[^pre-supra]: **supra-** — above (Latin): supraventricular (above the ventricles — atria and AV node), suprarenal. Don't confuse with **super-** above · **sub- / infra-** below.
[^pre-tachy]: **tachy-** — swift, fast: tachyarrhythmia, tachycardia, tachypnoea. Don't confuse with **brady-** slow · **brachy-** short.
[^pre-a]: **a- / an-** — without, not: arrhythmia (literally "without rhythm"), anaemia, anoxia. Don't confuse with **ana-** up, again · **ab-** away · **ad-** towards.
[^pre-per]: **per-** — through, thoroughly (Latin): perfusion, permeable, percutaneous. Don't confuse with **peri-** around (Greek) · **pre-** before.
[^pre-vaso]: **vaso-** — vessel (Latin *vas*): vasomotor, vasoconstriction, vasopressin. Don't confuse with **vas deferens / vasectomy** (sperm duct) · **angio-** vessel (Greek equivalent).
[^pre-in]: **in- (im-)** — two Latin prefixes: **in-** "into" (inspiration, infarction, infusion) and **in-** "not" (inactive). Context decides. Don't confuse with **en-** in (Greek: endemic) · **intra-** within.
[^pre-ex]: **ex- (ef-, e-)** — out of: expiration, excretion, efferent. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek: exocrine) · **extra-** beyond.
[^pre-myo]: **myo- / my-** — muscle: myocardial, myocardium, myoglobin. Don't confuse with **myelo-** marrow, spinal cord · **myco-** fungus.
[^pre-brady]: **brady-** — slow: bradycardia, bradykinin, bradykinesia. Don't confuse with **tachy-** fast · **baro-** pressure · **brachy-** short.
[^suf-cardia]: **-cardia** — a condition of the heart: bradycardia, tachycardia, dextrocardia. Don't confuse with **cardia** (the part of the stomach next to the heart) · **-cardium** a heart layer (myocardium).
[^pre-intra]: **intra-** — within: intracranial, intracellular, intravascular. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **intro-** into.
[^pre-uro]: **uro-** — two Greek roots: *ouron*, **urine** (urology, urobilinogen) and *oura*, **tail** (urotensin, urophysis, *Uro*dela — tailed amphibians). Don't confuse them; and don't confuse with **ur-** German "original".
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, excessive: hypertension, hyperkalaemia, hypertrophy. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under · **supra- / super-** above.
[^suf-sentan]: **-sentan** — drug stem for endothelin receptor antagonists: bosentan, ambrisentan. Don't confuse with **-sartan** angiotensin II receptor blockers (losartan) · **-pril** ACE inhibitors (enalapril).
[^suf-pressin]: **-pressin** — vasopressin and its analogues: vasopressin, terlipressin, desmopressin, felypressin. Don't confuse with **-pressor** a drug that raises BP (vasopressor) · **-tocin** oxytocin analogues (carbetocin).
[^suf-tide]: **-tide** — drug stem for peptides: nesiritide (BNP), liraglutide, teriparatide, octreotide. Don't confuse with **-tidine** H₂-blockers (ranitidine) · **-ide** a simple chemical ending (chloride).
