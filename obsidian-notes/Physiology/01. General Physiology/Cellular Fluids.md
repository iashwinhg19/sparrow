# 💧 Cellular Fluids

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Water is the maximum contributor of the body / cell** — about **60 % of body weight** (42 L in a 70-kg man). It sits in two main compartments[^ety-compartment]:
> - ✅ **Intracellular fluid (ICF) = 2/3 of TBW** (28 L) — K⁺-rich.
> - ✅ **Extracellular fluid (ECF) = 1/3 of TBW** (14 L) — Na⁺-rich: interstitial fluid, plasma and transcellular fluid.
>
> Each volume is **measured by indicator dilution (V = A / C)**, and every disturbance of volume and osmolarity can be read off a **Darrow–Yannet diagram**.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Two Tanks Joined by a Water-Permeable Wall

Imagine two tanks — a big one (ICF, 2/3) and a small one (ECF, 1/3) — separated by a wall that lets **water** through freely but not **Na⁺**. Na⁺ is trapped in the small tank, so **the small tank's salt decides where the water goes**: add salt to the ECF and water leaves the cells; lose salt from the ECF and water enters the cells. Adding or losing *isotonic* fluid changes only the small tank's size.

```
TOTAL BODY WEIGHT → 50–60 % water · 18 % protein · 15 % fat · 7 % minerals
TBW 42 L (60 %) = ICF 28 L (40 %, 2/3) + ECF 14 L (20 %, 1/3) → plasma 3.5 L · IF 10.5 L · TF 1–2 L
MEASURE: indicator dilution V = A / C  (TBW: D2O, ³H2O · ECF: inulin · PV: ¹³¹I-albumin, Evans blue · BV: ⁵¹Cr-RBC)  IF = ECF − PV · ICF = TBW − ECF
DARROW–YANNET (Y = osmolarity, X = litres):
   0.9 % saline in → ECF ↑, osm same      diarrhoea → ECF ↓, osm same
   ↑ NaCl (HTN)    → ECF ↑, ICF ↓, osm ↑   adrenal insufficiency → ECF ↓, ICF ↑, osm ↓
```

## 🧪 Measurements of Body Fluids

**Method: Stewart Hamilton**[^ety-stewart-hamilton] **method / indicator**[^ety-indicator] **dilution**[^ety-dilution][^pre-di-dis] **method.**

**Procedure:**

```
Inject an indicator into the fluid compartment
                     ↓
   Measure its concentration in the fluid
```

**Formula:**
- **Amount of fluid injected (A) = Concentration (C) × Volume of the compartment (V).**

```latex
A = C \times V \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad V = \frac{A}{C}
```

**Modified formula:**

```latex
\text{Volume} = \frac{\text{Amount injected} - \text{Amount excreted}}{\text{Concentration}}
```

🧠 Drop a known amount of dye into a bucket of unknown size, stir, and measure how dilute it became: a big bucket dilutes it a lot, a small one a little. Subtract whatever leaked out (excreted) before you divide.[^4]

### Indicators & formulas

| Volume | Indicators[^5] |
|---|---|
| **Total body water** | **Deuterium**[^ety-deuterium] **oxide**, **Tritium oxide** |
| **Extracellular fluid** | **Inulin**[^ety-inulin] **(m/c)**, Sucrose, Mannitol[^ety-mannitol], Sodium thiosulphate |
| **Plasma volume (PV)** | **Albumin**[^ety-albumin] **(Radiolabelled)**, **Evans blue**[^ety-evans-blue] **dye** |
| **Blood volume (BV)** | **Chromium tagged RBCs** |

| Volume | Formulas |
|---|---|
| **Interstitial fluid** | **No indicators. Calculated by indirect method:** ECF = PV + IF → **IF = ECF − PV** |
| **Intracellular fluid** | **No indicators:** TBW = ECF + ICF → **ICF = TBW − ECF** |

> [!note] Note
> 1. **Formula to calculate blood volume:**
>
> ```latex
> BV = \frac{PV}{1 - \text{Hematocrit}}
> ```
>
> 2. **Blood volume in child = 70 mL/kg.**[^6]

🧠 Plasma is only part of blood; the haematocrit[^ety-haematocrit] is the fraction that is cells, so dividing plasma volume by the plasma fraction (1 − Hct) scales it up to the whole blood volume.

## 🧫 Fluid Compartments

- **Fluid compartments contain water and ions.**

| | ECF[^7] | ICF |
|---|---|---|
| **Dominant cations** | **Na⁺** | **K⁺ > Mg²⁺** |
| **Dominant anions** | **Cl⁻** | **Phosphates > Protein anions** |
| **pH** | **7.4** | **7** |

### Water loss

```
                                  Types
               ┌────────────────────┴────────────────────┐
       Sensible water loss                  Insensible water loss (600–700 ml/day)
               │                                          ↓
      Sweating (100 ml/day)                         By evaporation
          m/c exercise                               ↙          ↘
                                                   Skin          Lung
```

- **Sensible**[^ety-sensible] **water loss:** **sweating (100 ml/day)** — m/c exercise.
- **Insensible**[^pre-in] **water loss (600–700 ml/day):** **by evaporation**[^ety-evaporation] — **skin** and **lung**.[^8]

### Plasma osmolarity

- **Plasma osmolarity**[^ety-osmolarity] **= 300 mosm/L.**
- **Mole**[^ety-mole]**: gram molecular weight of a substance.**
  - Eg: 1 mole of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = **58.5**.
- **Equivalent**[^ety-equivalent][^pre-equi]**: gram molecular weight / valency**[^ety-valency].
  - Eg: Ca²⁺ = 40 / 2 = **20**.

```latex
\text{Equivalent} = \frac{\text{Gram molecular weight}}{\text{Valency}}
```

**Contributors:**

| Contributor | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Na⁺** | **270 mosm/L** | **Major contributor** |
| **2. Proteins** | **0.75 mosm/L** | **Minor contributor** due to **high molecular weight** and **low concentration** |

🧠 Osmolarity counts **particles**, not grams. A protein molecule weighs thousands of times more than Na⁺, so even 7 g/dL of protein adds very few particles — under 1 mosm/L.[^9]

### Tonicity[^ety-tonicity]

```
                                RBC placed in
          ┌─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┐
  Isotonic solution         Hypotonic solution          Hypertonic solution
    (0.9 % NaCl)          (300 mmol/L of urea)       (300 mmol/L of mannitol)
          ↓                         ↓                          ↓
 No change in shape          Water enters RBC            Water moves out
                                    ↓                          ↓
                              Swells & bursts                Shrinks
```

| RBC placed in | Example on the page | Water | RBC |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isotonic**[^pre-iso] **solution** | **0.9 % NaCl** | No net movement | **No change in shape** |
| **Hypotonic**[^pre-hypo] **solution** | **300 mmol/L of urea**[^ety-urea] | **Water enters RBC** | **Swells & bursts**[^ety-haemolysis] |
| **Hypertonic**[^pre-hyper] **solution** | **300 mmol/L of mannitol**[^10] | **Water moves out** | **Shrinks**[^ety-crenation] |

### Factors preventing edema

- **Edema**[^ety-oedema] **is accumulation of fluid in interstitial space.**

![[Cellular Fluids - interstitial fluid compartment.png]]
*Interstitial fluid compartment — a capillary (arterial end → venous end) running between tissue cells; the space around the cells holds the interstitial fluid (cropped from the handwritten page).*

**Safety factor (SF):**

1. **Distensibility**[^ety-distensibility] **of the compartment:**
   - Normally **interstitial space is less distensible.**
   - **SF = 3 mm of Hg.**
2. **↑ Lymphatic**[^ety-lymph] **flow:**
   - Normally, **proteins in the blood vessels → colloid oncotic**[^ety-oncotic] **pressure.**
   - **Inflammation**[^ety-inflammation] **→ leakage of proteins from blood vessels.**
   - **Compensatory**[^ety-compensatory][^pre-com]**: ↑ lymphatic flow → moves proteins back inside → washout of proteins.**
     - a. **↑ Lymphatic flow → SF = 7 mm Hg.**
     - b. **Washout of proteins → SF = 7 mm Hg.**
   - **Total SF = 3 + 7 + 7 = 17 mm Hg.**[^11]

| Safety factor against edema | mm Hg |
|---|---|
| Low distensibility of interstitial space | **3** |
| ↑ Lymphatic flow | **7** |
| Washout of interstitial proteins | **7** |
| **Total** | **17** |

🧠 Before oedema appears, the body has a 17 mm Hg "buffer": a stiff interstitium that resists filling, lymph that speeds up, and lymph that carries away the leaked protein so its oncotic pull disappears.

## 📊 Darrow Yannet Diagram

- **Alterations in plasma osmolarity & volume → diseases.**
- **These diseases can be understood via Darrow Yannet**[^ety-darrow-yannet] **diagram.**

![[Cellular Fluids - normal Darrow Yannet diagram.png]]
*Normal Darrow Yannet diagram — **Y axis = osmolarity** (300 mOsm/L), **X axis = litres**; the box is split into **ICF (2/3rd)** on the left and **ECF (1/3rd)** on the right; the height is the same in both because water moves freely until both have the same osmolarity (cropped from the handwritten page).*

🧠 How to read every Darrow–Yannet box: **width = volume, height = osmolarity**, and **ICF and ECF heights always end up equal** (water crosses cell membranes freely). The solid line is the new state, the dashed line the normal.[^12]

### Saline infusion

- **0.9 % NaCl infusion.**
- **Treatment of shock.**

| | Volume | Osmolarity |
|---|---|---|
| **ECF** | **↑** | **No change** |
| **ICF** | **No change** | **No change** |

![[Cellular Fluids - Darrow Yannet saline infusion.png]]
*DY: saline infusion — the ECF box widens (more litres) while the height (osmolarity) and the ICF box stay the same (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Diarrhea

- **Loss of 0.9 % NaCl.**

| | Volume | Osmolarity |
|---|---|---|
| **ECF** | **↓** | **No change** |
| **ICF** | **No change** | **No change** |

![[Cellular Fluids - Darrow Yannet diarrhoea.png]]
*DY: diarrhoea — the ECF box narrows; osmolarity and ICF unchanged (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Hypertension[^ety-hypertension]

**Pathogenesis:**

```
↑ NaCl in ECF (↑ osmolality / hypertonic)
                 ↓
   Fluid movement from ICF to ECF
```

| | Volume | Osmolarity |
|---|---|---|
| **ECF** | **↑** | **↑** |
| **ICF** | **↓** | **↑** |

![[Cellular Fluids - Darrow Yannet hypertension.png]]
*DY: hypertension — the whole box rises (↑ osmolarity in both); the ECF widens and the ICF narrows as water moves out of cells (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Adrenal insufficiency

- **Hypoosmotic volume contraction.**

**Pathogenesis:**

```
Loss of aldosterone
        ↓
   Loss of Na⁺
        ↓
↓ ECF osmolarity
        ↓
Fluid moves from ECF to ICF
```

- **Loss of aldosterone**[^ety-aldosterone] → **loss of Na⁺** → **↓ ECF osmolarity** → **fluid moves from ECF to ICF**.[^13]

| | Volume | Osmolarity |
|---|---|---|
| **ECF** | **↓** | **↓** |
| **ICF** | **↑** | **↓** |

![[Cellular Fluids - Darrow Yannet adrenal insufficiency.png]]
*DY: adrenal insufficiency — **hypo-osmotic volume contraction**: the whole box drops below 300 mOsm/L, the ECF narrows and the ICF widens (axes: osmolarity 0–300 mOsm/L, volume 0–40 L) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **Water follows solute, and Na⁺ is the ECF's solute.** Cell membranes let water through freely but keep Na⁺ out (thanks to the Na⁺–K⁺ pump), so the ICF and ECF always end at the **same osmolarity**: gaining or losing isotonic fluid changes only the ECF's size, while gaining or losing **salt** drags water between the compartments. The Darrow–Yannet diagram is simply this rule drawn as two boxes.
>
> Every volume can be measured because of one equation, **V = A / C**, with an indicator that stays in exactly the compartment you want (D₂O everywhere, inulin outside cells, albumin/Evans blue in plasma); the compartments without their own indicator (IF, ICF) are found by subtraction.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> TBW 42 L (60 % of 70 kg): ICF 28 L, ECF 14 L (IF 10.5, plasma 3.5, TF 1–2); plasma osmolarity ~300 mosm/L, 270 from Na⁺. Insensible loss 600–700 mL/day is replaced by drinking; the interstitium carries a 17 mm Hg safety margin against oedema.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Isotonic loss (diarrhoea) shrinks only the ECF — tachycardia, thirst, postural drop, but normal Na⁺; infants, with 75 % water and high turnover, reach this stage fastest. A mild rise in capillary leak is absorbed by ↑ lymph flow and protein washout without visible oedema.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Large ECF loss → hypovolaemic shock; adrenal insufficiency → hyponatraemia with water moving into cells (hypo-osmotic contraction — hypotension and cerebral swelling together); excess salt → hyperosmolality, cells shrink, ECF expands (hypertension). Once the 17 mm Hg safety factor is exceeded, pitting oedema appears.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → Trigger (fluid or salt loss/gain, ↓ aldosterone, capillary leak) → Early Changes (ECF volume or osmolarity shift) → Compensation (thirst, ADH, aldosterone, lymph flow) → Decompensation (shock, cell swelling or shrinking, oedema) → Full Clinical Syndrome
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> The fluid chosen for treatment follows the diagram: isotonic saline (0.9 % NaCl) expands only the ECF — ideal for shock and diarrhoea; hypotonic fluids spread into cells; hypertonic saline or mannitol pulls water out of swollen brain cells. Adrenal crisis needs both salt (saline) and hormone (hydrocortisone).

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Isosmotic volume contraction (diarrhoea, haemorrhage):** "Loss of 0.9 % NaCl", "ECF ↓, osmolarity unchanged, ICF unchanged" → isotonic loss, no water shift into or out of cells.
>
> **Isosmotic volume expansion (saline infusion):** "0.9 % NaCl infusion", "treatment of shock", "ECF ↑ only" → isotonic gain stays in the ECF.
>
> **Hyperosmotic volume expansion (high NaCl intake):** "↑ NaCl in ECF", "water moves from ICF to ECF", "ICF ↓, osmolarity ↑" → the salt-driven picture of hypertension.
>
> **Hypo-osmotic volume contraction (adrenal insufficiency / Addison's):** "Loss of aldosterone", "salt wasting", "ECF ↓, ICF ↑, osmolarity ↓" → Na⁺ lost more than water.
>
> **Indicators:** "Deuterium / tritium oxide" → TBW; "inulin" → ECF; "Evans blue / radio-albumin" → plasma volume; "⁵¹Cr-tagged RBC" → blood (red cell) volume.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> In a Darrow–Yannet box: wider = more volume, taller = higher osmolarity; ICF and ECF heights always equal. On the page's RBC tree: swell and burst = hypotonic (urea), shrink = hypertonic.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Plasma osmolarity normal ≈ 300 (285–295) mosm/L; Na⁺ contributes ~270. Total safety factor against oedema = 17 mm Hg. Insensible loss rises with fever and tachypnoea.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Iso-osmotic means isotonic"**
>
> **What students think:** 300 mmol/L of urea is iso-osmotic, so RBCs in it keep their shape.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Tonicity counts only solutes that **cannot** cross the membrane. Urea enters the RBC freely, water follows it, and the cell swells and bursts — so 300 mmol/L urea is iso-osmotic but **hypotonic**. NaCl and mannitol stay outside and are "effective" osmoles.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Osmolarity counts all particles; tonicity counts only the ones that stay outside."
>
> **Trap 2: Interstitial fluid and ICF have their own indicators**
>
> **What students think:** There must be a dye for every compartment.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** No substance stays only in the interstitium or only inside cells; IF = ECF − PV and ICF = TBW − ECF are calculated by subtraction.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Measured: TBW, ECF, PV, BV. Calculated: IF, ICF."
>
> **Trap 3: Diarrhoea changes plasma osmolarity**
>
> **What students think:** Losing fluid concentrates the plasma.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Diarrhoeal fluid is roughly isotonic (like 0.9 % NaCl), so the ECF shrinks with no change in osmolarity and no water shift from the cells — the ICF is untouched. Only if water is replaced without salt, or lost without salt, does osmolarity change.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Ask "what did the patient lose — water, salt, or both in equal proportion?"
>
> **Trap 4: Women and men have the same TBW percentage**
>
> **What students think:** 60 % applies to everyone.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** TBW tracks lean body mass; women have more adipose tissue (which is ~10 % water) and less lean mass, so ~50 %; men ~60 %; infants ~75 %; it falls further in obesity and old age.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Fat is dry, muscle is wet."
>
> **Trap 5: Proteins are important contributors to plasma osmolarity**
>
> **What students think:** Because albumin holds water in vessels, it must make up much of plasma osmolarity.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Proteins add only ~0.75 mosm/L of the ~300 (high molecular weight, few particles). Their importance is across the **capillary wall**, where they are the only solutes that cannot cross — that small difference (colloid oncotic pressure, ~25 mm Hg) is what holds fluid in vessels.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Proteins: tiny share of osmolarity, big share of oncotic pressure."

> 5. **Stewart–Hamilton / indicator dilution: V = A / C**; modified: **(amount injected − amount excreted) / concentration**.
> 6. Indicators: **TBW — D₂O, tritium oxide**; **ECF — inulin (m/c), sucrose, mannitol, Na thiosulphate**; **PV — radio-albumin, Evans blue**; **BV — Cr-tagged RBCs**.
> 7. **IF = ECF − PV**, **ICF = TBW − ECF** (no indicators); **BV = PV / (1 − Hct)**; blood volume in child **70 mL/kg**.
> 8. **ECF: Na⁺, Cl⁻, pH 7.4**; **ICF: K⁺ > Mg²⁺, phosphates > protein anions, pH 7**.
> 9. Insensible water loss **600–700 mL/day** (skin + lung, evaporation); sweating **100 mL/day**.
> 10. Plasma osmolarity **300 mosm/L**; **Na⁺ 270 (major)**, **proteins 0.75 (minor — high MW, low concentration)**; **equivalent = GMW / valency (Ca²⁺ = 20)**.
> 11. RBC in **0.9 % NaCl → no change**; **300 mmol/L urea → hypotonic → swells & bursts**; **mannitol → water out → shrinks** (as on the page).
> 12. Safety factors against oedema: **distensibility 3 + lymph flow 7 + protein washout 7 = 17 mm Hg**.
> 13. Darrow–Yannet: **saline → ECF ↑ only**; **diarrhoea → ECF ↓ only**; **HTN (↑NaCl) → ECF ↑, ICF ↓, osm ↑**; **adrenal insufficiency → ECF ↓, ICF ↑, osm ↓ (hypo-osmotic volume contraction)**.

[^1]: The page heads the last column "% of body water", but the figures 60 / 40 / 20 % are percentages of **body weight** (60 % of 70 kg = 42 L). As fractions of TBW the same compartments are 100 % / 67 % / 33 %. Guyton gives plasma ≈ 3 L and IF ≈ 11 L; the page's 3.5 L and 10.5 L (¼ and ¾ of the ECF) are the usual exam values.
[^2]: The page's formula is kept. In standard terms, **fat-free mass** = body mass − all fat, and **lean body mass** = body mass − storage (adipose) fat — LBM still includes bone, muscle, organs, water and a little essential fat. Because fat holds little water, TBW tracks LBM closely.
[^3]: Lean tissue is about 72–73 % water (≈ 70 mL per 100 g, as on the page); adipose tissue only about 10–20 %. This is why TBW can be estimated from LBM and vice versa (LBM ≈ TBW / 0.73).
[^4]: The method was developed for cardiac output by George Neil Stewart (1897) and refined by William F. Hamilton (1920s–30s); the same dilution principle measures any compartment, provided the indicator mixes evenly, stays in the compartment and is non-toxic. "Amount injected" refers to the amount of **indicator**.
[^5]: Typical indicators in more detail: TBW — ³H₂O, D₂O, antipyrine; ECF — inulin (most accurate), ²²Na, ¹²⁵I-iothalamate, thiosulfate, sucrose, mannitol; plasma — ¹²⁵I-albumin, Evans blue (T-1824); red cell volume — ⁵¹Cr-labelled RBCs (blood volume then = RCV / Hct).
[^6]: Textbook values: adult blood volume ≈ 70–80 mL/kg (about 5 L in a 70-kg man, ~7 % of body weight); newborns ≈ 80–90 mL/kg. The page's value for children (70 mL/kg) is kept in the body.
[^7]: Typical values (mEq/L): ECF Na⁺ 142, K⁺ 4, Cl⁻ 103–108, HCO₃⁻ 24–28; ICF K⁺ 140, Mg²⁺ ~58, Na⁺ 10–14, phosphates ~75 and protein anions ~40. Intracellular pH is about 7.0–7.2.
[^8]: Guyton's daily water balance (~2.3 L): insensible loss ~700 mL (skin ~350, lungs ~350), sweat ~100 mL, faeces ~100 mL, urine ~1.4 L. Sweating can rise to 1–2 L/hour in heavy exercise in the heat. Urine and faeces are also sensible (measurable) losses.
[^9]: Plasma proteins (~7 g/dL, molecular weight ~70 000 for albumin) give under 1 mosm/L, yet they are the only solutes that cannot cross the capillary wall — so this tiny amount produces the whole **colloid oncotic pressure** (~25–28 mm Hg) that holds fluid in vessels.
[^10]: ⚠ The page gives "300 mmol/L of mannitol" as the hypertonic example. Mannitol does not enter cells, so 300 mmol/L of mannitol is iso-osmotic **and isotonic** to plasma (no change in shape); a solution is hypertonic only above ~300 mosm/L of impermeant solute (e.g. 20 % mannitol ≈ 1100 mosm/L, 3 % NaCl ≈ 1030 mosm/L). The urea example is correct: 300 mmol/L urea is iso-osmotic but hypotonic because urea enters the cell.
[^11]: Guyton's safety factors against oedema: low compliance of the interstitium in the negative-pressure range (~3 mm Hg), increased lymph flow (~7 mm Hg) and washout of interstitial protein, which lowers interstitial oncotic pressure (~7 mm Hg) — together ~17 mm Hg; capillary pressure can rise ~17 mm Hg above normal before oedema appears.
[^12]: Darrow–Yannet rules: isotonic changes alter only ECF width; changes of ECF osmolarity shift water until ICF and ECF heights match again. Standard names: saline infusion = **isosmotic volume expansion**; diarrhoea = **isosmotic volume contraction**; high salt intake (the page's "hypertension") = **hyperosmotic volume expansion**; adrenal insufficiency = **hyposmotic volume contraction**; SIADH = hyposmotic volume expansion; sweating/dehydration = hyperosmotic volume contraction.
[^13]: In primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) aldosterone deficiency causes renal Na⁺ loss with K⁺ and H⁺ retention, so hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypotension and hypovolaemia occur together; cortisol deficiency adds impaired free-water excretion.
[^ety-compartment]: **compartment** — Latin *com-*, "together, thoroughly" + *partiri*, "to share, divide" → a part divided off: a body space whose fluid has its own volume and composition (ICF, ECF, plasma, IF, TF).
[^ety-transcellular]: **transcellular fluid** — Latin *trans-*, "across, beyond" + *cellula*, "small room" + *-ar* → fluid that has passed beyond (been secreted across) epithelial cells into special spaces: CSF, synovial, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, intraocular fluids, digestive juices. **Differentiate:** *transcellular fluid* (a compartment) · *transcellular transport* (a route through a cell).
[^ety-lubrication]: **lubrication** — Latin *lubricus*, "slippery" + *-ation* → making slippery: transcellular fluids (synovial, pleural, pericardial) let surfaces glide.
[^ety-plasma]: **plasma** — Greek *plasma*, "something moulded, formed" (*plassein*, to mould) → the fluid part of blood. **Differentiate:** *plasma* (with fibrinogen) · *serum* (after clotting, no fibrinogen) · *cytoplasm* (cell fluid).
[^ety-interstitial]: **interstitial** — Latin *inter*, "between" + *sistere*, "to stand" (*interstitium*, a space between) + *-al* → the fluid in the spaces between cells. **Differentiate:** *interstitial* (between cells) · *intravascular* (in vessels) · *intracellular* (in cells) · *transcellular* (in special cavities).
[^ety-diarrhoea]: **diarrhoea** — Greek *dia-*, "through" + *rhoia*, "a flow" → a flowing through: frequent loose stools; the fluid lost is roughly isotonic. **Differentiate:** *diarrhoea* (watery, ↑ frequency) · *dysentery* (blood and mucus) · *steatorrhoea* (fatty stools).
[^ety-dehydration]: **dehydration** — Latin *de-*, "removal" + Greek *hydōr*, "water" + *-ation* → loss of body water. **Differentiate:** *dehydration* (strictly, water loss → ↑ osmolarity) · *volume depletion / hypovolaemia* (salt and water loss from the ECF, osmolarity may be normal — the diarrhoea picture).
[^ety-adipose]: **adipose** — Latin *adeps, adipis*, "fat, lard" + *-ose*, "full of" → fatty tissue; ~10–20 % water, hence it lowers the TBW percentage. **Differentiate:** *adipose tissue* (fat-storing tissue) · *lipid* (the chemical class) · *lean mass* (non-fat tissue).
[^ety-stewart-hamilton]: **Stewart–Hamilton method** — **George Neil Stewart** (1860–1930), Canadian-born physiologist, who introduced indicator dilution (1897), and **William Fernington Hamilton** (1893–1964), American physiologist, who refined it with rapid dye injection and recirculation correction (1928–32).
[^ety-indicator]: **indicator** — Latin *indicare*, "to point out, show" (*in-*, towards + *dicare*, to proclaim) + *-tor* → a substance that shows (by its concentration) the volume it has mixed into. **Differentiate:** *indicator* (marker of volume) · *tracer* (radioactive marker followed over time) · *dye* (coloured indicator, Evans blue).
[^ety-dilution]: **dilution** — Latin *dis-* (*di-*), "apart" + *luere*, "to wash" + *-ion* → washing apart, making thinner: the injected indicator becomes more dilute the larger the volume it spreads into.
[^ety-deuterium]: **deuterium / tritium** — Greek *deuteros*, "second" / *tritos*, "third" → hydrogen isotopes with a mass of 2 and 3 (one or two neutrons); deuterium oxide (D₂O, "heavy water") and tritium oxide (³H₂O) mix with all body water. Deuterium was discovered by Harold Urey (1931).
[^ety-inulin]: **inulin** — Latin *Inula helenium*, the elecampane plant, from whose roots it was first extracted + *-in* → a fructose polymer (~5000 Da) that stays outside cells and is freely filtered but neither reabsorbed nor secreted (also the gold standard for GFR). **Don't confuse** with *insulin* (the hormone).
[^ety-mannitol]: **mannitol** — from *manna* (the sweet exudate of the manna ash, *Fraxinus ornus*) + *-itol*, a sugar alcohol → a six-carbon sugar alcohol that does not enter cells; used as an ECF marker and as an osmotic diuretic.
[^ety-albumin]: **albumin** — Latin *albumen*, "egg white" (*albus*, white) → the most abundant plasma protein (~4 g/dL), the main source of colloid oncotic pressure; radiolabelled (¹³¹I/¹²⁵I) albumin measures plasma volume.
[^ety-evans-blue]: **Evans blue (T-1824)** — named after **Herbert McLean Evans** (1882–1971), American anatomist and endocrinologist, who introduced the azo dye in 1914–15; it binds tightly to plasma albumin, so it stays in the plasma and measures plasma volume.
[^ety-haematocrit]: **haematocrit** — Greek *haima*, "blood" + *kritēs*, "judge" (*krinein*, to separate) → "blood judge/separator": the fraction of blood volume occupied by red cells after centrifugation (~0.40–0.45). **Differentiate:** *haematocrit* (packed cell volume, %) · *haemoglobin* (g/dL) · *RBC count* (cells/µL).
[^ety-sensible]: **sensible / insensible** — Latin *sensibilis*, "perceptible" (*sentire*, to feel) → *sensible* loss can be perceived and measured (sweat, urine, faeces); *in-* "not" → *insensible* loss cannot be felt (evaporation from skin and lungs).
[^ety-evaporation]: **evaporation** — Latin *e-* (*ex-*), "out" + *vapor*, "steam" + *-ation* → turning a liquid into vapour; each litre evaporated removes ~580 kcal of heat. **Differentiate:** *evaporation* (diffusion of water vapour, no glands) · *sweating* (active secretion by sweat glands, then evaporation).
[^ety-osmolarity]: **osmolarity / osmolality** — *osmole* (Greek *ōsmos*, "push" + *mole*) + *-arity* (per litre) or *-ality* (per kilogram of water) → concentration of osmotically active particles per litre of solution / per kg of solvent.
[^ety-mole]: **mole** — German *Mol*, short for *Molekül* (Latin *moles*, "mass" + diminutive *-cula* → a small mass) → the amount of a substance whose weight in grams equals its molecular weight (6.02 × 10²³ particles). Coined by Wilhelm Ostwald (1894).
[^ety-equivalent]: **equivalent** — Latin *aequus*, "equal" + *valere*, "to be worth" → "of equal worth": the amount of an ion that carries one mole of charge (gram molecular weight ÷ valency); 1 mmol Ca²⁺ = 2 mEq.
[^ety-valency]: **valency** — Latin *valentia*, "strength, capacity" (*valere*, to be strong) → the number of charges (combining power) an ion carries: Na⁺ 1, Ca²⁺ 2.
[^ety-tonicity]: **tonicity** — Greek *tonos*, "tension, stretching" + *-icity* → the effective osmotic "pull" of a solution on a cell, counting only the solutes that cannot enter it. **Differentiate:** *osmolarity* (all particles) · *tonicity* (impermeant particles only): iso- / hypo- / hyper-tonic.
[^ety-urea]: **urea** — Greek *ouron*, "urine" → the main nitrogenous waste; synthesised by Friedrich Wöhler (1828). It crosses cell membranes freely, so it is an "ineffective" osmole.
[^ety-haemolysis]: **haemolysis (swells & bursts)** — Greek *haima*, "blood" + *lysis*, "loosening, breaking" → bursting of red cells, here from water entry in a hypotonic solution. **Differentiate:** *haemolysis* (RBC rupture) · *haemostasis* (stopping of bleeding) · *haematolysis* (old synonym).
[^ety-crenation]: **crenation (shrinks)** — Latin *crena*, "a notch" + *-ation* → the notched, shrunken outline of an RBC that has lost water in a hypertonic solution. **Differentiate:** *crenation* (shrinking, notched — hypertonic) · *swelling / lysis* (hypotonic) · *echinocyte* (the spiky cell seen on a smear).
[^ety-oedema]: **oedema / edema** — Greek *oidēma*, "a swelling" (*oidein*, to swell) → excess fluid in the interstitial space. **Differentiate:** *oedema* (interstitial) · *effusion* (in a body cavity) · *anasarca* (generalised oedema).
[^ety-distensibility]: **distensibility** — Latin *dis-*, "apart" + *tendere*, "to stretch" + *-ibility* → the ability to be stretched; a less distensible (stiff) interstitium resists filling, giving the first safety factor. **Differentiate:** *distensibility* (volume change per pressure change relative to volume) · *compliance* (volume change per pressure change) · *elasticity* (tendency to recoil).
[^ety-lymph]: **lymph / lymphatic** — Latin *lympha*, "clear water" (linked to *nympha*, a water nymph) → the clear fluid returned from the interstitium to the blood through lymphatic vessels; ~2–3 L/day.
[^ety-oncotic]: **oncotic** — Greek *onkos*, "mass, bulk, swelling" + *-tic* → the osmotic pressure of the bulky plasma proteins (colloid oncotic pressure, ~25 mm Hg). **Differentiate:** *oncotic* (proteins only) · *osmotic* (all solutes) · *hydrostatic* (fluid pressure).
[^ety-inflammation]: **inflammation** — Latin *inflammare*, "to set on fire" (*in-*, into + *flamma*, flame) + *-ation* → the tissue's response to injury: redness, heat, swelling (from protein-rich leak), pain and loss of function. **Differentiate:** *inflammation* (the response) · *infection* (invasion by organisms — one cause of inflammation).
[^ety-compensatory]: **compensatory** — Latin *com-*, "together" + *pensare*, "to weigh" + *-ory* → weighing together, counterbalancing: a response that offsets a disturbance (↑ lymph flow offsets protein leak). **Differentiate:** *compensated* (disturbance offset, function preserved) · *decompensated* (reserves exhausted, disease overt).
[^ety-darrow-yannet]: **Darrow–Yannet diagram** — **Daniel Cady Darrow** (1895–1965), American paediatrician at Yale, and **Herman Yannet**, his colleague, who described in 1935 how changes in ECF salt shift water between ICF and ECF in dehydrated children; the box diagram bears their names.
[^ety-hypertension]: **hypertension** — Greek *hyper-*, "over, excessive" + Latin *tensio*, "stretching" (*tendere*, to stretch) → excessive pressure in the arteries; on this page it stands for the salt-loaded (hyperosmotic volume expansion) state.
[^ety-aldosterone]: **aldosterone** — *aldehyde* + *sterol* + *-one* → the adrenal-cortex steroid with an aldehyde group at C-18; it makes the distal nephron reabsorb Na⁺ (and water) and secrete K⁺, so its loss causes salt wasting.
[^pre-intra]: **intra-** — within: intracellular, intravascular, intraocular. Don't confuse with **inter-** between (interstitial) · **intro-** inward · **endo-** within (Greek).
[^pre-extra]: **extra-** — outside, beyond: extracellular, extravascular, extravasation. Don't confuse with **exo-** outside (Greek) · **ex-** out of · **intra-** within.
[^pre-trans]: **trans-** — across, through, beyond: transcellular, transport, transfusion. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **dia-** through (Greek) · **per-** through, thoroughly.
[^pre-inter]: **inter-** — between, among: interstitial, intercellular, intercostal. Don't confuse with **intra-** within · **infra-** below · **intro-** inward.
[^pre-dia]: **dia-** — through, across, apart: diarrhoea, dialysis, diaphragm. Don't confuse with **di-** two · **dys-** bad, difficult · **dis-** apart (Latin).
[^suf-rrhoea]: **-rrhoea / -rrhea** — flow, discharge: diarrhoea, rhinorrhoea, steatorrhoea, amenorrhoea. Don't confuse with **-rrhage** heavy bleeding (haemorrhage) · **-rrhaphy** suturing · **-rrhexis** rupture.
[^pre-de]: **de-** — down, away, removal: dehydration, deficiency, depolarisation. Don't confuse with **dis-** apart · **di-** two · **dys-** bad.
[^pre-di-dis]: **di- / dis- / dif- (Latin)** — apart, away: dilution, diffusion, distensibility, dissociation. Don't confuse with **di-** two (Greek: dimer) · **dia-** through · **dys-** bad.
[^pre-in]: **in- (im-, il-, ir-)** — two Latin prefixes: (1) "not" — insensible, insufficiency, impermeable; (2) "into" — inflammation (into flame), infusion, injection. Context decides; don't confuse with **intra-** within · **inter-** between.
[^pre-equi]: **equi-** — equal (Latin *aequus*): equivalent, equilibrium, equimolar. Don't confuse with **equine** of a horse · **iso-** equal (Greek equivalent).
[^pre-iso]: **iso-** — equal, same (Greek *isos*): isotonic, isosmotic, isotope, isometric. Don't confuse with **eso-** inward · **equi-** equal (Latin) · **homo-** same, identical.
[^pre-hypo]: **hypo-** — under, below, deficient: hypotonic, hypo-osmotic, hyponatraemia, hypovolaemia. Don't confuse with **hyper-** over · **hypno-** sleep · **sub-** below (Latin equivalent).
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, above, excessive: hypertonic, hypertension, hyperosmotic. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under · **supra- / super-** above (Latin equivalents).
