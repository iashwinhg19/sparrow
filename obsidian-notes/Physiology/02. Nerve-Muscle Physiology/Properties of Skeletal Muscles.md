# 💪 Properties of Skeletal Muscles

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> The **properties of skeletal muscle** describe how much **tension**[^ety-tension] a muscle makes and for how long — and every one of them comes back to **how many actin–myosin cross-bridges can form at a moment**. The chapter answers four questions:
> - ✅ **At what length** does a fibre pull hardest? → the **length–tension relationship** (optimal overlap = T max).
> - ✅ **Which fibres and motor units** do the work? → **Type I / IIa / IIb fibres** recruited by the **size principle**.
> - ✅ **What happens when stimuli come faster?** → single twitch → **incomplete tetanus** → **complete tetanus** (Ca²⁺ accumulation).
> - ✅ What is the by-product? → **muscle heat** (resting, activation, shortening, relaxation, recovery).

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Tug-of-War Team

Picture the muscle as a tug-of-war team. **Overlap** is how many hands are on the rope: crowded together (too short) or stretched so far that hands slip off (too long) both give a weak pull; the best pull comes at the middle length. **Fibre types** are the team's marathoners (Type I) and sprinters (Type II). **Motor units** are squads called in smallest first. **Stimulus frequency** is how fast the coach shouts "pull!" — shout fast enough and the separate pulls fuse into one long heave (tetanus).

```
LENGTH     → actin–myosin overlap → no. of cross-bridges → ACTIVE tension (max = plateau B–C)
STRETCH    → titin               → PASSIVE tension      → TOTAL tension = active + passive
FIBRES     → Type I  small, red, slow, fatigue resistant      → sitting / posture   (recruited 1st)
             Type IIa large, red, fast, fatigue resistant     → standing            (2nd)
             Type IIb large, white, fast, fatigue prone       → running             (last)
FREQUENCY  → single twitch → incomplete tetanus (ripples) → complete tetanus (Ca²⁺ accumulates)
```

## 📏 Length Tension Relationship

**Contraction**[^ety-contraction][^pre-con][^suf-ation] of a muscle fibre does two things at once — the fibre gets shorter and it pulls. The length–tension relationship asks how the **starting length** decides the **pull**.

```
              CONTRACTION OF A MUSCLE FIBRE
                ┌───────────┴────────────┐
   Shortening of muscle fibre      Generation of force
                ↓                        ↓
        Change in LENGTH          Change in TENSION
```

- Shortening of muscle fibre → change in **length**.
- Generation of force → change in **tension**.

![Sarcomere relaxed and contracted — actin and myosin filaments overlapping](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d8/Sarcomere_relaxed_contracted.PNG/800px-Sarcomere_relaxed_contracted.PNG)
*Actin–myosin overlap — the same thick (myosin) and thin (actin) filaments in a relaxed and a contracted sarcomere[^ety-sarcomere][^pre-sarco]; the page sketches this as red myosin sliding between blue actin (Wikimedia Commons).*

### Length tension relationship graph

- **Length is compared to active tension.**
- The amount of tension depends on how many myosin[^ety-myosin][^pre-myo][^suf-in] heads can grab actin[^ety-actin] — i.e. on the **actin–myosin overlap**.

| Point | Actin myosin overlap | Tension generated | Image |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Point D** | **Absent** | **No tension** | No overlap |
| **Point B & C** | **Optimal** | **Maximum tension (T max)** | Optimal overlap |
| **Point A** | **Present, but not optimal** | **Less tension** | Not optimal overlap |

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - overlap at points D, B–C and A.png]]
*The 'Image' column of the table — no overlap (point D), optimal overlap (points B & C) and not-optimal overlap (point A) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - length tension graph.png]]
*Length–tension graph — tension rises A → B, stays at its maximum on the plateau B–C (the '2μ' optimal zone) and falls to zero at D (cropped from the handwritten page).*

Reading the graph from left to right:
- **A** — fibre too short: the filaments crowd into each other, overlap is "present but not optimal" → less tension.
- **B–C** — the plateau, about **2μ** wide on the page's length axis: every myosin head faces actin → **T max**.[^1]
- **C → D** — fibre over-stretched: actin is pulled out from between the myosin → fewer cross-bridges → at **D** there is **no overlap and no tension**.

## ⚖️ Types of Tension & Length

```
                    TYPES OF TENSION
          ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
        Active          Passive            Total
```

### Active v/s passive tension

| | **Active** | **Passive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Tension generated** | When muscle **is stimulated**[^ety-stimulation][^suf-ation] | When muscle **is not stimulated** |
| **Proteins involved** | Actin myosin overlap → **cross bridge formation** → tension | **Titin**[^ety-titin] → passive stretching / **elasticity**[^ety-elasticity][^suf-ity] |

🧠 Active tension is made by the motor (cross-bridges, ATP); passive tension is made by a spring (titin) that resists stretch even in a resting muscle.

### Factors affecting active tension

- **Cross bridge formation.**
- **Diameter**[^ety-diameter][^pre-dia][^suf-meter] **of muscle fibre.**
- **Length of muscle fibre.**
- **AP frequency.**
- **No. of motor units recruited**[^ety-recruitment][^pre-re].

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - types of tension graph.png]]
*Types of tension — active tension (red dome), passive tension (black, rising only on stretch) and total tension = active + passive (green) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Total tension = Active + Passive.**
- Passive tension is near zero at short lengths and climbs steeply once the muscle is stretched beyond its optimal length — exactly where active tension is falling; so total tension dips and then rises again.[^2]

### Types of length

```
                      TYPES OF LENGTH
             ┌───────────────┴────────────────┐
  Optimal / resting length            Equilibrium length
  = maximum tension generated         = muscles are cut from
    by all body muscles                 bony attachments
```

| Type of length | Meaning on the page |
|---|---|
| **Optimal / resting length** | **Maximum tension** generated by all body muscles — the length a muscle has in the body |
| **Equilibrium**[^ety-equilibrium][^pre-equi] **length** | The length a muscle takes when it is **cut from its bony attachments** (no external stretch)[^3] |

### Types of muscle fibres

```
                      TYPES OF MUSCLE FIBRES
              ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
           Type I                                Type II
       SMALL & RED                   ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
                                  Type IIa                      Type IIb
                                LARGE & RED                  LARGE & WHITE
```

| Characteristics | Type I fibres | Type IIa fibres | Type IIb fibres |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Color** | Red | Red | White |
| **Myoglobin**[^ety-myoglobin][^suf-globin] **content** | (+) High | (+) High | (−) Absent |
| **Diameter** | Small | Large | Large |
| **Myosin ATPase**[^ety-atpase][^suf-ase] **(Contraction)** | Slow | Fast | Fast |
| **Metabolism**[^ety-metabolism][^pre-meta][^suf-ism] **— O₂ storage** | (+) | (+) | (−) |
| **Pathway to generate ATP** | **Aerobic**[^ety-aerobic][^pre-aero][^suf-ic] pathway | Aerobic or **anaerobic**[^pre-an] pathway (**glycolysis**[^ety-glycolysis][^pre-glyco][^suf-lysis]) | Anaerobic pathway (glycolysis) |
| **Lactate**[^ety-lactate][^suf-ate] | (−) | (+) | (+) **Pain: muscle fatigue**[^ety-fatigue] |
| **Fatigue** | Fatigue resistant | Fatigue resistant | **Fatigue prone** |
| **Motor units innervation**[^ety-innervation][^pre-in] | Slow fatigue resistant | Fast fatigue resistant | Fast fatiguable |
| **Recruitment order** | **First** d/t small diameter (**size principle**[^ety-henneman]) | Second | Last |
| **Activities best suited** | **Sitting, maintaining posture**[^ety-posture] | **Standing** | **Running** |

🧠 Red = myoglobin + mitochondria + O₂ → aerobic, slow, tireless. White = little myoglobin → anaerobic glycolysis → fast, strong, but lactate builds and it tires quickly.[^4]

## 🧩 Motor Unit

> [!info] **Single α motor neuron + the muscle fibres it innervates → motor unit.**
> One motor neuron fires → every fibre it supplies contracts together. The smaller the motor unit, the finer the control.[^5]

| Movement | Example | One motor unit innervates |
|---|---|---|
| **Fine precise movements** | **Extraocular**[^ety-extraocular][^pre-extra] **muscles** | **3–6 muscle fibres** |
| **Coarse movements** | **Leg / back muscles** | **600 muscle fibres** |

## 🔥 Muscle Heat

- **During contraction & relaxation → muscle produces heat.**

**Types of heat:**[^6]

| | **Resting heat** | **Contraction — Activation heat** | **Contraction — Shortening heat** | **Relaxation — Relaxation proper** | **Relaxation — Recovery heat** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Due to** | **Basal metabolic rate** | **Activation of actin & myosin** | **Overlap of actin & myosin** | **Isotonic**[^ety-isotonic][^pre-iso] **contraction — no tension change** | **Muscle regains original length** |

> [!note] Examples of ↑ resting heat
> - **Lean body.**
> - **After exercise (weight loss).**[^7]

🧠 Heat is the "exhaust" of contraction: ATP is split for cross-bridges and for pumping Ca²⁺ back, and most of that energy ends up as heat — which is why shivering warms you.

## ⚡ Stimulation of a Muscle

### Single muscle twitch[^ety-twitch]

One stimulus → one action potential → one brief contraction and relaxation.

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - single muscle twitch.png]]
*Single muscle twitch — stimulus → latent period → contraction (to peak) → relaxation (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Phase | When |
|---|---|
| **Latent**[^ety-latent] **period** | **Before contraction, after stimulation** |
| **Contraction** | **From end of latent period to peak concentration**[^8] |
| **Peak contraction** | The top of the curve |
| **Relaxation**[^ety-relaxation] | **After contraction** |

![Muscle twitch myogram — latent period, contraction phase, relaxation phase (OpenStax)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/1012_Muscle_Twitch_Myogram.jpg/800px-1012_Muscle_Twitch_Myogram.jpg)
*The same three phases of a twitch as a myogram[^ety-myogram][^suf-gram] (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY).*

### Incomplete tetanus

- **↑ frequency of stimulus → ↑ frequency of contraction.**
- **Incomplete relaxation** — each new stimulus arrives before the fibre has fully relaxed, so the twitches stack (summation[^ety-summation]) but the trace still ripples.

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - incomplete tetanus.png]]
*Incomplete tetanus — the trace climbs but keeps its ripples: relaxation between stimuli is incomplete (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Complete tetanus[^ety-tetanus]

- **↑↑↑ frequency of stimulus → sustained contraction & no relaxation.**
- **D/t Ca²⁺ ion accumulation** — Ca²⁺ is released again before the SR can pump it back, so it stays high and cross-bridges keep cycling.[^9]
- **Eg: Tetanospasmin**[^ety-tetanospasmin][^suf-spasm] (in tetanus d/t ***Clostridium tetani***[^ety-clostridium]):

```
Tetanospasmin (tetanus d/t Clostridium tetani)
        ↓
Spastic contracted state
        ↓
Body bends like a bow
```

🧠 "Body bends like a bow" is **opisthotonus**[^ety-opisthotonus] — the back muscles, being the strongest, win over the abdominal muscles and arch the whole body backwards.[^10]

![[Properties of Skeletal Muscles - complete tetanus.png]]
*Complete tetanus — the ripples fuse into one smooth sustained contraction (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Tetanising frequency (T_f)

- **Frequency of stimulus at which complete tetanus is produced.**

```latex
T_f = \frac{1}{\text{Contraction period}}
```

🧠 If a new stimulus comes at least once per contraction period, the fibre never gets the chance to relax — so the shorter the contraction period (fast fibres), the higher the frequency needed to tetanise it.[^11]

## 〰️ Fibrillation v/s Fasciculation

| | **Fibrillation**[^ety-fibrillation] | **Fasciculation**[^ety-fasciculation] |
|---|---|---|
| **No. of muscle fibres** | **Single** | **Multiple** |
| **No. of motor units** | **Single (α motor neuron or its axons)** | **Multiple** |

🧠 Fibrillation is one fibre twitching on its own — too small to see through the skin; fasciculation is a whole bundle twitching together — a visible flicker under the skin.[^12]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **Tension = the number of cross-bridges working at one moment.** Every property in this chapter changes that number in one of three ways: by **geometry** (how much actin each myosin can reach — the length–tension curve, maximum on the B–C plateau and zero at D), by **how many fibres are switched on** (motor units recruited small → large by the size principle, Type I → IIa → IIb), or by **how long Ca²⁺ stays up** (single twitch → incomplete tetanus → complete tetanus as Ca²⁺ accumulates).
>
> Passive tension is the exception that proves the rule — it uses no cross-bridges at all, only the titin spring — which is why it rises exactly where active tension falls.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> Muscles rest at their optimal (resting) length, so any stimulus finds maximal overlap. Posture is held by fatigue-resistant Type I units recruited first; bigger Type II units join only when more force is needed. Graded force comes from recruitment and from frequency (twitch → incomplete tetanus), never from a bigger action potential.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> Prolonged anaerobic work recruits Type IIb fibres, which depend on glycolysis → lactate and H⁺ accumulate → pain and fatigue; the person slows and Type I/IIa units take over. Partial denervation is compensated by surviving motor neurons sprouting to adopt orphan fibres, making fewer but larger motor units.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Loss of the motor neuron leaves single fibres firing on their own — **fibrillation** (seen only on EMG); a sick anterior horn cell fires its whole unit — visible **fasciculation**. Toxins that remove spinal inhibition (tetanospasmin) cause continuous firing → sustained, tetanic contraction → spastic rigidity, trismus and opisthotonus ("body bends like a bow").
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → overuse / denervation / toxin → fatigue or reinnervation → compensation by recruitment → loss of motor neurons or loss of inhibition → fibrillation / fasciculation / spastic tetanic state
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> EMG findings (fibrillation vs fasciculation) localise a lesion to the muscle fibre or the motor neuron; recognising tetanus as loss of inhibition (not over-excitation of muscle) explains why benzodiazepines and antitoxin, not muscle stimulants, are the treatment.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Optimal overlap:** "Point B & C", "T max", "plateau", "2μ" → maximal cross-bridges; resting length = optimal length.
>
> **No overlap:** "Point D", "over-stretched", "no tension" → actin pulled out of the A band; zero active tension.
>
> **Passive tension:** "Not stimulated", "titin", "elasticity" → the spring of the sarcomere; adds to active to give total tension.
>
> **Type I fibre:** "small & red", "slow", "aerobic", "posture", "recruited first", "size principle" → slow-twitch oxidative fibres (soleus, back muscles).
>
> **Type IIb fibre:** "large & white", "myoglobin absent", "glycolysis", "lactate", "fatigue prone", "running" → fast glycolytic fibres.
>
> **Complete tetanus:** "↑↑↑ frequency", "sustained contraction", "no relaxation", "Ca²⁺ accumulation" → fused tetanus.
>
> **Tetanus (disease):** "Clostridium tetani", "tetanospasmin", "spastic", "body bends like a bow" → opisthotonus from loss of inhibition.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Rippled rising trace = incomplete tetanus; smooth plateau = complete tetanus; trapezoid curve with a flat top = length–tension curve; dome + rising exponential = active vs passive tension.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Fine control: 3–6 fibres per motor unit (extraocular); coarse: ~600 (leg/back). Tetanising frequency = 1 / contraction period — shorter contraction period → higher frequency needed.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "More stretch always means more force"**
>
> **What students think:** Stretching a muscle always increases its active tension (they over-apply Frank–Starling).
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Active tension is maximal only on the B–C plateau; beyond C actin slides out of reach of myosin and active tension falls to zero at D. What keeps rising on stretch is **passive** (titin) tension, which uses no cross-bridges.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Ask "active or total?" — active is a dome, passive is a ramp, total is their sum.
>
> **Trap 2: Mixing up Type IIa and Type IIb**
>
> **What students think:** All Type II fibres are white and fatigue quickly.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Type IIa is **large and red** — fast, but rich in myoglobin and fatigue resistant, using aerobic or anaerobic metabolism; only Type IIb is white, myoglobin-absent, purely glycolytic and fatigue prone.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "a = aerobic-capable (red); b = burns out (white)."
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking big motor units are recruited first**
>
> **What students think:** The strongest units fire first to give force quickly.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The size principle recruits the **smallest** motor neurons (Type I, small diameter, easiest to excite) first, then IIa, then IIb last. This makes force increments small at low force and precise.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Small, slow, first — big, fast, last."
>
> **Trap 4: Tetanus the disease = tetanus the twitch summation**
>
> **What students think:** Tetanospasmin works by raising the stimulus frequency at the muscle.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The page uses it as an example of a sustained contracted state, but the toxin acts in the spinal cord: it blocks release of inhibitory transmitters (glycine, GABA) so α motor neurons fire unchecked; the muscle then receives high-frequency volleys and tetanises.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Physiological tetanus = high frequency at the muscle; clinical tetanus = loss of inhibition in the cord that produces that high frequency.
>
> **Trap 5: Fibrillation vs fasciculation**
>
> **What students think:** Both are visible twitches.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Fibrillation is a single fibre (needs EMG); fasciculation is a whole motor unit (visible). Their numbers on the page: fibrillation — single fibre, single unit; fasciculation — multiple fibres, multiple units.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Fibr-illation = fibre; Fascicul-ation = fascicle (a bundle)."

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. Contraction = **shortening (change in length)** + **force generation (change in tension)**.
> 2. Length–tension graph compares **length with active tension**: **D** no overlap → no tension; **B & C** optimal overlap → **maximum tension (T max)**; **A** not-optimal overlap → less tension.
> 3. **Active tension** — muscle stimulated — actin–myosin overlap → cross-bridges; **passive tension** — muscle not stimulated — **titin** (elasticity); **total = active + passive**.
> 4. Factors affecting active tension: **cross-bridge formation, fibre diameter, fibre length, AP frequency, number of motor units recruited**.
> 5. **Optimal / resting length** = maximum tension; **equilibrium length** = muscle cut from its bony attachments.
> 6. **Type I** small & red, high myoglobin, slow ATPase, aerobic, no lactate, fatigue resistant, **recruited first (size principle)**, **posture/sitting**.
> 7. **Type IIa** large & red, fast, aerobic or anaerobic, fatigue resistant, second, **standing**; **Type IIb** large & white, myoglobin absent, anaerobic glycolysis, lactate → pain, **fatigue prone**, last, **running**.
> 8. **Motor unit = single α motor neuron + the fibres it innervates**; extraocular **3–6 fibres** (fine), leg/back **600 fibres** (coarse).
> 9. Heat: **resting** (BMR) · **activation** (actin–myosin activation) · **shortening** (overlap) · **relaxation proper** (isotonic, no tension change) · **recovery** (regains length); ↑ resting heat in a **lean body** and **after exercise**.
> 10. Twitch: **latent period** (after stimulus, before contraction) → **contraction** → **relaxation**.
> 11. **Incomplete tetanus** = ↑ frequency, incomplete relaxation; **complete tetanus** = ↑↑↑ frequency, sustained contraction, **no relaxation, d/t Ca²⁺ accumulation**.
> 12. **Tetanospasmin** (*Clostridium tetani*) → spastic contracted state → **body bends like a bow**; **T_f = 1 / contraction period**.
> 13. **Fibrillation** = single fibre, single unit; **fasciculation** = multiple fibres, multiple units.

[^1]: Guyton gives full active tension at a sarcomere length of about 2.0–2.2 µm; below ~2 µm the thin filaments overlap each other and below ~1.65 µm the thick filaments butt into the Z discs (point A side); tension falls to zero at ~3.65 µm, when actin no longer overlaps myosin (point D). The page labels the plateau width "2μ" without stating whether it means sarcomere length; the page's value is kept.
[^2]: Passive tension comes mainly from titin and connective tissue (endomysium, perimysium). Because active tension cannot be measured directly, physiologists record total tension and subtract the passive curve.
[^3]: A muscle in the body is held under slight stretch by its attachments, at or close to its optimal length; cut free it recoils to its shorter equilibrium length, where it generates less tension.
[^4]: Modern work shows fatigue is caused less by lactate itself than by accumulation of H⁺, inorganic phosphate and extracellular K⁺; delayed muscle soreness is micro-injury, not lactate. In humans the "Type IIb" fibre is properly called **Type IIx** (true IIb occurs in rodents) — exams still use IIb.
[^5]: Guyton: an average motor unit has about 80–100 fibres; small, finely controlled muscles (laryngeal, extraocular) have very few fibres per unit, large postural muscles such as the soleus several hundred. The ratio fibres : motor neuron is the *innervation ratio*.
[^6]: The classification follows A. V. Hill's myothermic studies: resting heat; initial heat (activation/maintenance heat + shortening heat) during contraction; relaxation heat; and recovery heat — produced after the contraction by oxidative resynthesis of ATP and creatine phosphate, roughly equal to the initial heat. Textbooks describe relaxation heat as the work done on an isotonically contracting muscle being turned into heat when the load lowers it back to its original length; the page's wording is kept.
[^7]: Skeletal muscle is the largest metabolically active tissue at rest, so more lean mass → higher BMR → more resting heat. After exercise, oxygen consumption stays raised (excess post-exercise oxygen consumption, EPOC), which keeps heat production and energy expenditure up.
[^8]: The page reads "to peak concentration"; it means peak **contraction** (the top of the twitch curve). Ganong: twitch duration ranges from about 7.5 ms in fast fibres to about 100 ms in slow fibres; the skeletal muscle action potential itself lasts only 2–4 ms and is over before the contraction begins.
[^9]: Because the skeletal muscle action potential (and so its refractory period) is far shorter than the twitch, a second stimulus can arrive while the fibre is still contracting — mechanical summation is possible. In cardiac muscle the refractory period lasts as long as the contraction, so it cannot be tetanised ([[Cardiac and Smooth Muscles]]).
[^10]: Tetanospasmin is carried retrogradely to the spinal cord and cleaves synaptobrevin in inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw cells), blocking release of glycine and GABA; disinhibited α motor neurons fire continuously → trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus and opisthotonus.
[^11]: Worked example of the page's formula: a contraction period of 0.05 s gives T_f = 1 / 0.05 = 20 stimuli per second; a fast fibre with a contraction period of 0.01 s needs 100 per second. Guyton notes most muscles fuse at roughly 30–60 per second.
[^12]: Textbook definitions: **fibrillation** — spontaneous, repetitive contraction of single denervated muscle fibres, appearing a few weeks after a lower motor neuron lesion (denervation hypersensitivity) and seen only on EMG; **fasciculation** — spontaneous contraction of all the fibres of one motor unit, visible through the skin, typical of anterior horn cell disease (e.g. ALS). The page's row "No. of motor units: fibrillation — single (α motor neuron or its axons)" is kept as written.
[^ety-tension]: **tension** — Latin *tendere*, "to stretch" + *-sio(n)*, the state → the state of being stretched taut; in muscle, the pulling force exerted on the load. Same root as *tendon* and *hypertension*.
[^ety-contraction]: **contraction** — Latin *con-*, "together" + *trahere*, "to draw, pull" + *-ion*, the process → the process of drawing together. **Differentiate:** *isometric* contraction (tension rises, length constant) · *isotonic* contraction (length changes, tension constant) · *contracture* (a fixed, prolonged shortening without action potentials, e.g. rigor).
[^ety-sarcomere]: **sarcomere** — Greek *sarx, sarkos*, "flesh" + *meros*, "part" → a part of flesh; the repeating contractile unit between two Z lines. Same root in *sarcolemma* (flesh-husk) and *sarcoplasmic reticulum*.
[^ety-myosin]: **myosin** — Greek *mys, myos*, "muscle" + *-in*, a protein → "muscle protein", named by Willy Kühne (1864); the thick filament whose heads form cross-bridges.
[^ety-actin]: **actin** — Latin *actio*, "a doing, action" (from *agere*, "to drive") + *-in* → "the protein that acts"; named in 1942 by Brunó Straub's group because it *activated* myosin. The thin filament.
[^ety-stimulation]: **stimulation** — Latin *stimulus*, "a goad, a cattle prod" + *-are* + *-ation*, the process → the process of prodding an excitable tissue into action. **Differentiate:** *stimulation* (the act) · *stimulus* (the prod itself) · *excitation* (the tissue's response — an action potential).
[^ety-titin]: **titin** — named after the **Titans** of Greek myth (giants) + *-in*: the largest known protein (~3–4 MDa, ~27 000 amino acids), spanning half a sarcomere from Z line to M line and acting as a molecular spring.
[^ety-elasticity]: **elasticity** — Greek *elastos*, "ductile, beaten out" (from *elaunein*, "to drive, beat out") + *-ity*, the quality → the ability to return to the original shape after stretch. **Differentiate:** *elasticity* (springs back) · *plasticity* (stays stretched — smooth muscle) · *compliance* (the inverse of elasticity — how easily something is stretched).
[^ety-diameter]: **diameter** — Greek *dia-*, "through, across" + *metron*, "measure" → the measure across; thicker fibres hold more myofibrils, so more parallel cross-bridges.
[^ety-recruitment]: **recruitment** — French *recrue*, "new growth, a fresh supply" (from *recroître*, "to grow again": *re-* "again" + *croître* "to grow") → calling in new soldiers; in physiology, bringing additional motor units into action. **Differentiate:** *recruitment* (more units) · *rate coding / summation* (each unit fires faster).
[^ety-equilibrium]: **equilibrium** — Latin *aequus*, "equal" + *libra*, "balance, scales" → equal balance; the *equilibrium length* is where the muscle's own elastic forces balance with no external stretch.
[^ety-myoglobin]: **myoglobin** — Greek *mys*, "muscle" + Latin *globus*, "ball" + *-in* → the globular (O₂-binding) protein of muscle; one haem, binds O₂ more avidly than haemoglobin and gives red fibres their colour.
[^ety-atpase]: **ATPase** — *ATP* (adenosine triphosphate) + *-ase*, the enzyme ending → the enzyme that splits ATP; myosin's own ATPase speed sets how fast its cross-bridges cycle (fast vs slow fibres).
[^ety-metabolism]: **metabolism** — Greek *meta-*, "change" + *ballein*, "to throw" + *-ism*, a process/state → a "throwing into a changed form"; all chemical change in the body. **Differentiate:** *anabolism* (*ana-*, up — building) · *catabolism* (*kata-*, down — breaking down).
[^ety-aerobic]: **aerobic / anaerobic** — Greek *aēr*, "air" + *bios*, "life" + *-ic* → living with air (O₂); *an-*, "without" → without O₂. Aerobic = oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria (~30–32 ATP per glucose); anaerobic = glycolysis only (2 ATP + lactate).
[^ety-glycolysis]: **glycolysis** — Greek *glykys*, "sweet" (sugar) + *lysis*, "loosening, splitting" → the splitting of sugar; glucose → 2 pyruvate in the cytosol. **Differentiate:** *glycolysis* (glucose broken down) · *glycogenolysis* (glycogen broken down) · *gluconeogenesis* (*neo-* new + *genesis* — glucose made anew).
[^ety-lactate]: **lactate** — Latin *lac, lactis*, "milk" + *-ate*, salt of an acid → salt of lactic (milk) acid, first found in sour milk by Scheele (1780); the end product of anaerobic glycolysis.
[^ety-fatigue]: **fatigue** — Latin *fatigare*, "to tire out" (from *ad fatim*, "to bursting") → loss of force with continued activity. **Differentiate:** *muscle fatigue* (within the muscle — metabolites) · *central fatigue* (reduced drive from the CNS) · *synaptic fatigue* (transmitter depletion at the NMJ).
[^ety-innervation]: **innervation** — Latin *in-*, "into" + *nervus*, "sinew, nerve" + *-ation* → the supplying of a part with nerves. **Differentiate:** *innervation* (nerve supply) · *denervation* (*de-*, removal — nerve supply lost) · *reinnervation* (*re-*, again — regrown supply).
[^ety-henneman]: **size principle (Henneman's principle)** — Elwood Henneman, American neurophysiologist (1915–1996), who showed in 1957 that motor neurons are recruited in order of increasing size: small (Type I) units first, large (Type II) last.
[^ety-posture]: **posture** — Latin *positura*, "position, placing" (from *ponere*, "to place") → the position the body is held in against gravity — the job of the tireless Type I fibres.
[^ety-extraocular]: **extraocular** — Latin *extra-*, "outside" + *oculus*, "eye" + *-ar* → outside the eyeball; the six muscles that move the eye (four recti, two obliques), with the smallest motor units in the body.
[^ety-isotonic]: **isotonic** — Greek *isos*, "equal" + *tonos*, "tension, stretching" + *-ic* → equal tension; a contraction in which the muscle shortens while the tension stays constant. **Differentiate:** *isometric* (*metron*, measure — length constant, tension rises) · *isotonic* (tension constant, length changes) · *isotonic solution* (same effective osmolality as plasma — a different use of the same word).
[^ety-twitch]: **twitch** — Old English *twiccian*, "to pluck, pull sharply" → a single brief contraction in response to one action potential.
[^ety-latent]: **latent** — Latin *latere*, "to lie hidden" → the hidden interval when the stimulus has been given but no contraction is yet visible (excitation–contraction coupling is under way). Same root as *latent infection*.
[^ety-relaxation]: **relaxation** — Latin *re-*, "back" + *laxare*, "to loosen" + *-ation* → the process of loosening back; Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR and cross-bridges detach. **Differentiate:** *relaxation* (active, needs ATP) · *rigor* (no ATP → cross-bridges cannot detach).
[^ety-myogram]: **myogram** — Greek *mys*, "muscle" + *gramma*, "something written" → a written trace of muscle contraction. **Differentiate:** *myogram* (the trace) · *myograph* (the instrument) · *electromyogram, EMG* (the electrical trace).
[^ety-summation]: **summation** — Latin *summa*, "the total, the top" + *-ation* → the process of adding up; here twitches add because a new one starts before the last has ended. **Differentiate:** *multiple-fibre (spatial) summation* (more motor units) · *frequency (temporal / wave) summation* (faster firing of the same units).
[^ety-tetanus]: **tetanus** — Greek *tetanos*, "taut, stretched" (from *teinein*, "to stretch") → a sustained, fused contraction. **Differentiate:** *tetanus* (physiological fused contraction, or the disease caused by *C. tetani*) · *tetany* (spasms from low ionised Ca²⁺ — carpopedal spasm, Chvostek and Trousseau signs) · *tetanic* (the adjective).
[^ety-tetanospasmin]: **tetanospasmin** — Greek *tetanos*, "taut" + *spasmos*, "a convulsion, pulling" + *-in*, a toxin/protein → the spasm-making toxin of *Clostridium tetani*; a zinc protease that cleaves synaptobrevin.
[^ety-clostridium]: **Clostridium tetani** — Greek *klōstēr*, "spindle" + *-idium*, little → "little spindle" (the shape of the spore-bearing rod, drumstick appearance with a terminal spore); *tetani*, "of tetanus".
[^ety-opisthotonus]: **opisthotonus** — Greek *opisthen*, "behind, backwards" + *tonos*, "tension" → tension bending backwards; the arched-back posture of tetanus and strychnine poisoning. **Differentiate:** *emprosthotonus* (*emprosthen*, forwards) · *pleurothotonus* (*pleurothen*, sideways).
[^ety-fibrillation]: **fibrillation** — Latin *fibrilla*, "a little fibre" (diminutive of *fibra*) + *-ation* → the twitching of little fibres; individual fibres contract out of step. Same word in *atrial / ventricular fibrillation* — uncoordinated myocyte activity.
[^ety-fasciculation]: **fasciculation** — Latin *fasciculus*, "a little bundle" (diminutive of *fascis*, "bundle") + *-ation* → the twitching of a little bundle — all fibres of one motor unit. **Memory:** fibrillation = fibre; fasciculation = fascicle.
