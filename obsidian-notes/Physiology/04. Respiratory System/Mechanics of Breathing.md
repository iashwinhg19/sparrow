# 🎈 Mechanics of Breathing

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Mechanics**[^ety-mechanics] of breathing is the physics of moving air: the respiratory muscles change the **volume** of the chest, and by **Boyle's law** (P ∝ 1/V) the **pressures** in the pleura and alveoli change, so air flows from high to low pressure. Everything on these pages answers three questions:
> - ✅ **Which pressures exist and how do they change?** — intrapleural (always negative at rest), alveolar (zero at rest, − in inspiration, + in expiration) and transpulmonary (AP − IPP, keeps alveoli open).
> - ✅ **How easily does the lung stretch?** — **compliance** = ΔV/ΔP (200 mL/cm H₂O), opposed by **elastance**; it differs between inspiration and expiration (**hysteresis**), and rises in emphysema, falls in fibrosis.
> - ✅ **What does breathing cost?** — **work of breathing**: 65 % against elastic forces, 35 % against resistance.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — A Balloon in a Box Held by a Wet Film

The lung is a **balloon** that always wants to shrink (elastic recoil, surface tension); the chest wall is a **spring** that wants to open out. A thin **film of pleural fluid** sticks the two together like two wet glass slides, so the space between them is pulled into a **negative pressure**. Pull the floor down (diaphragm) and the box enlarges → pressure inside falls (Boyle) → air rushes in. Let go and the balloon recoils → air goes out without any muscle work.

```
                        REST (FRC)          INSPIRATION            QUIET EXPIRATION       FORCEFUL EXPIRATION
Intrapleural (IPP)      −2.5 mm Hg (neg.)   −6 (more negative)     back to rest value     POSITIVE
Alveolar (AP)           0                   −1 → air flows IN      +1 → air flows OUT     strongly positive
Transpulmonary AP−IPP   POSITIVE → keeps alveoli distended
Muscles                 —                   diaphragm (+ accessory) passive (recoil)      abdominals + internal intercostals

COMPLIANCE  C = ΔV/ΔP = 200 mL/cm H2O   ∝ 1/elastance
   emphysema ↑C (elastance lost)   ·   fibrosis/ILD ↓C (stiff lung)   ·   expiration limb > inspiration limb = HYSTERESIS
WORK = force × displacement = 0.5 kg·m/min → 65 % elastic (⅔ surface tension, ⅓ tissue) + 35 % resistance (28 % airway, 7 % tissue)
```

## 🫁 Pressures in the Lung

![[Mechanics of Breathing - pleura and lung pressures.png]]
*The lungs inside the chest wall, as on the page — **parietal pleura**, **visceral pleura**, **pleural cavity**, **chest wall**, **lung**; the three pressures labelled are the **transpleural pressure**, the **intrapleural pressure (IPP)** and the **alveolar pressure** (cropped from the handwritten page).*[^1]

- The **parietal**[^ety-parietal] **pleura**[^ety-pleura] lines the chest wall, the **visceral**[^ety-visceral] **pleura** covers the lung, and the **pleural cavity**[^ety-cavity] between them holds only a thin film of fluid.
- **Boyle's law**[^ety-boyle]**: Pressure ∝ 1 / Volume (tidal**[^ety-tidal] **volume).** When the chest enlarges, the pressure inside falls and air is sucked in; when it shrinks, pressure rises and air is pushed out.

![[Mechanics of Breathing - alveolar and intrapleural pressure during a breath.png]]
*One quiet breath (0–4 s), as on the page: **pressure in alveoli** falls to −1 mm Hg in **inspiration** (green ✗), is 0 at **rest** (yellow ○) and rises to +1 in **expiration** (blue ✗); **intrapleural pressure** falls from about −2.5 mm Hg at rest to −6 in inspiration; **volume of breath** rises to about 0.5 L (cropped from the handwritten page).*

### Types of pressure

**1. Intrapleural**[^ety-intrapleural][^pre-intra] **pressure (IPP):**

- **At rest: negative** (d/t **vacuum**[^ety-vacuum] created by **pleural stretch**).
- **Inspiration**[^ety-inspiration][^pre-in]**: more negative.**
- **Expiration**[^ety-expiration][^pre-ex]**: same as rest.**
- **Forceful expiration: positive.**[^2]

**2. Alveolar**[^ety-alveolus] **pressure (AP):**

- **At rest: zero.**
- **Inspiration: negative.**
- **Expiration: positive.**[^3]

**3. Transpulmonary**[^ety-transpulmonary][^pre-trans] **pressure = AP − IPP:**

- **At rest: positive.**
- **Keeps alveoli distended**[^ety-distension][^pre-dis].

| Pressure | At rest | Inspiration | Expiration | Forceful expiration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **IPP** | **Negative** (pleural stretch) | **More negative** | Same as rest | **Positive** |
| **AP** | **Zero** | **Negative** | **Positive** | — |
| **Transpulmonary (AP − IPP)** | **Positive** → keeps alveoli distended | — | — | — |

## 🪀 Lung compliance[^ety-compliance][^pre-com][^suf-ance]

**Lung compliance = change in volume ÷ change in pressure:**

```latex
C = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P} = 200\ \text{mL/cm H}_2\text{O (normal)} \qquad\qquad \text{Compliance} \propto \frac{1}{\text{Elastance}}
```

- **C = ΔV/ΔP = 200 ml/cm H₂O (normal).**[^5]
- **Compliance ∝ 1 / elastance**[^ety-elastance].

![[Mechanics of Breathing - opposing forces on the alveolus.png]]
*Opposing forces, as on the page — an alveolus at **rest** expands in **inspiration**; the **action** of the inspiratory muscles produces **distension (compliance)**, while two forces oppose it: **surface tension forces** and **lung elasticity → elastic recoil (elastance)** (cropped from the handwritten page).*

> [!info] Compliance vs elastance in plain words
> **Compliance** is how far the lung stretches for a given push; **elastance** is how hard it snaps back. They are reciprocals — a stiff (fibrotic) lung has high elastance and low compliance; a floppy (emphysematous) lung has low elastance and high compliance. The snap-back has two sources on the page: the **surface tension** of the fluid lining the alveoli and the **elastic tissue** of the lung itself.

### Hysteresis[^ety-hysteresis]

![[Mechanics of Breathing - hysteresis loop.png]]
*Hysteresis loop, as on the page — volume against pressure: for the same **ΔP** the **expiration** limb holds a larger **ΔV** than the **inspiration** limb; the area between them is the **hysteresis** (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Difference b/w inspiratory & expiratory compliance of lung.**
  - **Compliance greatest during expiration.**
- **Occurs d/t:**

**1. Surface tension forces — air filled vs saline**[^ety-saline] **filled lung:**

![[Mechanics of Breathing - saline-filled vs air-filled lung.png]]
*Pressure–volume loops of the two lungs, as on the page — the narrow, steep loop on the left is the **saline-filled lung** (no air–fluid interface: little hysteresis); the wide loop on the right is the **air-filled lung** (cropped from the handwritten page).*

- **Saline filled → no air fluid interface**[^ety-interface][^pre-inter].
- **Air filled lung → air fluid interface → ↑ surface tension → ↑ hysteresis / ↑ compliance.**[^6]

```
Saline filled lung → NO air–fluid interface → little hysteresis

Air filled lung
      ↓
Air–fluid interface
      ↓
↑ Surface tension
      ↓
↑ Hysteresis / ↑ compliance   (page wording — see footnote)
```

**2. Surfactant**[^ety-surfactant]**:**

![[Mechanics of Breathing - surfactant spacing in inspiration and expiration.png]]
*Surfactant molecules (S) on an alveolus — spread far apart on the large alveolus of **inspiration**, crowded close together on the small alveolus of **expiration** (cropped from the handwritten page).*

```
        INSPIRATION                           EXPIRATION
 Widely placed surfactant              Closely placed surfactant
            ↓                                     ↓
 Less reduction in surface tension     More reduction in surface tension
            ↓                                     ↓
      LESS COMPLIANT                        MORE COMPLIANT
```

- **Inspiration:** widely placed surfactant → **less reduction in surface tension → less compliant.**
- **Expiration:** closely placed surfactant → **more reduction in surface tension → more compliant.**

### Compliance curves

![[Mechanics of Breathing - compliance curves of lung, chest wall and total.png]]
*Compliance curves, as on the page — volume against airway force: **A, CCW** (chest wall, green) on the **expanding-force** side; **B, CL** (lung, yellow) on the **collapsing-force** side; **C, CT = CL + CCW** (black); where the expanding and collapsing forces cancel is the **FRC**; below the lung's **minimal volume** lies the **point of complete collapse** (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **CCW** | **Compliance of chest wall** |
| **CL** | **Compliance of lung** |
| **CT** | **Total compliance** (**CT = CL + CCW** on the page)[^7] |
| **FRC** | **Functional residual**[^ety-residual][^pre-re] **capacity**[^ety-capacity] — **point of equilibrium**[^ety-equilibrium][^pre-equi] **between expanding & collapsing forces** |

- **Applied aspect: minimum volume seen in pneumothorax**[^ety-pneumothorax][^pre-pneumo][^suf-thorax].[^8]

### Types of compliance

| **1. Static**[^ety-static] | **2. Dynamic**[^ety-dynamic] | **3. Specific** |
|---|---|---|
| **During breath holding** | **During normal breathing** — **d/t impedance**[^ety-impedance][^pre-im] (**resistance**[^ety-resistance] **to air flow**) | **= Compliance ÷ FRC**[^9] |

### Applied aspect

![[Mechanics of Breathing - compliance in emphysema and fibrosis.png]]
*Applied aspect, as on the page — lung volume (L) against transpulmonary pressure (cm H₂O): the **emphysema** curve lies above **normal** (↑ compliance, d/t loss of elastance; gross lung riddled with holes; chest X-ray showing **hyperinflation of lung**), the **fibrosis** curve lies flat below it (↓ compliance, d/t stiff lung; gross **pulmonary fibrosis / ILD**) (cropped from the handwritten page).*

| Condition | Compliance | Why (page) |
|---|---|---|
| **Emphysema**[^ety-emphysema][^pre-em] | **↑ compliance** | **D/t loss of elastance** → **hyperinflation**[^ety-hyperinflation][^pre-hyper] **of lung** on X-ray |
| **Normal** | 200 mL/cm H₂O | — |
| **Pulmonary fibrosis**[^ety-fibrosis][^suf-osis] **/ ILD**[^ety-ild] | **↓ compliance** | **D/t stiff lung** |

> [!tip] Why emphysema "over-complies" yet the patient cannot breathe out
> Emphysema destroys alveolar walls and their elastic fibres, so the lung stretches easily (↑ compliance) but has lost the elastic recoil that drives expiration and holds small airways open — air is trapped and the lung becomes **hyperinflated**. Fibrosis lays down collagen, so every breath needs a large pressure for a small volume (↓ compliance) and the lungs are small.[^10]

## 🏋️ Work of breathing

**Work = Force × displacement**[^ety-displacement] **= 0.5 kg/m/min (by lung).**

- **Force → pressure changes.**
- **Displacement → volume changes.**

So the work of breathing is the area of the pressure–volume loop: how much pressure the muscles must create, times how much volume they move.[^11]

```
                      WORK DONE (100 %)
                          overcome
            ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
  65 % work — ELASTIC FORCES        35 % work — RESISTANCE
     ├─ 2/3rd  surface tension forces      ├─ 28 %  airway resistance
     └─ 1/3rd  tissue elasticity           └─  7 %  lung tissue resistance
```

| Work done to overcome | Share | Split |
|---|---|---|
| **Elastic forces** | **65 %** | **2/3rd surface tension forces** · **1/3rd tissue elasticity**[^ety-elasticity] |
| **Resistance** | **35 %** | **28 % airway resistance** · **7 % lung tissue resistance** |

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> **Air moves only down a pressure gradient, and the lung only changes volume when the pressure across it changes.** The muscles enlarge the chest; Boyle's law turns that volume change into a fall of intrapleural and alveolar pressure; the positive transpulmonary pressure (AP − IPP) holds the alveoli open. How much volume each unit of pressure buys is **compliance** — set by tissue elasticity and, above all, by surface tension, which surfactant reduces more in expiration than in inspiration (hence hysteresis).
>
> The same balance explains disease: lose elastic recoil (emphysema) and compliance rises but expiration fails; stiffen the lung (fibrosis) and compliance falls so every breath costs more work; break the pleural seal (pneumothorax) and the lung recoils to its minimal volume.

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> At FRC the lung's inward recoil and the chest wall's outward spring are equal and opposite; IPP is about −2.5 mm Hg and AP is zero. A quiet breath lowers IPP to −6 and AP to −1, moves about 0.5 L, and costs 0.5 kg·m/min of work — 65 % elastic, 35 % resistive. Expiration is passive recoil.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> A stiffer lung (early fibrosis, pulmonary oedema) or narrower airways (asthma) raises the work of breathing. The patient compensates by changing pattern — rapid shallow breaths for stiff lungs (less elastic work), slow deep breaths for obstructed airways (less resistive work) — and recruits accessory muscles (scalenes, sternocleidomastoid) on exertion.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Emphysema: compliance rises, recoil is lost, airways collapse in forceful expiration (IPP positive), air is trapped → hyperinflated chest, flattened diaphragm. Fibrosis/ILD: compliance falls, lungs shrink, breathing becomes rapid and shallow. Pneumothorax: IPP becomes atmospheric, the lung collapses to minimal volume and the chest wall springs out. Neonatal RDS: no surfactant → very low compliance and huge work of breathing.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal recoil–spring balance → trigger (elastin loss / fibrosis / pleural breach / surfactant lack) → altered compliance → ↑ work of breathing → accessory muscle use and changed breathing pattern → respiratory muscle fatigue → respiratory failure
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Recognising which side of the balance has failed decides the treatment — drain the pleura in pneumothorax, give surfactant in RDS, bronchodilate and reduce hyperinflation in obstructive disease, and set ventilators with compliance in mind (low tidal volumes for stiff lungs).

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **Emphysema:** "↑ compliance", "loss of elastance / elastic recoil", "hyperinflated lungs, barrel chest, flat diaphragm" → destroyed alveolar walls; the P–V curve lies above and left of normal.
>
> **Pulmonary fibrosis / ILD:** "↓ compliance", "stiff lung", "rapid shallow breathing", "flat P–V curve" → collagen in the interstitium.
>
> **Pneumothorax:** "Lung collapsed to minimal volume", "IPP = atmospheric", "chest wall springs out" → loss of the pleural vacuum.
>
> **Hysteresis:** "Expiratory limb above inspiratory limb", "compliance greatest in expiration", "abolished in a saline-filled lung" → surface tension and surfactant.
>
> **Transpulmonary pressure:** "AP − IPP", "keeps alveoli distended" → the distending pressure of the lung.
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Chest X-ray with hyperinflation (more ribs visible, flat diaphragm, long narrow heart) = emphysema; small lungs with reticular shadows = fibrosis; a visible pleural line with no lung markings beyond = pneumothorax.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> Normal lung compliance 200 mL/cm H₂O; IPP −2.5 at rest, −6 in inspiration, positive only in forceful expiration; work of breathing 0.5 kg·m/min, of which 65 % is elastic.

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "Intrapleural pressure is positive in expiration"**
>
> **What students think:** Air goes out in expiration, so every pressure must turn positive.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** In **quiet** expiration only the **alveolar** pressure becomes positive (+1); IPP simply returns to its resting negative value. IPP turns **positive only in forceful expiration**, when the abdominal and internal intercostal muscles squeeze the chest.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Quiet out = AP positive, IPP still negative; forced out = both positive."
>
> **Trap 2: High compliance = healthy lung**
>
> **What students think:** More compliance is always better.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Emphysema has the **highest** compliance because elastance is lost — the lung inflates easily but cannot recoil, so expiration fails and air is trapped. Compliance and elastance are reciprocals; both extremes are disease.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Emphysema — easy in, hard out; fibrosis — hard in."
>
> **Trap 3: Adding compliances directly**
>
> **What students think:** CT = CL + CCW, so total compliance = 200 + 200 = 400 mL/cm H₂O.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The lung and chest wall are inflated together, **in series**; their *elastances* add, so 1/CT = 1/CL + 1/CCW and the total compliance (~100 mL/cm H₂O) is **less** than either. The page's "CT = CL + CCW" describes how the total curve is built from the two, not arithmetic addition.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Compliances in series add like resistors in parallel — the total is always smaller than the smallest.
>
> **Trap 4: Thinking surface tension is a minor force**
>
> **What students think:** Lung recoil comes mainly from elastic fibres.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** About **two-thirds** of the elastic work is against **surface tension** (the page's 2/3rd vs 1/3rd tissue elasticity) — proved by the saline-filled lung, which inflates far more easily and shows almost no hysteresis because the air–fluid interface is gone.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Remember the split "65 % elastic → ⅔ surface tension, ⅓ tissue".
>
> **Trap 5: Mixing up the muscles of forced expiration**
>
> **What students think:** External intercostals help forced expiration.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** External intercostals (with the scalenes and sternocleidomastoid) **raise** the ribs — inspiration. Forced expiration uses the **internal** intercostals, rectus abdominis and transverse abdominis. Quiet expiration uses no muscle at all.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "**E**xternal = **E**nter (air in); **I**nternal = **I**s out."

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Boyle's law: P ∝ 1/V** — the chest enlarges → pressure falls → air flows in.
> 2. **IPP**: rest **negative** (vacuum from pleural stretch) → inspiration **more negative** → expiration **same as rest** → forceful expiration **positive**.
> 3. **Alveolar pressure**: rest **zero**, inspiration **negative**, expiration **positive**.
> 4. **Transpulmonary pressure = AP − IPP** — **positive at rest**, **keeps alveoli distended**.
> 5. **1 mm Hg = 1.36 cm H₂O.**
> 6. Inspiration: **primary = diaphragm**; accessory = **external intercostal, scalene, sternocleidomastoid**. Expiration: **normal passive**; forceful **active** — **rectus abdominis, transverse abdominis, internal intercostal**.
> 7. **Compliance = ΔV/ΔP = 200 mL/cm H₂O**; **compliance ∝ 1/elastance**; opposing forces = **surface tension + lung elasticity (elastic recoil)**.
> 8. **Hysteresis** = difference between inspiratory and expiratory compliance; **compliance greatest in expiration**; due to **surface tension** (air vs saline-filled lung) and **surfactant** (closely placed in expiration → more compliant).
> 9. **FRC = point of equilibrium between expanding (chest wall) and collapsing (lung) forces**; **minimum volume seen in pneumothorax**.
> 10. Compliance types: **static** (breath holding), **dynamic** (normal breathing, includes impedance), **specific = C/FRC**.
> 11. **Emphysema ↑ compliance** (loss of elastance, hyperinflation); **fibrosis/ILD ↓ compliance** (stiff lung).
> 12. **Work = force × displacement = 0.5 kg·m/min**: **65 % elastic** (⅔ surface tension, ⅓ tissue elasticity) + **35 % resistance** (28 % airway, 7 % lung tissue).

[^1]: The figure's label "transpleural pressure" marks the pressure across the pleural layers. Standard texts name two transmural pressures: **transpulmonary** (alveolar − pleural, across the lung — item 3 on the page) and **trans-chest-wall** (pleural − atmospheric); their sum is the **transrespiratory** pressure (alveolar − atmospheric).
[^2]: Guyton quotes pleural pressure in cm H₂O: about **−5 cm H₂O** at the start of inspiration falling to **−7.5 cm H₂O**; the page's graph uses mm Hg (−2.5 → −6). IPP is negative because the lung's inward recoil and the chest wall's outward spring pull the two pleural layers apart against the incompressible fluid film. It is more negative at the apex than at the base (gravity). In forceful expiration the positive IPP compresses the airways — dynamic airway compression, which limits expiratory flow (see [[Lung Volumes and Capacities]]).
[^3]: In Guyton, alveolar pressure falls to about −1 cm H₂O in quiet inspiration — just enough to pull 0.5 L into the lungs in 2 seconds — and rises to about +1 cm H₂O in expiration.
[^4]: The diaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–C5) produces most of the volume change of quiet inspiration (about two-thirds to three-quarters). Many textbooks count the **external intercostals** with the diaphragm as primary muscles of inspiration; the page lists them as accessory, which is kept in the body. In heavy breathing the diaphragm descends up to ~10 cm.
[^5]: Guyton: compliance of the **lungs alone** ≈ 200 mL/cm H₂O; of **lungs + thorax together** ≈ 110 mL/cm H₂O. Elastance is the reciprocal (cm H₂O per litre).
[^6]: The page writes "↑ hysteresis / ↑ compliance" for the air-filled lung. Standard texts (von Neergaard, 1929; Guyton; West) show that the **saline-filled** lung inflates at a far lower pressure — i.e. it is **more compliant** — and has almost no hysteresis; the air-filled lung therefore has **↑ hysteresis and ↓ compliance**, because surface tension accounts for about two-thirds of the lung's elastic recoil. The page's own figure agrees (narrow steep saline loop, wide flatter air loop).
[^7]: Lung and chest wall are inflated together, **in series**, so their *elastances* add: **1/CT = 1/CL + 1/CCW**. With CL ≈ CCW ≈ 200 mL/cm H₂O, CT ≈ 100 mL/cm H₂O — less than either. The page's "CT = CL + CCW" is kept in the body; read it as "the total curve is built from the lung and chest-wall curves". At FRC the lung's recoil inward equals the chest wall's spring outward (relaxation volume of the respiratory system).
[^8]: When air enters the pleural space, IPP becomes atmospheric, so the transpulmonary pressure falls to zero: the lung recoils to its **minimal volume** (some air stays trapped behind closed small airways) while the chest wall springs outward. The "point of complete collapse" lies below minimal volume and is reached only if all trapped air is absorbed.
[^9]: Dynamic compliance is measured during breathing at the moments of zero flow (end-inspiration and end-expiration) and falls with rising breathing frequency when small airways are obstructed ("frequency dependence of compliance"). Specific compliance corrects for lung size, so a child's or a single lung's compliance can be compared with an adult's (normal ≈ 0.05–0.06 per cm H₂O).
[^10]: In emphysema the destruction of elastic fibres removes the radial traction that holds small airways open, so they collapse early in expiration — air trapping, ↑ RV, ↑ FRC, ↑ TLC. Hyperinflation on X-ray: low flat diaphragms, more than about 6 anterior ribs above the diaphragm, a long narrow heart and ↑ retrosternal air space.
[^11]: Guyton gives the same split: about **65 %** of inspiratory work is compliance (elastic) work, **7 %** tissue-resistance work and **28 %** airway-resistance work. In quiet breathing this costs only about 3–5 % of the body's total energy use; it rises steeply in exercise and in lung disease. Stiff lungs make elastic work dominate (rapid shallow breathing is cheaper); narrowed airways make resistive work dominate (slow deep breathing is cheaper).
[^ety-mechanics]: **mechanics** — Greek *mēchanē*, "machine, contrivance" + *-ikos* → the science of forces and motion; respiratory mechanics treats the chest as a pump moved by muscles.
[^ety-parietal]: **parietal** — Latin *paries, parietis*, "wall" + *-al* → belonging to the wall (of a cavity). **Differentiate:** *parietal* layer (lines the cavity wall) vs *visceral* layer (covers the organ) — the same pair in pleura, pericardium and peritoneum.
[^ety-pleura]: **pleura** — Greek *pleura*, "side, rib" → the membrane of the side of the chest. Same root as *pleurisy* (inflammation of the pleura) and *pleural effusion*.
[^ety-visceral]: **visceral** — Latin *viscus* (plural *viscera*), "internal organ" + *-al* → covering the organ itself; the visceral pleura is fused to the lung surface.
[^ety-cavity]: **cavity** — Latin *cavus*, "hollow" + *-ity*, the state → a hollow space; the pleural cavity is a *potential* space holding only ~10–20 mL of fluid.
[^ety-boyle]: **Boyle's law** — Robert Boyle (1627–1691), Anglo-Irish natural philosopher, who published in 1662 that at constant temperature the pressure of a gas varies inversely with its volume (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂).
[^ety-tidal]: **tidal** — Old English *tīd*, "time, season" → the regular rise and fall of the sea; *tidal volume* is the air that ebbs and flows with each quiet breath (~500 mL).
[^ety-intrapleural]: **intrapleural** — Latin *intra-*, "within" + Greek *pleura*, "side" + *-al* → within the pleural space. **Differentiate:** *intrapleural* = *pleural* pressure (in the space) vs *intrapulmonary* = alveolar pressure (in the lung) vs *transpulmonary* (across the lung).
[^ety-vacuum]: **vacuum** — Latin *vacuus*, "empty" → a space at sub-atmospheric pressure; the pleural "vacuum" is really a negative pressure created by the lung pulling in and the chest wall pulling out.
[^ety-inspiration]: **inspiration** — Latin *in-*, "into" + *spirare*, "to breathe" + *-ation*, the process → breathing in. **Differentiate:** *inspiration* (air in) · *expiration* (air out) · *respiration* (the whole process, *re-* again) · *ventilation* (bulk air movement).
[^ety-expiration]: **expiration** — Latin *ex-*, "out" + *spirare*, "to breathe" + *-ation* → breathing out; also "breathing one's last" (to *expire*).
[^ety-alveolus]: **alveolus** — Latin, "small hollow, little trough" (diminutive of *alveus*) → the tiny air sac where gas exchange occurs; plural *alveoli*.
[^ety-transpulmonary]: **transpulmonary** — Latin *trans-*, "across" + *pulmo*, "lung" + *-ary*, "pertaining to" → the pressure across the lung (alveolar − pleural), the distending pressure that keeps alveoli open.
[^ety-distension]: **distension** — Latin *dis-*, "apart" + *tendere*, "to stretch" + *-ion*, the process → stretching apart; the alveolus is *distended* by transpulmonary pressure. **Differentiate:** *distension* (stretched by pressure from inside) vs *dilatation* (widened) vs *expansion* (enlarged).
[^ety-diaphragm]: **diaphragm** — Greek *dia-*, "across" + *phragma*, "fence, partition" → the muscular partition across the trunk; the primary muscle of inspiration.
[^ety-passive]: **passive / active** — Latin *pati*, "to suffer, to undergo" → *passive* = happening to it without effort (elastic recoil); *agere*, "to do" → *active* = done by muscle contraction.
[^ety-accessory]: **accessory** — Latin *ad-* (*ac-*), "to" + *cedere*, "to go" → *accessorius*, "added, supplementary" → muscles that join in only when breathing is increased.
[^ety-intercostal]: **intercostal** — Latin *inter-*, "between" + *costa*, "rib" + *-al* → between the ribs. **Differentiate:** *external* intercostals (fibres down and forward — raise ribs, inspiration) vs *internal* intercostals (down and back — lower ribs, forced expiration).
[^ety-rectus-abdominis]: **rectus abdominis / transversus abdominis** — Latin *rectus*, "straight" + *abdomen* (origin uncertain), genitive *abdominis*, "of the abdomen" → the straight muscle of the abdomen; *transversus*, "turned across" → the transverse one. Both push the diaphragm up in forced expiration.
[^ety-scalene]: **scalene** — Greek *skalēnos*, "uneven, unequal-sided" (as in a scalene triangle) → the three neck muscles (anterior, middle, posterior) that lift the first two ribs.
[^ety-sternocleidomastoid]: **sternocleidomastoid** — Greek *sternon*, "chest, breastbone" + *kleis*, "key, clavicle" + *mastos*, "breast" + *-oeidēs*, "like" (the breast-shaped mastoid process) → the muscle running from sternum and clavicle to the mastoid process; with the head fixed it lifts the sternum.
[^ety-compliance]: **compliance** — Latin *complere*, "to fill up, complete" (via Italian *compire*, to fulfil) + *-ance* → yielding, giving way: how readily the lung yields (volume) to a pressure. **Differentiate:** *compliance* (ΔV/ΔP) · *elastance* (ΔP/ΔV, the recoil) · *resistance* (ΔP/flow).
[^ety-elastance]: **elastance** — Greek *elastos* (*elaunein*, "to drive, to push") + *-ance* → the tendency to spring back after being pushed; the reciprocal of compliance.
[^ety-hysteresis]: **hysteresis** — Greek *hysterēsis*, "a lagging behind, a shortfall" (*hysteros*, "later") → the lung's volume lags behind the pressure, so inflation and deflation follow different paths; term coined by James Ewing (1881) for magnetism. Not related to *hystero-*, uterus (Greek *hystera*).
[^ety-saline]: **saline** — Latin *sal*, "salt" + *-ine* → salt solution; filling a lung with saline removes the air–liquid interface and therefore surface tension.
[^ety-interface]: **interface** — Latin *inter-*, "between" + *facies*, "face, surface" → the boundary surface between two phases (air and the liquid lining the alveolus) where surface tension acts.
[^ety-surfactant]: **surfactant** — a 1950 coinage from **surf**ace **act**ive **a**ge**nt** → a substance acting at the air–liquid interface to lower surface tension; in the lung, DPPC from type II pneumocytes.
[^ety-residual]: **residual** — Latin *residuus*, "left over" (*re-*, "back" + *sedere*, "to sit") → what remains behind; *functional residual capacity* = the air left in the lungs at the end of a quiet expiration.
[^ety-capacity]: **capacity** — Latin *capax*, "able to hold" (*capere*, to take) + *-ity* → the amount a container holds. **Differentiate:** a lung *volume* is one undivided compartment (TV, RV); a *capacity* is the sum of two or more volumes (FRC = ERV + RV).
[^ety-equilibrium]: **equilibrium** — Latin *aequus*, "equal" + *libra*, "balance, scales" → equal balance: at FRC the lung's collapsing force and the chest wall's expanding force are equal.
[^ety-pneumothorax]: **pneumothorax** — Greek *pneuma*, "air, breath" + *thōrax*, "breastplate, chest" → air in the chest (pleural space). **Differentiate:** *pneumothorax* (air) · *haemothorax* (blood) · *hydrothorax* (fluid) · *pyothorax / empyema* (pus) · *chylothorax* (lymph).
[^ety-static]: **static** — Greek *statikos*, "causing to stand" (*histanai*, to stand) → measured standing still (no airflow, breath held), so only elastic forces are measured.
[^ety-dynamic]: **dynamic** — Greek *dynamis*, "power, force" → measured in motion, during breathing, so airflow resistance (impedance) is included.
[^ety-impedance]: **impedance** — Latin *impedire*, "to shackle the feet" (*in-* + *pes, pedis*, foot) + *-ance* → total hindrance to flow in a moving system (resistance + elastic and inertial components).
[^ety-resistance]: **resistance** — Latin *re-*, "against" + *sistere*, "to stand" + *-ance* → standing against flow; airway resistance = driving pressure ÷ airflow.
[^ety-emphysema]: **emphysema** — Greek *en-* (*em-* before *ph*), "in" + *physan*, "to blow, inflate" + *-ma*, the result → "an inflation": air spaces enlarged by destroyed walls. **Differentiate:** *pulmonary* emphysema (air spaces) vs *surgical / subcutaneous* emphysema (air in the tissues).
[^ety-hyperinflation]: **hyperinflation** — Greek *hyper-*, "over, excessive" + Latin *inflare*, "to blow into" (*in-* + *flare*, blow) + *-ation* → over-inflated lungs from trapped air.
[^ety-fibrosis]: **fibrosis** — Latin *fibra*, "fibre" + Greek *-osis*, a condition → replacement of normal tissue by fibrous (collagen) scar, making the lung stiff. **Differentiate:** *fibrosis* (scarring) vs *sclerosis* (hardening) vs *cirrhosis* (nodular fibrosis of the liver).
[^ety-ild]: **interstitial lung disease (ILD)** — Latin *inter-*, "between" + *sistere*, "to stand" → *interstitium*, the tissue that "stands between" the alveoli and capillaries; ILD inflames and scars this layer.
[^ety-displacement]: **displacement** — Old French *des-* (Latin *dis-*), "apart" + *placer*, "to place" + *-ment* → moving something from its place; in breathing, the volume moved.
[^ety-elasticity]: **elasticity** — Greek *elastos*, "ductile, driven" + *-icity*, the quality → the ability to return to shape after stretch; lung tissue elasticity comes from elastin and collagen fibres.
