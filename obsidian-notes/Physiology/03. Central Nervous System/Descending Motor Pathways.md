# 🏃 Descending Motor Pathways

> [!abstract] Core Definition
> **Descending**[^ety-descending][^pre-de] **motor pathways** carry commands **brain → spinal cord → skeletal muscle**: the **efferent**[^ety-efferent][^pre-ex] / **descending pathway** works as a **top-down approach**. Three questions run through the chapter:
> - ✅ **Who gives the order?** — association areas, premotor and supplementary (area 6), primary motor cortex (area 4) = **upper motor neurons**.
> - ✅ **Which road does it take?** — the **pyramidal (corticospinal)** tract for fine skilled movements vs the **extrapyramidal** brainstem tracts for posture.
> - ✅ **What happens when a level is cut?** — **decerebrate, decorticate, decerebellate rigidity** and **UMN vs LMN lesions**.

---

## 🧠 Mental Model — Generals, a Direct Hotline and the Sergeants

Think of an army. The **cortex** is the general staff: association areas have the *idea*, premotor and supplementary areas *plan*, the primary motor cortex (area 4) gives the *order*. The **corticospinal (pyramidal) tract** is a direct hotline from the generals to the soldiers who handle fine work (distal flexors — writing). The **brainstem tracts** are sergeants who keep everyone standing (posture, antigravity extensors). The **lower motor neuron** is the soldier — the **final common pathway**. Normally the generals keep the sergeants in check; remove the cortex (or cut below it) and the sergeants run unchecked — **rigidity**.

```
IDEA (cortical association areas) → PLAN (premotor 6: postural set-up, mirror neurons · supplementary 6: bimanual) → EXECUTE (primary 4, precentral gyrus)
        ↓ UMN
PYRAMIDAL: Betz cells → internal capsule (posterior limb) → midbrain → pons → PYRAMID → decussation
           → lateral CST 80–90 % (opposite side) · anterior CST 10 % (same side)
EXTRAPYRAMIDAL: lateral = rubrospinal (flexors of upper limb) · medial = vestibulo-, reticulo-, tectospinal (posture, extensors, head & eyes)
        ↓
LMN = FINAL COMMON PATHWAY: α → extrafusal fibres (contraction) · γ → intrafusal fibres / spindle (tone)
LESIONS: decerebrate (between colliculi → γ, extensor) · decorticate (rubrospinal intact → flexor UL) · decerebellate (α, extensor) · UMN vs LMN
```

## ⚠️ Disorders

### Extensor rigidity vs flexor rigidity

| | **Extensor rigidity** — **Decerebrate**[^ety-decerebrate][^suf-ate] **rigidity**[^ety-rigidity] | **Flexor rigidity** — **Decorticate**[^ety-decorticate] **rigidity** |
|---|---|---|
| **Pathophysiology**[^ety-pathophysiology] | **Transection**[^ety-transection][^pre-trans] **b/w superior colliculus & inferior colliculus** → cortical inhibition lost → **dorsal sensory roots: activated** → (++) **reticulospinal tract** (brainstem pathway) → (++) **γ-motor neurons** → (++) **↑ extensor muscle tone** → **Decerebrate rigidity / γ rigidity** | **Decortication (cortex removed)** → **cortical inhibition lost** + **rubrospinal tract: intact** → **↑ flexor muscle tone** → **decorticate rigidity** |
| **Posture** | **Head and neck extended** · **upper limb extended** · **finger & forearm pronated**[^ety-pronation] · **lower limbs: extended** · **toes: pointing inward** | **Upper limbs flexed against chest** · **head: extended** · lower limbs extended, toes pointing inward (the page's arrows point to both figures) |
| **Cause** | **Uncal herniation**[^ety-uncus][^ety-herniation] **(d/t space occupying lesion) → decerebrate rigidity** | **Thrombosis**[^ety-thrombosis][^suf-osis]**/hemorrhage**[^ety-hemorrhage][^suf-rrhage] **of internal capsule → decorticate rigidity** |

**Decerebrate rigidity — the chain:**

```
Transection b/w superior colliculus & inferior colliculus
     ↓
Cortical inhibition lost
     ↓
Dorsal sensory roots : activated
     ↓ ++
Reticulospinal tract  (brainstem pathway)
     ↓ ++
γ-motor neurons
     ↓ ++
↑ extensor muscle tone
     ↓
Decerebrate rigidity / γ rigidity
```

**Decorticate rigidity — the chain:**

```
Decortication (cortex removed)
     ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
Cortical inhibition lost     Rubrospinal tract : intact
                                   ↓
                          ↑ flexor muscle tone
                                   ↓
                          Decorticate rigidity
```

> [!tip] Why one posture is flexor and the other extensor
> The **red nucleus** (rubrospinal tract, flexors of the upper limb) lies in the **midbrain above the colliculi**. A lesion **above** it (decorticate) leaves the rubrospinal tract working → the arms flex. A transection **below** it (decerebrate, between the colliculi) removes the rubrospinal drive and releases the pontine reticulospinal and vestibulospinal tracts → every limb extends.[^7]

## Core Dictum & Pattern Recognition

> [!check]+ **🎯 Core Dictum - The Unifying Principle**
>
> Movement is commanded **top-down** through a hierarchy: association areas generate the idea, premotor and supplementary areas (area 6) plan it, the primary motor cortex (area 4) executes it through the **corticospinal tract**, and the **lower motor neuron** is the **final common pathway**. Two parallel systems reach the LMN: the **lateral system** (corticospinal + rubrospinal — new, fine skilled movement, flexors, distal muscles) and the **medial system** (vestibulo-, reticulo-, tectospinal — old, posture, antigravity extensors, neck, head and eyes).
>
> The cortex normally **inhibits** the brainstem extensor drive. Every rigidity in this chapter is a **release phenomenon**: cut below the red nucleus → extensors win (decerebrate, γ rigidity); cut above it → the rubrospinal tract still flexes the arms (decorticate); remove the anterior cerebellum → α motor neurons are released (decerebellate). UMN lesions release the stretch reflex (↑ tone, ↑ reflexes, Babinski +); LMN lesions destroy the final common pathway (atrophy, fasciculations, ↓ tone, ↓ reflexes).

> [!example]+ **📊 Clinical Pattern Recognition**
>
> Understanding how the condition progresses through stages helps you recognize it at different phases and predict outcomes.
>
> **Stage 1 - Normal Physiology:**
> The idea arises in association cortex, is planned in areas 6 (postural set-up, bimanual coordination, mirror neurons) and executed by area 4; 80–90 % of CST fibres cross at the pyramids to the opposite lateral column; the brainstem keeps posture against gravity; α motor neurons contract extrafusal fibres and γ motor neurons set spindle sensitivity and tone. Plantar response is flexor.
>
> **Stage 2 - Compensated/Early Pathology:**
> An early UMN lesion (stroke in the internal capsule, cord compression) first gives weakness of fine distal movements (writing, buttons) with a positive Babinski; in the first hours to days tone and reflexes can be low (spinal/cerebral shock) before hypertonia and hyperreflexia appear. An early LMN lesion shows fasciculations and segmental weakness before visible wasting.
>
> **Stage 3 - Decompensated/Overt Disease:**
> Established UMN lesion: spasticity, ↑↑ reflexes, regional (pyramidal-pattern) weakness, Babinski +. Raised intracranial pressure → uncal herniation compresses the midbrain → decerebrate (extensor) posturing; a large hemispheric/capsular lesion → decorticate (flexor) posturing. LMN disease → marked atrophy, fasciculations, flaccidity, areflexia.
>
> **Progression Timeline:**
> Normal → lesion (infarct, haemorrhage, tumour, herniation, motor neuron disease) → shock phase (flaccid) → release phase (spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski) → posturing as the lesion descends (decorticate → decerebrate) → brainstem death
>
> **Why This Matters Clinically:**
> Distinguishing UMN from LMN localises the lesion to brain/cord vs anterior horn, root or nerve. A change from decorticate to decerebrate posturing (GCS motor 3 → 2) means the lesion is moving down into the midbrain — a herniation emergency.

> [!warning]+ **🔍 Diagnostic Buzzwords & Pattern Recognition**
>
> These are the key terms, phrases, and clinical patterns that should immediately trigger specific diagnoses in your mind. When you see these in a clinical vignette or real patient, you should think of the associated condition instantly.
>
> **Buzzwords by Condition:**
>
> **UMN lesion:** "Spasticity", "↑↑ reflexes", "Babinski positive", "no wasting, no fasciculations", "regional weakness" → lesion of corticospinal pathway in cortex, internal capsule, brainstem or cord.
>
> **LMN lesion:** "Wasting", "fasciculations", "flaccid", "↓↓ / sluggish reflexes", "segmental weakness" → anterior horn cell, root or peripheral nerve.
>
> **Decerebrate rigidity:** "Arms extended and pronated, legs extended, toes pointing in", "uncal herniation", "intercollicular transection", "γ rigidity" → midbrain/upper pons lesion below the red nucleus.
>
> **Decorticate rigidity:** "Arms flexed on the chest, legs extended", "internal capsule haemorrhage/thrombosis", "rubrospinal intact" → lesion above the red nucleus.
>
> **Decerebellate rigidity:** "Anterior cerebellum removed", "α rigidity", "exaggerated extensor activity" → loss of cerebellar inhibition of α motor neurons.
>
> **Mirror neurons / bimanual:** "Imitation" → premotor cortex (area 6); "using two hands together" → supplementary motor area (area 6).
>
> **Visual/Imaging Buzzwords:**
> Big toe going up on stroking the sole = Babinski +; huge fingers, thumb, lips and tongue on the homunculus = representation by use (writing, vocalization); X-shaped crossing at the lower medulla = pyramidal decussation.
>
> **Severity Indicators:**
> CST origin 30 / 30 / 40 %; lateral CST 80–90 % crossed, anterior CST 10 % uncrossed; area 4 (primary), area 6 (premotor and supplementary), areas 3, 1, 2 (sensory); decerebrate (extensor) posturing carries a worse prognosis than decorticate (flexor).

> [!danger]+ **⚠️ Common Traps & Pitfalls**
>
> These are the mistakes that students and clinicians commonly make with this topic. Being aware of these traps helps you avoid diagnostic errors and answer tricky exam questions correctly.
>
> **Trap 1: "The rubrospinal tract is a medial pathway"**
>
> **What students think:** All extrapyramidal tracts are medial postural pathways.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The rubrospinal tract is the **only extrapyramidal tract that is lateral**: it runs with the lateral CST and, like it, controls flexors of the upper limb. Medial = vestibulo-, reticulo-, tectospinal.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Lateral = Corticospinal + Rubrospinal (CR — 'cry' with flexed arms); medial = VRT (posture)."
>
> **Trap 2: Swapping decerebrate and decorticate**
>
> **What students think:** Decerebrate means flexion because "the brain is gone".
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Decerebrate = transection **between the colliculi**, below the red nucleus, so the rubrospinal flexor drive is lost and the extensor reticulo/vestibulospinal drive is released → extension of all four limbs. Decorticate = cortex gone but red nucleus intact → arms flex onto the chest ("to the core" — de**cor**ticate).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "DeCORticate → hands to the CORe (chest)"; "decEREBRATE → Extended Everything".
>
> **Trap 3: Thinking γ rigidity and α rigidity are the same**
>
> **What students think:** All rigidity comes from the same motor neurons.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** Decerebrate rigidity is **γ rigidity**: it depends on the spindle loop, which is why the page has "dorsal sensory roots: activated" in the chain — cutting the dorsal roots abolishes it. Decerebellate rigidity is **α rigidity**: the α motor neurons are driven directly and dorsal root section does not abolish it.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "γ needs the loop (dorsal roots); α does not."
>
> **Trap 4: Calling the anterior CST permanently uncrossed**
>
> **What students think:** The 10 % anterior CST controls the same side of the body for good.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** The anterior CST runs on the same side in the cord but most of its fibres cross in the anterior white commissure at the segment where they end, mainly to axial and proximal muscles of both sides — so a unilateral cortical lesion still gives contralateral weakness.
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** "Lateral crosses at the pyramids, anterior crosses at its own segment."
>
> **Trap 5: Using muscle size or wasting to call a lesion UMN**
>
> **What students think:** Any weak, thin limb is an LMN lesion; any stiff limb is UMN.
>
> **Why this is wrong:** UMN lesions cause little or no atrophy (only late disuse), no fasciculations, ↑ tone and ↑ reflexes with a positive Babinski and regional weakness; LMN lesions give marked atrophy, fasciculations, ↓ tone and ↓ reflexes with segmental weakness. Rigidity from extrapyramidal (basal ganglia) disease is different again (lead-pipe, not velocity-dependent).
>
> **How to avoid this trap:** Go through the whole table (atrophy, fasciculations, tone, weakness distribution, reflexes, Babinski) before deciding.

> [!tip]+ **🔗 Related Concepts & Cross-Topic Connections**
>
> Understanding how this topic connects to other areas of medicine deepens your knowledge and helps you see the bigger picture. These connections also help you answer integrated questions on exams.
>
> **Related Topic 1: [[Spinal Reflexes]]**
> Muscle spindles, γ motor neurons and the stretch reflex explain tone, spasticity, hyperreflexia and why decerebrate (γ) rigidity disappears after dorsal root section.
>
> **Related Topic 2: [[Cerebellum and Basal Ganglia]]**
> The cerebellum's anterior lobe inhibits extensor α motor neurons (decerebellate rigidity when removed); basal ganglia lesions give a different, lead-pipe or cogwheel rigidity.
>
> **Related Topic 3: [[Somatosensory Pathways]]**
> The sensory cortex (areas 3, 1, 2) sits just behind the motor cortex (area 4) and supplies 40 % of corticospinal fibres; Brown-Séquard syndrome combines an ipsilateral corticospinal lesion with sensory loss.
>
> **Related Topic 4: [[Skeletal Muscles]]**
> Actin and myosin of the extrafusal fibres are the final effector; the LMN and its muscle fibres form the motor unit.
>
> **Related Topic 5: [[CSF, CBF and BBB]]**
> Raised intracranial pressure from a space-occupying lesion is what drives the uncus through the tentorial notch to cause decerebrate posturing.
>
> **Clinical Integration:**
> The UMN/LMN table is the first step of every neurological examination; posturing is scored in the GCS (decorticate = M3, decerebrate = M2) and signals herniation; capsular strokes give contralateral UMN hemiplegia with Babinski +.

## 🏆 Pearls (High-Yield Summary)

> [!success] Must-Know Pearls
> 1. **Brain → spinal cord → skeletal muscle**; efferent/descending = **top-down**; cortex and brainstem neurons = **UMNs**.
> 2. **Primary motor = area 4 (execution)**; **supplementary = area 6 (bimanual coordination)**; **premotor = area 6 (postural set-up, mirror neurons — imitation)**; motor cortex = **precentral gyrus**; **association areas = idea generation and initiation**.
> 3. Motor (Penfield) homunculus: **area ∝ use, not size** — **fingers, thumb (writing)** and **lips, tongue (vocalization)** have maximum representation.
> 4. **CST / pyramidal tract:** recently evolved, **contralateral**, **distal muscles**, **fine skilled voluntary movements (writing)**; **origin Betz cells — 30 % primary motor, 30 % premotor + supplementary, 40 % sensory parietal + association (sensory-motor integration)**.
> 5. Course: **internal capsule (posterior limb) → midbrain → pons → pyramid → decussation → lateral CST 80–90 % (opposite) / anterior CST 10 % (same)** → LMN.
> 6. **Medial pathways: 1 vestibulospinal, 2 reticulospinal, 3 tectospinal; lateral: 4 rubrospinal, 5 corticospinal; 1–4 extrapyramidal, 5 pyramidal.**
> 7. **Vestibulospinal:** medial → neck; lateral + reticulospinal → **posture (paravertebral, proximal extensors = antigravity)**. **Reticulospinal: pontine excitatory, medullary inhibitory.**
> 8. **Tectospinal:** origin **superior colliculus**, head & eye movements. **Rubrospinal: only lateral extrapyramidal tract, red nucleus, flexors of upper limb.**
> 9. **Lateral = new, fine skilled, flexors; medial = old, posture, extensors (antigravity).**
> 10. **Spinal cord = final common pathway (LMN):** **α → contracts muscle (overactivity → spasticity)**; **γ → regulates tone via intrafusal fibres (overactivity → ↑ tone)**.
> 11. **Decerebrate (extensor, γ) rigidity:** transection **between superior and inferior colliculi** → ++ reticulospinal → ++ γ → ↑ extensor tone; posture all-limb extension, forearms pronated, toes in; cause **uncal herniation**.
> 12. **Decorticate (flexor) rigidity:** cortex removed, **rubrospinal intact** → upper limbs flexed against chest; cause **internal capsule thrombosis/haemorrhage**.
> 13. **Decerebellate (α) rigidity:** **anterior cerebellum removed** → exaggerated extensor activity.
> 14. **UMN vs LMN:** atrophy –/+++, fasciculations –/+++, tone ↑↑/↓, weakness regional/segmental, reflexes ↑↑/↓↓, **Babinski +/–**.

[^1]: Upper motor neurons (cortex, brainstem nuclei) never leave the CNS; lower motor neurons (anterior horn cells and cranial motor nuclei) send their axons to the muscles. Lesion signs depend on which of the two is damaged (table at the end).
[^2]: Mirror neurons fire both when an action is performed and when the same action is watched; they were found in the premotor cortex (area F5) of monkeys by Giacomo Rizzolatti's group (1990s) and are thought to underlie imitation and understanding of others' actions.
[^3]: Betz cells are the giant pyramidal neurons of layer V of area 4, but they supply only about 3–5 % of the ~1 million corticospinal fibres; most CST axons come from smaller pyramidal cells. The page's "Origin: Betz cells" is kept as the exam answer. The 30/30/40 % split is Guyton's.
[^4]: Most anterior CST fibres cross at the segment where they end (anterior white commissure), supplying axial and proximal muscles of both sides; a few stay ipsilateral. In standard texts the lateral CST is 75–90 % and the anterior CST 10–25 % of fibres.
[^5]: The rubrospinal tract starts in the magnocellular red nucleus of the midbrain and crosses at once (ventral tegmental decussation); it facilitates flexors of the upper limb. In humans it is small, and much of its role is taken over by the CST.
[^6]: Muscle spindles contain intrafusal fibres (nuclear bag and nuclear chain) innervated by γ motor neurons; γ activity keeps the spindle taut and sensitive, so it sets the gain of the stretch reflex and therefore tone. α motor neurons supply the extrafusal (working) fibres. Spasticity in standard texts is a velocity-dependent increase in tone from an over-active stretch reflex after a UMN lesion.
[^7]: Sherrington produced decerebrate rigidity in cats (1898) by an intercollicular transection; it is abolished by cutting the dorsal roots (it needs the spindle → γ loop). In humans decerebrate posturing usually means midbrain/upper pontine damage and carries a worse prognosis than decorticate posturing (lesion above the midbrain).
[^8]: Removal of the anterior lobe of the cerebellum removes Purkinje-cell inhibition of the lateral vestibular (Deiters') nucleus, which then drives α motor neurons of extensors directly. Because the γ loop is not needed, dorsal root section does not abolish this "α rigidity".
[^9]: Babinski's sign is normal in infants under about 1–2 years, until the corticospinal tract is fully myelinated. Fanning of the other toes may accompany the extensor plantar response. A UMN lesion may show mild disuse atrophy late, but never the marked wasting and fasciculations of an LMN lesion.

[^ety-reticulospinal]: **reticulospinal** — Latin *reticulum*, "little net" (*rete*, net) + *spina* + *-al* → from the net-like reticular formation of the brainstem to the cord; pontine part facilitates, medullary part inhibits extensors.
[^ety-medulla]: **medulla** — Latin, "marrow, pith" → the medulla oblongata (*oblongata*, elongated), lowest part of the brainstem; home of the medullary reticulospinal tract and the pyramids.

[^suf-ity]: **-ity** — a quality or state: spasticity, rigidity, activity. Don't confuse with **-itis** inflammation · **-ation** a process.
[^suf-ate]: **-ate** — having, made into (adjective/participle): decerebrate, decorticate, decerebellate. Don't confuse with **-ase** an enzyme · **-ate** as a salt (glutamate).
[^pre-trans]: **trans-** — across, through: transection, transmission, transtentorial. Don't confuse with **inter-** between · **dia-** through (Greek) · **hemi-** half (hemisection).
[^suf-osis]: **-osis** — a condition or process (often abnormal): thrombosis, sclerosis, necrosis. Don't confuse with **-asis / -iasis** a diseased state, often parasitic (ascariasis) · **-itis** inflammation.
[^suf-rrhage]: **-rrhage / -rrhagia** — bursting forth, heavy bleeding: haemorrhage, menorrhagia. Don't confuse with **-rrhoea** flow (diarrhoea) · **-rrhaphy** suturing · **-rrhexis** rupture.
[^pre-a]: **a- / an-** — without, not: atrophy, ataxia, apraxia, areflexia. Don't confuse with **ab-** away from (abduction) · **ad-** towards (afferent) · **ana-** up, again.
[^suf-trophy]: **-trophy / -trophic** — nourishment, growth: atrophy, hypertrophy, dystrophy, neurotrophic. Don't confuse with **-tropy / -tropic** turning, acting on (inotropic, gonadotropic) · **-plasia** formation of cells (hyperplasia).
[^pre-re]: **re-** — back, again: reflex, receptor, release. Don't confuse with **retro-** backwards · **pre-** before.
[^pre-hyper]: **hyper-** — over, excessive: hyperreflexia, hypertonia. Don't confuse with **hypo-** under (hypotonia, hyporeflexia) · **hypno-** sleep.
